### 2.3

### Environment Control

The need for a clean and dry isolated operating field provides assurances when carrying out adhesive bonding procedures to prevent contamination of the substrate and adhesive materials being utilised.

Isolation with a rubber dam provides a means to ensure consistent moisture and contamination control throughout most restorative operative procedures. Time invested appropriately isolating the oral environment restricted to the area of clinical interest enhances soft tissue retraction and improved access. This pays dividends through improved visibility and allows the dental team to focus efforts on the bonding protocol rather than the potential distraction that constant moisture control can bring (Fig. 2.4). Further to this, patient airway and soft tissue protection is vital, especially when using caustic materials required for adhesive procedures (Fig. 2.5).

### 2.4

### Substrate Management

The key to optimising adhesion is managing and conditioning the substrate in conjunction with the paired crucial steps in applying the adhesive bonding agent and composite resin material.

### 2.4.1

### Barrier Removal

Any intervening layer (e.g. oral fluids, pellicle, plaque, calculus, restorative material) that compromises the contact between the resin and tooth structure will reduce adhesive bonding efficacy. Poor surface adhesive bonding could manifest as marginal discolouration, gingival inflammation, caries development and restoration loss/failure.

Contamination of the pre-etched proposed bonding surface will prevent appropriate acid etch conditioning and infiltration with the resin bonding agent. Hence, it is good practice to first clean the tooth surface with an ultrasonic scaler and oil-free prophy paste (using a prophy brush or cup) or pumice powder to reduce any barrier remnants that could compromise the adhesive bond. Alumina blasting tooth surfaces in addition will aid the further removal of stubborn surface stains and any remnant restorative material (Fig. 2.13).

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) produced by odontoblasts are inherently present in dentine and can be reactivated in acidic environments (during the caries process or acid etching). Their presence can cause degradation of the hybrid layer within the adhesive–dentine bond interface. MMP inhibitors, e.g. chlorhexidine, may be scrubbed onto the dentine surfaces to negate this prior to acid etching.

Ideally, a patient’s self-maintenance of plaque control provides the best outcome as prophylaxis just before bonding procedures can result in unwanted haemorrhage and contamination. If plaque is present on smooth surfaces with associated gingival inflammation, it may be prudent to reconsider the provision of the restorations until such time that the patient’s self-maintenance improves. The cervical and palatal/ lingual surfaces of teeth may be more commonly implicated as areas with biofilm.
This represents a key issue for the clinician. If areas of plaque are identified (via disclosing solution or test etching), the clinician must decide whether to provide oral hygiene instruction and abort bonding provision or provide tooth surface cleaning and prophylaxis and continue treatment.

The intaglio surfaces of indirect restorations may be contaminated before and during try-in procedures by inappropriate handling, oral fluids, try-in oil-based paste mediums and oil/water droplets from the 3:1 syringe (Fig. 2.14). ### 2.4.2

### Finish Lines

Teeth to be restored may require minor preparation to augment adhesive bonding procedures. The shape of the tooth may be modified at the proposed restoration junction by the placement of a finish line.

Bevel finish lines prepared on the facial surface of a tooth allow an increased surface area of the composite resin restoration to be bonded to. The oblique bevel opens the enamel prisms in cross-section to allow for improved resin infiltration and micromechanical adhesion. The bevel will also aid blending the transition of the composite resin restoration into the tooth seamlessly allowing optimal aesthetic integration. The bevel (short or long) may be continuous or interrupted (feathered) in its shape and along its length to allow scattering of light to help permit restoration aesthetic transition to tooth (Figs. 2.15 and 2.16).

Chamfer finish lines prepared on the palatal/lingual surface of a tooth allow for a thicker placement of restorative material, improving restoration durability and functional performance over the long term. In these non-visible areas, the demarcation line that may show at the restoration margin is of no aesthetic concern. The finish line must be placed away from areas of occlusal static and dynamic contact such that the restoration margin is not placed in high mechanical stress that would allow premature breakdown and failure of the adhesive bond (Fig. 2.16).

### 2.4.3

### Sand (Alumina) Blasting

A further method to prepare the bonding surface of teeth is the utilisation of sandblasting (alumina blasting) with alumina particles (Al2O3). Sandblasting tooth surfaces will aid the removal of surface stains, remnant restorative materials (temporary or definitive) and imparts a rough surface topography to create a reactive surface for
‘wetting’ to aid micromechanical retention (Figs. 2.13c, 2.17 and 2.18). In a similar fashion, restoration surfaces (metal alloys and resin-based materials) should also be prepared for adhesive bonding by sandblasting treatment, which cleans and abrades the restoration surface prior to adhesive bonding treatment. This produces a rough surface topography to aid micromechanical retention of the resin (Figs.  2.19 and 2.20).

The blasting process occurs ahead of the handpiece, involving the propulsion of alumina particles (20–50 microns diameter size) under high pressure (30–45 PSI) that collide with the surface of the tooth or restoration, resulting in abrasion cleaning and roughening. This can be carried out in wet or dry conditions in conjunction with high volume aspiration (Fig. 2.21). The further the handpiece sits from the instrumented site, the wider the area of abrasion treatment. The converse is true if the handpiece is closer to the site, resulting in a more focused area of action. The efficiency of sandblasting abrasion is inherently multifactorial. Particle size plays a role, where larger particles result in greater aggressiveness of the blasting. The greater the pressure applied, the increased efficacy of abrasion, which also concurrently raises the risk of patient discomfort during the procedure.

### 2.5

### Adhesion

Dental adhesion typically involves joining a dental restorative material (direct or indirect restoration) to tooth structure resulting in two adhesive interfaces; one between the tooth structure and the adhesive resin, and the other between the adhesive resin and the restorative material. It is a complex physico-chemical process that occurs at the interface of the two intimately contacting materials, which results in the formation of an attractive force. The strength of the adhesive bond is chemo-­ mechanical, and failure can occur either adhesively (failure at the adhesive interface, i.e. substrate separation) or cohesively (failure within the adhesive layer).

### 2.5.1

### Tooth Substrate

Adhesion to enamel tooth structure is the key to success for any adhesive-based procedure. In 1955, Michael Buonocore, at the Eastman Dental Centre in Rochester,
United States, discovered enamel bonding through a process of enamel etching and resin infiltration. Enamel adhesion is more ideal than that to dentine due to the consistency of the bond, its strength and durability. This marries with the minimally invasive approach as conservation of as much of the tooth as possible and results in the more ideal bond interface. As such, bonding procedures that are more invasive and purposefully remove the full enamel surface are inherently less efficient.

Dentine, due to its significantly different structural characteristics, organic composition and moisture content, has a reduced bonding potential. The organic component of dentine, which is approximately 30%, and the presence of odontoblasts means there is fluid present in its surface further complicating adhesive bonding potential when compared to enamel. Further to this, adherent debris produced when instrumenting the tooth surface known as the smear layer also compromises the adhesive bond. The smear layer is a 1–2μm thick layer of with plugs that block the dentinal tubules and so reduce their porosity.

Adhesive bonding to enamel and dentine structures produces bond strengths of between 10 and 30  MPa, which may be carried out by etch and rinse methods (Fig.  2.23), or self-etch methods utilising either fourth-, fifth-, sixth-, seventh- or eighth-generation bonding agents with the option of both light-cure and dual-cure capabilities (Fig. 2.24). Both methods will aim to fully or partially remove the smear layer that is left on the tooth tissue after instrumentation. The smear layer is an aggregate of mainly fragmented and compacted tooth material and debris (Figs.  2.25
& 2.26).

### 2.5.2

### Replacing, Refurbishing or Repairing Composite

### Resin Restorations

The task of optimising the adhesive bond becomes more crucial where restorations require replacement and the enamel substrate has already been bonded to once before. The clinician is faced with the challenge of removing the residual restoration in its entirety whilst also avoiding damage to the precious enamel substrate, which is key for the achievement of the remedial treatment. Utilisation of magnification in combination with LED lighting can aid the process and remove the guesswork in whether the defective restoration has been removed and if the enamel is clean and intact, ready for the new restoration (Fig. 2.36).

When refurbishing or repairing composite resin restorations where the defect lies within the restoration and not on tooth structure, the surface to be repaired should be roughened with a diamond bur or sandblasting; cleaned with acid etch and water, primed with an unfilled resin primer and then repaired with a similar composite resin material. Where defects lie on both restoration and tooth substrate, a traditional approach to the adhesive strategy should be utilised.

Composite resin undergoes a process of volumetric shrinkage when polymerising. This is caused by largely dispersed monomer components linking tightly together, resulting in a net reduction in volume proportional to the degree of monomer to polymer conversion. The amount of polymerisation shrinkage (1–4+ vol%) varies upon the classification of the composing resin material used and light polymerisation conditions (Table  2.3). Resin-based cements show similar or higher shrinkage values. Some unwanted effects can result from this process; namely, debonding between the restoration/tooth interface or at restoration margins, leading to internal or marginal spaces. This may be a prelude to post-operative sensitivity, marginal staining or caries. Where tooth tissue is undermined, or craze lines exist, cracks or cuspal fractures can also develop. There are some recognised methods for minimising polymerisation shrinkage. Shrinkage occurs towards the light source, with most stress occurring at the bonding interface. Bearing this in mind is crucial during composite resin restoration placement as it can reduce the amount of unfavourable shrinkage. Incremental composite resin material placement and slowing the rate of polymerisation also reduce the amount of shrinkage stresses developing (Table 2.4).

The light polymerisation activation of composite resin materials is a key stage in the provision of adhesive restorations. Light polymerisation activation methods are listed in (Table 2.5).

Reducing the rate (kinetics/speed) of the initial polymerisation shrinkage will benefit the restoration bond integrity. This may be achieved by step, ramp or pulse light polymerisation activations. Without adequate polymerisation, the restoration provision is compromised as the optimum physical characteristics of the material are not achieved. This is not only important for the main body of the restoration but essential for the bonding interface junction between the resin material and the tooth structure.

Final light polymerisation of any composite resin material may be carried out through a water-based oxygen-blocking translucent barrier material, e.g. glycerine (Fig. 2.40) to order to eliminate the oxygen inhibition unreacted resin layer (approximately 10 microns thick). Any factor that reduces the intensity of light transmission compromises the proficiency and completeness of resin material polymerisation and, therefore, adhesive restoration success. Resin materials can be light polymerised through tooth structure, translucent restorations or oxygen-blocking barrier mediums, but this reduces the intensity of the light (Figs. 2.40, 2.41 & 2.42). One crucial factor for adequate material polymerisation is the position and distance of the light source from the restoration surface. Placing the curing light tip at multiple positions around the restoration can aide the totality of cure. This is especially relevant where there are areas blocked to light penetration as a result of access issues or adjacent teeth or restorations. The position of the light cure tip should be approximated close to the surface of the restoration at a 90° angulation (Figs. 2.40 & 2.41).
The further the distance of the light cure tip, the light intensity diminishes (Fig. 2.42).
The depth and thickness of the restoration also require consideration, perhaps the need for incremental light polymerisation where possible. The darker the shade is of the composite resin material, the more light absorbent the material is and less likely the efficiency of light polymerisation, and so the more likely the need to cure from multiple angles. Where filler content results in a more opaque mass, the ability to cure diminishes much in the same way as the darkness of the material. For all factors, light polymerisation activation durations extended over the recommended timings suggested by the manufacturers will not improve upon polymer conversion.
The light output intensity should be optimised, and the light-cure unit meter tested on a regular basis. The output of the light cure unit is specified in the manufacturer’s guide, and meter testing methods for light output efficiency should be adhered to.
