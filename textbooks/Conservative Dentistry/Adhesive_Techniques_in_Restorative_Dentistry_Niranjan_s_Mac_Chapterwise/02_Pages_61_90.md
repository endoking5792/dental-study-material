### 3.1

### Introduction

Teeth have been shown to be important for self-esteem and confidence. This is largely associated with tooth shape, form, colour and their relative positioning. A common aesthetic concern of patients is tooth discolouration. This can present generally or can be isolated to one unit. Patients with discoloured teeth are more likely to face social challenges, have lower dental self-esteem and may also associate the discoloured tooth with a disturbing time in their life, such as non-accidental injury or a traumatic incident. As such, the management of discoloured teeth may be a priority for the patient, especially when considering their social well-being.

The size, shape and colour of teeth are all critical in the perception of aesthetics.
The colour or shade of teeth, which is uniform and ‘whiter’, provides measurable benefit to the patient regardless of the mode or method of the improvement. In this respect, the utilisation of the most conservative method of colour improvement should take precedence over other more aggressive forms of treatment, which result in the removal of sound tooth tissue or otherwise intact teeth with a minimal amount of restorations.

### 3.2

### Aetiology

There are various causes of tooth discolouration, which can either be internal or external in origin. Internal discolouration arises where there are developmental or congenital defects of the enamel and dentine structure, or physiological and pathological processes that have discoloured the pulpal surface of the tooth, manifesting as discolouration. External discolourations arise primarily from diet and social habits.

### 3.2.1

### Internal Discolouration

The process of enamel and dentine formation provides the basis for the colour of teeth. This process may be entirely without defects, but where dentine deposition is great and enamel is thin, teeth may seem more yellow despite the absence of any developmental abnormality. This can be illustrated by the maxillary canine tooth.
The canine tooth has a comparatively thick dentine layer when compared to the adjacent enamel layer, resulting in a significant difference in colour between the canine and adjacent teeth units. The formation of tooth tissue in the unborn, neonate, child and adult can be affected by a number of metabolic, genetic, iatrogenic, traumatic, idiopathic, as well as natural ageing processes which can modify the perceived colour of teeth.

Metabolic disorders resulting in physiological changes can manifest in teeth depending on the stage of development and the severity of the disturbance. Amelogenesis imperfecta occurs where enamel is either poorly mineralised or organised. As a result of the differences in aetiology, problems in colour are likely to manifest in different ways. Where enamel is thin, such as in hypoplastic amelogenesis imperfecta, the underlying yellow/brown tinge of the unaffected dentine is likely to be a source of apparent discolouration (Fig. 3.2). Hypoplastic areas can also manifest as pitting or striations in the enamel surface (Fig. 3.3). This in itself is unlikely to be a source of discolouration, but as these areas can harbour bacteria and food products, resulting in external staining, patients report concerns relating to colour. Where mild hypomineralised enamel presents with intense white areas coronally, it is likely to be the significant contrast with the remaining body of the crown that patients are more concerned about than the area of whiteness itself. If hypomineralisation is severe, the discolouration is likely to be significantly darker and more uniform as a result compared to milder forms.

Dentine-related malformations such as dentinogenesis imperfecta and dentine dysplasia may present with the obliteration of the pulp chambers, resulting in a greater intensity of the underlying dentine colour through the enamel. The enamel itself commonly fractures away from the dentine, which can result in expediting the need for treatment. Clinically, the appearance is amber, grey, to purple blue (Fig. 3.4). If the enamel fractures away from the dentine and is exposed to the oral environment, the intensity of discolouration may increase as chromogens are absorbed into the dentine surface more readily.

Dental fluorosis occurs when excessive amounts of fluoride are ingested during the formative stages of tooth development. The ideal times to avoid high fluoride exposure are between 1 and 4 years of age, with the critical time between 20 and

During early carious lesion development, there will be a balance of mineral loss and gain within the enamel tissue lesion, the balance of which determines disease activity. Intracanal medicaments such as those containing tetracycline constituents may also discolour tooth structure. Root canal sealers can cause gradual discolouration of teeth through penetration of the dentinal tubules, resulting in a yellow hue. In contrast to sealants, a starker change in colour is caused by mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA). MTA apexification for non-vital immature incisors is an established method for the management of wide-open apices. As such, the use of MTA should be confined to the apical 3 mm without filling the whole canal length. In such situations, the clinician may consider utilising thermoplastic GP as the more aesthetic alternative despite the potential lack of seal and bioactivity that MTA provides.

Other more natural modes of discolouration include natural ageing of teeth, where secondary and tertiary dentine deposition results in darkening of the tooth, which may be more apparent as the enamel concurrently thins due to functional wear. Reactionary dentine is less porous and more mineralised than naturally deposited dentine. The laying down of additional dentine layers increases the contribution of the naturally more yellow-brown dentine (due to an increased percentage of yellow phosphoprotein) when compared to the static or diminishing thickness of the enamel. This intensifies the chroma of the tooth and reduces translucency (Fig. 3.17).

### 3.2.2

### External Discolouration

Teeth naturally discolour over time as they are exposed to the oral environment. In function, the changes in colour occur on exposure to chromophores from food, liquids and social habits such as cigarette smoking. The prolonged use of chlorhexidine-­ containing mouth rinses may also be an additional cause.

The process is yet to be fully understood, but it seems as though the constituents of food and drink, such as polyphenols, which provide the colouring to foods or tannins present in tea, coffee and red wine, contribute to the process. Enamel is not impervious, and its porosities can absorb these molecules present within food and drink beverages. There are broadly two sources of discolouration. Those molecules that have coloured constituents which may not attach to or be able to reside within the porosities of enamel. These are known as pigments. In contrast, and of greater significance, ‘dyes’ have reactive components that allow them to connect to organic material. Common foods and drinks that contain dyes include tomato and cocoa-­ based products, tea, coffee and red wine.

The presence of a biofilm and the acquired pellicle seems to contribute as bacteria react to the presence of the chromogens, whilst also potentially causing dissolution of enamel, resulting in a greater propensity for deep discolouration. Other less common sources of discolouration include the presence of chromogenic bacteria with green and orange staining in patients with poor oral hygiene and black/brown staining in those with good oral hygiene.

### 3.3

### Tooth Bleaching

The technique of bleaching teeth has long been described as a method of improving anterior aesthetics. Since this time, the delivery of tooth bleaching has undergone some modifications which have been driven by changes in legislation, increased experience, greater scientific knowledge of the process, demand for aesthetic treatments and changes in social attitudes toward dental treatment.

The alternatives to tooth bleaching treatment involve the removal of sound but discoloured tooth tissue to provide either direct or indirect veneer or crown restorations to disguise the appearance of the colour of the tooth (Fig. 3.18). This approach will inevitably weaken the remaining tooth tissue, providing less tooth tissue for future replacement or refurbishment of the restoration as and when this is needed.
However, a combination of both bleaching and restoration treatment modalities may be used in certain clinical cases where the colour change treatment may be challenging with bleaching techniques alone.

### 3.3.1

### Colour Mechanisms

A number of different compounds have been utilised to bleach and lighten discoloured teeth. The most commonly utilised bleaching agent gel substances are 10% carbamide peroxide and 6% hydrogen peroxide. The former breaks down into the latter during a process of degradation. A solution of 10% carbamide peroxide results in approximately 3% hydrogen peroxide over a period of 4 h.

Hydrogen peroxide is a colourless liquid that is soluble in water to produce an acidic solution. This solution contains reactive oxygen species that act to oxidise the large complex molecules responsible for discolouration through the formation of free radicals. The net effect of this reaction is the production of smaller molecules.

Indicators of bleaching success also include a consideration for the internal structure of the teeth, and more specifically related to age where physiological and pathological processes play a role. Older teeth, with a greater likelihood of secondary and tertiary dentine deposition, will be inherently more challenging to lighten as penetration of the bleach into the more mineralised tissues is challenging. Older teeth are also likely to have stains and degradation products for longer periods, which may also equate to the severity and intensity of the unsightly colour. Teeth that are pathologically aged, such as those with attritive or erosive toothwear, may also present with similar issues as dentine is deposited in a similar process.

### 3.3.2

### Patient Assessment

A detailed history should be taken from the patient, and an appreciation of the patient’s expectation of treatment needs to be balanced against the current shade of teeth and the desired improvement. Patients who consume strongly coloured foods and drinks should be made aware that the outcome may be compromised as a result, as well as the need for treatment due to a relapse of colour. Discussions on previous treatments may give some indication as to the patient’s motivations and expectations.

The clinical examination may reveal marked differences in the shades of each individual tooth, resulting in the need to carefully plot the scheme of bleaching whereby certain teeth would undergo a greater bleaching period than others for purposes of achieving an equal result by the end of treatment (Fig. 3.19). Due to their inherent thickness, canine teeth are likely to require more bleaching than their neighbouring lateral incisor teeth.
