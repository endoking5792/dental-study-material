Where anterior teeth present with a significant Class IV pattern of tooth loss, the clinician may consider various options for management. By virtue of the traumatic injury suffered in the unit, the pulp may have been exposed and severely compromised, hence the need for root canal treatment. It may be intuitive to consider such extensive Class IV defects as requiring a post-retained direct or indirect restoration or at the more severe end of the spectrum needing extraction. These approaches are likely to be fuelled by concerns at both the functional and aesthetic qualities of direct composite resin restorations. If a direct composite resin restoration is provided, anterior guidance on protrusion is likely to migrate from the tooth surface onto the restoration. This transition can put the interface between tooth and restoration under strain and tension, making failure more likely. From an aesthetic point of view, due to the differing optical properties between the enamel and composite resin, the mismatch in light absorbance and reflection may be apparent (Fig. 6.4).
These are realistic considerations for clinicians and patients. These aspects of direct restoration provision can be tempered by the manner in which the restoration is provided. Bleaching the residual tooth tissue can lighten and reduce variation in colour to aid shade match with the subsequent restoration. To maximise adhesive bonding capacity, the clinician can consider encasing the tooth within a composite resin restoration in its entirety on buccal and palatal surfaces to the cervical margin.

### 6.5

### Fibre Posts

Fibre post systems are indicated for the direct adhesive core build-up of anterior and posterior teeth that have lacking coronal supragingival tooth structure. They may be used to support and retain the coronal restoration. They have some notable advantages over traditional metal alloy post systems. The quartz fibres (approximately
60% volume) and epoxy resin (approximately 40% volume) composition of the post confers some attractive physical characteristics. Many manufacturers claim that the modulus of elasticity of the post is similar to that of human dentine (15 GPa), therefore, this theoretically confers a lower risk of fracture. The dental literature on this attractive quality when compared to metal alloy posts is evident.

The fibre posts are coloured white/opaque or are translucent in presentation, which would be aesthetically more favourable than a metal alloy post if used in conjunction with a coronal translucent restorative material. Translucency allows curing light to be transmitted throughout the length of the post surface, thus optimising photo polymerisation of the paired matched dual-cure adhesive bonding agent and resin cement in areas not readily accessible otherwise, especially in an apical direction (Fig. 6.6). Despite being translucent, the posts are identifiable radiographically as they demonstrate a degree of radio-opacity (Fig. 6.7).

Adhesive qualities of fibre posts are largely based on the surface characteristics and topography in conjunction with chemical bonding. Microporous surfaces allow for the ingress of resin to facilitate micromechanical retention (Fig. 6.6). A silanating agent is also required for the adhesion of the resin cement to the fibre

Prior to deciding to provide a post, a comprehensive assessment needs to take place in addition to the relative design of the post and how the latter can optimise restoration of the former. Posts that are parallel-sided generate more stress in the residual root on cementation but provide less stress during function. The converse is true for tapered posts, where in function the wedging effect concentrated towards the apex of the tooth during function increases stress but during cementation the stresses are much less in comparison to parallel-sided. The majority of fibre post systems are tapered and hence require less preparation of the residual root due to their inherent similarity with anatomical canals, which naturally taper also (Figs. 6.6 and 6.7). A sensible post design incorporates a hybrid of both parallel and tapered features to benefit the said advantages of both shape designs (Fig. 6.8).

The length of the post within the canal also needs to be considered. A longer post results in greater surface contact and hence improved retention. This needs to be balanced against the need to maintain the apical biological seal and the amount of gutta-percha remaining in the apical portion. The greater the amount of gutta-percha remaining at the apex, the less the likelihood that the seal would have been disturbed. A widely accepted minimum amount of gutta-percha remaining is 3–4 mm (Fig. 6.7).

The root morphology and the root diameter also need consideration. A severely curved root in either the middle or apical root third is likely to compromise the length of the post. Further, if the canal is wide, the ratio between the diameter of the post and the root and the associated resin cement becomes more unfavourable due to the greater thickness of the resin cement. The largest post diameter that remains passive within the canal is an arbitrary measure for post placement. Other arbitrary measures of post dimensions such as the post length being of a minimum length to the coronal crown are not heavily supported by evidence but are considered sensible biomechanical rules of thumb.

Once the provision of the post has been decided upon removal of the gutta-­ percha to accommodate the post space preparation can take place. The length of the post above the residual root should be decided upon with the final coronal restoration in mind. This may be determined by a diagnostic wax-up of the proposed external contour of the final coronal restoration. This can then be used to make a variety of silicone matrices to aid the provision of either direct composite resin or indirect ceramic restorations (Fig. 6.5).

More recently, the utilisation of indirect restorations that fully engage the pulp chamber has gained popularity, otherwise known as the ‘Endocrown’ (Fig. 6.13).
Although any material can be utilised for this technique, the overriding popular choice is the use of high-strength all-ceramic materials. As a concept, the
Endocrown aims to engage the pulp chamber as a single-piece restoration without the need for a post retained core and a traditional full coverage crown restoration. This conserves tooth tissue within the root system, maximises the adhesive surface area engagement whilst providing cuspal coverage and axial loading of the restoration and tooth. It is characterised by a supra-gingival margin and is cemented with an adhesive approach. Preparation features need to incorporate occlusal reduction of at least 2 mm. Unlike more traditional preparation techniques, the internal walls of the pulp chamber require preparation to ensure no undercuts to accommodate the restoration’s extension into this area. Once these stages are completed, attention can be directed to the floor of the pulp chamber.
The canal orifices should have a base subseal material placed to optimise biological seal, any sharp or protruding areas can be smoothed or rounded off to reduce risk of stress concentration. Bonding under rubber dam isolation should be provided and resin cements including self-­etching adhesives can be utilised (Figs. 6.13 and 6.14).

### 6.7.1

### Immediate Dentine Sealing

Immediate dentine sealing (IDS) is a technique to treat the tooth that may be utilised in conjunction with the provision of indirect adhesive onlay restorations. It involves adhesively sealing the freshly exposed dentine surfaces immediately after they are cut during tooth preparation prior to impression taking/digital scanning and placing the definitive restoration. The etching and bonding procedures are carried out in the normal preferred manner (as described in Chap. 2), aiding in sealing the dentine substructure from dehydration, moisture and bacterial contamination; decreasing post-operative sensitivity (in the case of vital teeth) and ensuring an extended time for the dentine bond to mature for improved efficacy.

### 6.8

### Adhesive Technique

### 6.8.1

### Fibre Post Cementation

Once the post diameter size, length and fit has been established and diagnostically confirmed (Fig. 6.7), the post should be disinfected and then cleaned with alcohol and air-dried. A silane primer is then applied. The root canal should be sandblasted and acid-etched. It then should be disinfected with hypochlorite and air-dried. The root canal should be coated with a dual-cure adhesive agent and air dried to allow evaporation of the solvent and prevent pooling. Application of the resin cement to the root canal requires an elongated delivery tip that will fill the apical portion of the prepared post space first. To prevent air entrapment, the tip of the applicator should remain within the cement as it is dispensed. Once filled, the post is seated with firm apical pressure within the canal. This is followed by light curing through the vertical length of the post to initiate the polymerisation of the adhesive agent and resin cement. The same cement material is then further dispensed around the post in a coronal direction to build up the core. This then is light-cured again to initiate the polymerisation of the resin cement. A time period allowing the self-cure to complete over a course of 5 min is elapsed before final shaping of the composite resin core and abutment tooth.

### 7.1

### Introduction

Tooth surface loss (TSL) is a multifactorial process resulting in the loss of enamel and dentine through a number of different modes that do not involve a bacterial component. This process, much in the same as carious tissue loss, threatens the survival of the tooth and results in a number of identifiable symptoms that patients report and signs that are readily clinically identifiable. For example, patients may readily complain about the increasing sensitivity of their teeth due to the progressive exposure of underlying dentinal tubules or the continual diminishing coronal height of their teeth as an aesthetic concern. Younger patients are presenting with more severe aspects of the disease and, as such, threaten the longevity of tooth retention during their lifetime. This needs to be balanced against the awareness that our patients are living for longer, and for such a process to be severe so early will undeniably result in a greater chance of tooth loss even in the short term.

TSL differs in its pattern of enamel and dentine destruction and as such the approach in its treatment will also differ from that for teeth requiring regular restoration or those needing crowns subsequent to plaque-associated disease. One overriding principle in managing TSL is the focus on maintenance of tooth tissue to retain pulp vitality and make the ability to replace restorations in the future a lot more achievable. In essence, as has been previously described, the enamel and dentine should not be considered perishable in the process of TSL management. On the contrary, maintenance of tooth tissue is the main focus.

### 7.2

### Aetiology and Pattern of TSL

### 7.2.1

### Erosion

Erosive tooth surface loss is the chemical dissolution of tooth tissue by way of non-­ bacterial related exposure to acid from extrinsic or intrinsic sources. Extrinsic sources include the consumption of acidic fizzy drinks as well as alcoholic beverages to levels that create erosive lesions. Intrinsic sources can be potentially more challenging to identify and manage. Patients can present with emotive issues such as alcohol dependence or bulimia, resulting in acid insult due to the continual contact between regurgitated acidic contents of the stomach. The amount, duration and episodes of contact the teeth are exposed to the acid directly correlate, up to a degree, with the amount of tooth tissue that is lost. Erosive features of enamel include the presence of a smooth, shiny and reflective tooth surface with the loss of tertiary anatomical features such as perikymata, mamelons and the shape of the cingulum. As the erosion progresses, the tooth surface becomes progressively flatter and darker as the darker subsurface dentine shines through the now thinner enamel.
One milestone in this process is the change in pattern and urgency for the need for treatment once the dentine has been breached. Once the harder enamel is removed, the softer dentine will dissolve at a greater rate, resulting in the characteristic ‘cupping’ defect. ### 7.2.2

### Attrition

Attrition is tooth surface loss caused by frictional tooth-to-tooth contact and is largely localised to the incisal and occlusal surfaces of teeth and is common amongst patients who present with parafunction or bruxism. The pattern of tooth surface loss contrasts with that of erosion, as the manner in which the teeth are obliterated differs. Unlike erosive lesions, there is a definite demarcation between the area that is being worn and those areas of the tooth that are being spared. For example, there is a greater tendency, but not exclusively, for the enamel and dentine to wear at the same rate. Such is the relative definition of the lesion that teeth can be sharp at the worn and unworn surfaces. The nature of the lesions is flat and rough, having been essentially worn by the opposing unit (Fig. 7.4).

When examining the dentition, the teeth that are being worn tend to lose their natural tooth contacts. In anterior segments, the natural anatomy of the maxillary cingulum and mandibular incisal edge is gradually diminished, resulting in both surfaces becoming flat and the contact becoming as wide and broad as the attritive lesion. This will change the pattern of anterior guidance, which may the over the longer period of time may culminate in its loss. The maxillary and mandibular teeth mirror each other in the shape of the occlusal plane. In a similar manner to erosive lesions, the pulp lays down tertiary dentine if provided the opportunity. In its most severe and progressed form, there is a complete loss of occlusal anatomy resulting in widespread flat surfaces both anterior and posterior segments (Fig. 7.5).

### 7.2.3

### Abrasion

This mechanism of tooth surface loss involves mechanical wearing of tooth tissue other than that caused by the opposing natural tooth tissue. This, in its most common form, may present as a cervical lesion due to abrasion from tooth brushing (Fig. 7.6). Other rarer manifestations include sand chewing habits and the presence of tongue piercings that may sit between anterior teeth. One emerging aetiological factor in abrasion cases is the iatrogenic development of tooth surface loss through previous treatment. Patients with historical treatment for localised tooth surface loss may have been provided with metal ceramic restorations that are inherently rougher than natural tooth tissue. This classically presents where the maxillary anterior teeth are restored with crowns and the mandibular anterior teeth are unrestored. Over time, the mandibular anterior teeth are chronically exposed to the rougher ceramic surfaces opposing them, resulting in abrasion.

### 7.2.4

### Abfraction

Abfraction is a form of tooth surface loss that occurs around the cervical portion of teeth on the buccal surface. Abfraction is thought to take place when excessive cyclic, non-axial tooth loading leads to cusp flexure and stress concentration in the vulnerable cervical region of teeth. Such stress is then believed to directly or indirectly contribute to the loss of cervical tooth substance. This, in its most common form, may present as a cervical lesion without the presence of associated gingival recession, as may be seen with abrasion lesions (Fig. 7.7).

### 7.3

### Management and Treatment Strategies

The most precious commodity in oral health is the presence of healthy, natural tooth tissue. The less tooth tissue is lost, the less extensive the restoration, and so the greater the longevity of the latter and the improved survival of the former. The second most precious commodity in oral health, especially when considering the need for tooth replacement or restoring individual teeth, is ‘restorative space’. This need can take the form of occlusal space or space between a restoration and the pulp canal system, in an effort to avoid the need for root canal treatment. The additive provision of adhesive restorations in supra-occlusion is a minimally invasive technique that protects and preserves underlying tooth tissue whilst simultaneously creating restorative space.

### 7.3.1

### Prevention

This aspect of management is of paramount importance in the management of tooth surface loss and is linked to a successful diagnostic process. The eradication of the tooth surface loss process is most ideal before the instigation of treatment.
It is common to require the input of medical colleagues in instigating a preventive regime.

A patient suffering from gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) may benefit from a consultation with their general medical practitioner for the prevention of further regurgitation. This may take the form of prescribed medicines such as proton pump inhibitors or further investigation by a gastroenterologist. Where clinicians suspect an eating disorder such as bulimia or anorexia nervosa, the situation can become more difficult to manage if the patient does not readily admit to their condition. Psychology and psychiatric management are invaluable in trying to achieve the optimal outcome for this cohort of patients. ### 7.3.2

### Restorative Challenges

The physiological process of dentoalveolar compensation brought about by pathologic tooth surface loss creates a dilemma for the clinician. One option may be to conform to the current occlusal scheme without changes in the vertical dimension.
This may be intuitive, but unfortunately, it may expose the patient and the dentist to risk. When lacking, conformative approaches require restorative space obtained by tooth tissue removal. This is more specifically problematic for the tooth surface loss patient because of the already reduced tooth volume and height. An option may be to consider surgical crown lengthening to increase the supra-gingival tissue volume.
This results in a reduction in periodontal support and a crown restoration shape that has root dentine as a significant portion. Due to the inherent difference between the root shape and circumference, and the need for a tapered full crown preparation, this is also likely to result in weakening of the tooth and further pulp insult. Such a tapering preparation may be unavoidably aggressive due to the existing tooth form shape and produce reduced proximity to the root canal system. Not only does this also result in reduced periodontal ligament support but it also increases the crown-to-­ root ratio.

An alternative treatment option to create restorative space is the use of orthodontic treatment to allow localised or generalised bodily tooth movements. This is likely to prolong treatment time and increase treatment financial expense. It may also be challenging achieve due to the limited coronal tooth tissue to bond orthodontic brackets to, and the high anchorage requirement needs. Communication between restorative and orthodontic colleagues and patients for this management strategy is key to its success.

Outside of conforming to the current occlusal scheme and orthodontic treatment to create restorative space is to increase the occlusal vertical dimension (OVD). Due to the challenges discussed, additive adhesive strategies could be considered more conservative. Depending on the manner in which this is delivered, this can navigate around these issues. ### 7.3.3

### Dahl Concept

What may be fairly daunting from the outset in the treatment of tooth surface loss is the multiple teeth it is affecting and the need to manage these units collectively. It is rare for a solitary tooth unit to be affected, and it is not possible to treat one tooth in isolation when other teeth have lesions. Further treatment challenges exist where the shape of the lesion itself inherently does not provide an avenue for retention and resistance form for the restoration of the tooth. The cupping and flat nature of erosive and attrition defects requires a wholly adhesive approach if the tooth is not to be prepared for a more destructive crown restoration. Reliance on the adhesion of resin cements to restorative materials requires an attention to detail of how best they are applied and an awareness of the advantages and limitations of such techniques.

One notable challenge in tooth surface loss treatment is the management of the occlusion and more crucially dentoalveolar compensation. This is a physiological compensatory process through which teeth maintain occlusal contact with the opposing teeth units. The OVD and the freeway space are unaltered as the occlusal contacts are maintained by alveolar bone remodelling, resulting in axial elongation of the alveolar process. As teeth gradually wear, a space is created inter-occlusally, which allows the tooth-periodontal ligament-alveolar process complex to erupt and maintain occlusal contact. The process of erosion, attrition or abrasion reduces the enamel and dentine thickness overlying the pulp, whilst axial tooth movement during the gradual process of dentoalveolar compensation diminishes the interocclusal space. The pulp recognises this process as a threat and lays down reactionary dentine depending on the severity. If the rate of tooth surface loss is greater than the eruptive potential of the teeth, an open bite will develop. As the tooth surface loss progresses, there is a net reduction in supragingival tooth tissue, a reduction in the interocclusal space due to the dentoalveolar compensation and disruption of the occlusal planes (Figs. 7.3 and 7.5). This lack of coronal tooth tissue and absence of any interocclusal space to tore to amongst a number of teeth units may appear daunting if more conventional approaches to treatment are considered. An approach that conforms to the current vertical dimension would involve a fairly invasive approach with significant tooth morbidity.
