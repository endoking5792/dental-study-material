### 5.8

### Maintenance

Refurbishment or restoration, replacement of direct resin restorations is an unfortunate inevitability. Due to the inherent nature of adhesive techniques being less invasive, their future management is also of minimal harm. One important aspect that the clinician needs to take special note of at the time of replacement is the aetiology of failure or the source of need for replacement. Where restorations are worn, marginally compromised or discoloured, this may be readily apparent. If the reason behind restoration failure is less apparent further investigation, such as occlusal analysis may reveal factors unapparent from the outset. Issues such as anterior or

Where restorations may present with partial fracture of the residual composite, the clinician will need to decide as to the benefit of replacing the entire restoration and utilising the superior enamel bond or bonding to the remaining composite resin.
The latter has an inferior bond and is likely to result in an obvious junction between the old and new resin in addition to a difference in shade. If the clinician has a choice, where resources are available, the replacement of the entire restoration would be most favourable.

Removal of the residual restoration can be challenging to achieve, especially where the previous restoration’s margin was seamless with the tooth surface or is similar in shade to the tooth itself. One beneficial resource to have at hand is any previous diagnostic wax-up or the accompanying PVS matrix. This can make restoration replacement much easier and quicker, as a new diagnostic wax-up will not be required, with replacement being provided in one clinical visit. Delineation between tooth and composite resin can be achieved through a number of techniques.
Composite removal burs or instruments, those commonly utilised for the removal of resin retaining orthodontic brackets, are also very useful in this respect. One further characteristic property is that composite will tend to mark if scraped with a probe which doesn’t not occur so readily with hard dental tissue. ‘Black light’ torches allow remnant composite resin material to be highlighted when exposed to specific light emitted from the torch (Fig. 5.46). Alternatively, etching enamel will give the tooth tissue a chalky appearance.

Once the clinician is confident that the majority of restoration has been removed, treating the surface of the dental tissue by alumina blasting further optimises the substrate for repeat adhesive bonding. Once fully cleaned, the tooth surface can be adhesively treated and the new restoration placed in the most appropriate manner.

If a repair is chosen over wholesale restoration replacement, the surface of the resin restoration should be roughened by way of diamond bur and alumina blasting.
The surface of the composite resin is reactivated by applying a thin coat of resin primer to allow adhesive bonding of the repair increment to the freshly activated surface (Fig. 5.47).

### 6.1

### Introduction

Root-canal-treated teeth represent a significant challenge to the clinician that differs from that of teeth otherwise vital with significant tooth tissue remaining. By virtue of their vitality loss and need for root canal treatment, the tooth is highly likely to have reduced volumetric mass or acquired structural defects. The tooth itself, having been accessed, will be more hollow and hence physically more vulnerable to mechanical functional forces imparted. The loss in mass needs to be considered not only through numerical amount but also strategic position. For example, a 30%

The concern for undermined adjacent cusps is compounded once the tooth has been accessed for root canal treatment. The removal of the pulp roof results in a loss of structural resilience due to the bracing role of the occluso-pulpal portion of the coronal dentine. This process of access actually makes already weakened cusp structures weaker and undermined as the cusp height increases once the pulp chamber is exposed.

Anterior teeth may be more likely to have suffered trauma resulting in tissue loss and loss of vitality. These units may be intact but harbouring weak points such as underlying fractures or cracks. In other cases, teeth may have suffered

At its most severe, an absence of any supragingival tooth tissue presents a challenge due to a lack of coronal tooth structure and absence of any substantial enamel bonding substrate (Fig. 6.1).

As a result of root canal treatment and tissue loss, the remaining tooth structure is weaker and less able to manage functional loading. Hence, restorations have a greater responsibility to redistribute load to the remaining tooth tissue and periodontal ligament evenly through the axial portion of the tooth. This may be even more fraught or challenging where patients have lost vitality as a result of non-axial forces due to habitual parafunction, a significant factor to consider which the clinician may find difficult to remove, mitigate against or even reduce when providing a future restoration. Cracked teeth that have lost vitality are also likely to have been weakened due to the presence of residual restorations, making cracks even more likely (Fig. 6.2).

### 6.2

### Tooth Restorability

Published methods have been proposed to quantify the restorability of a tooth unit and thereby suggest a restoration management strategy. However, the assessment of tooth restorability can be highly subjective. Any tooth that can be ‘restored’ is based upon operator skill level and their experience, but the essential component that makes any treatment option realistic is longevity. Survival of both tooth and restoration is symbiotic and is a significant factor for any patient embarking on treatment.
The question of ‘How long will this last ?’ can only be partially supported by quality evidence-based research that is applicable to the individual presenting clinical situation. The assessment of a tooth for restorability should not be conducted in isolation from the general clinical presentation. General plaque control and susceptibility to caries and periodontal disease may weigh against the provision of endodontics and restoration, especially if further teeth are likely to deteriorate in the meantime.
Patients with parafunction and bruxism place unfavourable occlusal functional loads on teeth, and as such, may be best treated with less aesthetic but more resilient restorations such as gold or non-precious metal alloys. This may not be aesthetically appealing to the patient, and a compromise may have to be accepted. The role the tooth will play in the future needs to be considered. The functional and aesthetic needs of a discoloured, optimally root-canal-treated and otherwise intact central incisor in a patient with a high smile line will differ significantly from a mandibular second molar with difficult root canal anatomy and a large MOD restoration.

The assessment of the remaining tooth begins with radiographic assessment of the root canal treatment and migrates to the coronal tooth tissue. Ideally, prior to the provision of root canal treatment, an assessment of restorability should be conducted but may not always have occurred. Removal of all existing restorative material and an appraisal of the remaining tooth tissue prior to deciding the definitive strategy aids the identification of opportunities, strengths and weaknesses of a tooth for the subsequent restoration. Unlike vital teeth, the surface area available for adhesive bonding is practically greater in non-vital teeth due to the exposure of the pulp chamber. This greater availability needs to be tempered against the unfortunate poorer bonding ability of dentine and the likely reduced enamel substrates.
Engagement of the pulp chamber may load the floor of the pulp unfavourably, especially where this is thin or anatomically unfavourable. The topography of the remaining tooth is also a source of deliberation. ### 6.3

### Bonding Issues

Dentine and root canal dentine differ in their bonding capacity in comparison to enamel. Outside of the differences in the effect of etching between enamel and dentine, root canal dentine provides a further challenge. Root canal dentine has inherently less dentinal tubules and as such the potential for micromechanical retention is reduced. The shape of the canal does not lend itself to optimal bonding. A tapering canal shape as the space progresses to the residual gutta-percha is likely to result in unfavourable polymerisation shrinkage away from the canal walls and so jeopardise the seal and attachment of the restoration. The bond within the canal system may be compromised by the materials present as a result of the endodontic treatment. At the macro level, the remnants of obturation, such as gutta-percha and cement sealer, hamper the bonding process purely through preventing contact with the canal surface. Optical magnification to remove materials to ensure a debris-free bonding surface is advisable, especially where gutta-percha is removed from within
4–5 mm of the apex of the tooth. This is unlikely to be achieved without good visualisation. The root surface can be cleansed by gentle ultrasonic instrumentation without significant contact with the root canal walls. Ultrasonic wave agitation is likely to disrupt any residual remaining biofilm in addition dental material. Concerns with regard to the effect that eugenol-containing cement sealers have on bonding due to their effect on composite initiators have not been fully proven in the literature, regardless, this effect can be theoretically mitigated against through flushing with ethanol once the root dentine is visibly clean and material-free. The canal space itself presents some practical challenges to the bonding process. In contrast to bonding to a smooth surface, the process of acid etching and applying the adhesive resin is likely to be more difficult due to inability to assure acid etch coverage throughout the length of the root canal and preventing the pooling of the bonding agent at the bottom of the canal.
