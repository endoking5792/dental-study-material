### 10.1

### Introduction

The traditional occlusal philosophy, the attainment of a cusp fossa relationship between opposing teeth statically and the ideal lateral and anterior guidance dynamically, may be a mantra that is unachievable for most patients outside of considering wholesale and destructive dentistry. Focus on attainment of the ideal for the dentition as a whole in this manner may distract away from situations where occlusal principles can be applied to individual tooth units or opportunities to prevent further

occlusally related pathology. Invariably, occlusal factors may contribute to the issues or tooth loss. Occlusal problems or developing pathology, such as non-­ working side interferences, are likely to be inconspicuous when compared to the more tangible signs of decay or periodontal disease. As such, recognising and treating occlusal problems from either a preventative or elective manner may not be as intuitive for practitioners.

The adhesive management of occlusal problems provides an avenue to optimise static and dynamic occlusal contacts to improve the dissipation of forces through the dentition to reduce forces that may either cause tooth surface loss or catastrophic fracturing or cracking of teeth.

### 10.2

### Cracked Teeth

With the marked increase in parafunction and bruxism associated with more prevalent mental health issues and the desire by our patients to retain teeth, heavily restored or otherwise, occlusally related problems such as cracked teeth now have a greater incidence. Cracked teeth are difficult to diagnose and even more difficult to treat, as they have significant repercussions where cracks develop into a catastrophic fracture, rendering the tooth unrestorable. Where patients constantly parafunction and brux teeth undergo repeated cycles of axial and nonaxial loading. Crack propagation results in pulpal symptoms where fluid, via the capillary effect, moves in and out of the crack and dentinal tubules as the tooth is loaded or exposed to temperature changes. As the crack widens, greater fluid exchange occurs, slowly increasing the painful stimulus. Once a crack becomes a fracture, the periodic nature of the pain can change to something more constant depending on the level at which the pulp is exposed or inflamed. Due to the nature of the fracture, teeth can become borderline restorable or even unrestorable. Steep angular fractures through the middle portion of teeth, mesio-distally or bucco-palatally, create defects difficult to retain either through direct or indirect restorations. The fractures tend to cleave through the passage of least resistance. This invariably puts teeth with mesio-occlusal or disto-­occlusal or a combination of both at a greater risk, especially where the restorative material is amalgam alloy, large with an undercut cavity form. Teeth that may be more susceptible to fractures are molars and premolars due to the increased mastication forces imparted upon these teeth (Fig.  10.1). Unlike anterior teeth, posterior teeth are loaded axially, but non-axial occlusal forces can be detrimental, imparting stress and strain on the teeth that are involved in lateral or protrusive guidance on working and non-working sides. Maxillary premolar teeth are morphologically at a disadvantage due to their occlusal shape. The inherent deep valley of the central fissure from the mesial to the distal makes the wedging of cusps apart more likely during function. This observation needs to be borne in mind when looking at the cusp fossa relationship of any dentition. Preventive devices such as stabilisation splints and bite raising appliances rely on compliance of the patient and, of course, cannot be worn all the time. These techniques could be considered to have their limitations in the prevention of further crack occurrences.

The purpose of a restoration, when faced with such extensive and catastrophic repercussions of non-axial loads, other than the widely accepted ‘functional’ and
‘aesthetic’ roles, should extend to the need for a ‘protective’ role. Restorations fill a void within a tooth but are also an apparatus that distributes occlusal force to the tooth, periodontal ligament and alveolus. A large amalgam alloy restoration, underor over-contoured or with sharp line angles, is likely to concentrate forces internally in the tooth that increase the chances of fracture. Through a change in restoration design concept, a restoration can be engineered to be a tool through which force is dissipated more evenly through the trunk instead of focusing force down the aforementioned path of least resistance. Cuspal coverage through indirect full crown, partial crown or onlay restoration provision achieves this by bracing the remaining cusps and preventing flexure. An alternative method is the provision of a direct composite resin onlay restoration that would act in the same protective manner, but also evenly redistributes occlusal force and can change the nature of the static and dynamic contacts (Fig. 10.1).

### 10.3

### Occlusal Redesign

The mechanical protection of teeth may occur through occlusal resign of the new restoration provided. The achievement of cuspal coverage has been more routinely achieved through the provision of an indirect crown, partial crown or onlay restoration. This can result in the removal of sound tooth tissue to achieve the occlusal aims to protect the remaining tooth tissue. In a situation where a molar tooth is heavily restored with an extensive three-surface amalgam alloy restoration, the treatment can take the form of simultaneous replacement of the restoration and reducing the risk of fracture. The first step would be to remove the restoration from the tooth and examine the floor of the cavity for signs of caries or fracture lines. At this point, if anything significant is observed, the patient should be made aware, as this may invariably signify a reduction in the prognosis of the tooth. Once the cavity has been restored with composite resin, attention may turn to the occlusal table. Rather than maintaining a deep cusp-fissure profile in the new restoration, an additive approach to cover both cusps with composite resin and flatten the occlusal table with a gentle cusp incline. This redesign results in the tooth and the restoration being loaded in compression with reduced wedging splitting forces (Fig. 10.1). To avoid localising occlusal contacts on this and other molar units in isolation, utilising this approach could be better suited in a direct adhesive rehabilitation where numerous teeth are restored and, as such, will share the occlusal load collectively in a different manner.
This change in occlusal design can also be achieved through a resin-bonded bridge provision for the replacement of a maxillary canine cantilevered from the first premolar tooth. This change in occlusal design of the molar may be prudent for the pattern of tooth loss. The absence of a canine and its guidance, which aids in the reduction of lateral forces, results in a greater need to protect premolar and molar teeth in this manner.

The alternative to providing direct cuspal coverage is the provision of an indirect adhesive restoration. The crucial consideration in this respect is the reduction of tooth tissue to an adequate level to accommodate a restoration without compromising available enamel surface for bonding, the pulp and the thickness of material required for strength in compression. Where full coverage is required, tooth reduction should be minimised to accommodate the chosen material and ensure optimal fit. Knowledge of the requirements of the material should be borne in mind when examining the tooth and planning the preparation. Incorporation of residual restorations can be considered, but it may be more beneficial to remove any restoration, resulting in fewer interfaces and potential sources of failure.

### 10.4

### Modifying Occlusal Guidance

Fortifying teeth, such as redesigning restorations and their occlusal loading, is one prophylactic measure to reduce cracks or fractures. A different approach may be to increase the likelihood of dis-occlusion of vulnerable units on anterior or lateral movements of the mandible. Anterior and canine guidance may be considered the gold standard for this role. Inevitably, over time, both may diminish as the teeth continually function and wear. In some patients, canine guidance may never have been present and can be created with the so-called ‘canine riser’ technique. This can be achieved through the direct provision of a composite restoration contoured to manage lateral loads to dis-occlude all other contacts on the working and non-­working sides. The composite resin needs to be applied in a thick increment of at least 2 mm, which can be designed through diagnostic waxing on articulated study casts and then translated into the patient’s mouth utilising a translucent silicone matrix technique (Fig. 10.2). Cases where aesthetic changes are proposed and require functional considerations may also be planned diagnostically and executed in this same manner, utilising matrix transfer techniques (Fig. 10.3).

Where the restoration is placed for this role is likely to be put under significant, and potentially constant occlusal loads, the clinician may consider an alternative material such as Type III gold alloy due to its superior wear characteristics and hardness (Figs. 10.4, 10.5 and 10.6). This would be retained utilising resin bonding in combination with a sandblasted intaglio surface. Other than ideal physical properties, this restoration modality can provide further advantages. The guidance surface can be designed on articulated models to ensure smooth transition from the intercuspal position laterally (Fig. 10.5). One disadvantage is that the metal alloy shows on the incisal and palatal surfaces, which are dependent on the smile line and tooth angulation, also (Fig. 10.6). Increasing parafunction and an ageing population are only likely to increase the instances where such restorations are indicated. Modification of functional occlusal guidance in this manner can be provided in isolation for patients with progressive wear of the canine tooth or incorporated into a treatment plan for either tooth replacement, such as implant provision, resin-­bonded bridge provision or for tooth surface loss cases.

### 10.5

### Tooth Over-Eruption

A variation on the presentation of localised occlusal changes is the overeruption of units into edentulous spaces. This situation can result in trauma to the opposing arch, an inability to restore the space with a prosthesis and restrictions on mandibular movements due to the ‘locked-in’ nature of the occlusion. This can be fairly frustrating and difficult to manage due to the extreme change in the occlusal plane.
This almost classically occurs when a tooth is extracted and the missing unit is not prosthetically replaced, or in cases of tooth wear (localised or generalised), resulting in progressive dento-alveolar compensation. The loss or absence of an opposing tooth unit can result in the passive eruption of the tooth into that space. This situation can result in drastic measures to aide restoration of the space. Clinicians may consider extraction, severe occlusal adjustment or orthodontic intrusion of the aberrant over-erupted tooth. Full mouth rehabilitation to even out the occlusal plane and accommodate a prosthetic replacement may also be considered.

Occlusal situations where a tooth presents requires restoration without the requisite interocclusal space can be challenging to manage. This is compounded further where there is a lack of occluso-pulpal space, which makes any mode of preparation fraught with concern about maintaining pulpal integrity. The occlusal height in this situation is also likely to be compromised, hence reducing relative axial wall height for retention.

An occlusal intrusion device acts like an orthodontic appliance, and parallels can be drawn from the use of Dahl appliances. They can take many forms. Where a molar tooth has severe tooth surface loss, space for restoration is either gained from a change in the occlusal vertical dimension or by creating greater height through procedures such as crown lengthening. A direct restoration can be applied to the occlusal surface of the tooth, increasing its coronal height and localising contact on this unit in isolation. This, of course, results in a lack of contact on all remaining units in the intercuspal position. The design of the occlusal platform of the local intrusion device should be flat, lacking any occlusal morphology, to aid axial loading. Commonly, the device, due to its interim nature, can be provided in glass ionomer cement or composite resin. This change induces intrusion and extrusion movements to re-establish contacts on the adjacent teeth. Once the requisite movements are achieved, a more definitive restoration can be provided in composite resin, ceramic or gold alloy. This restoration, unlike the intrusion device, can have occlusal morphology incorporated with a more definitive cusp–fossa relationship (Figs. 10.7 and 10.8).

Where posterior teeth are over-erupted and occluding on the opposing edentate space, cementation of a prosthesis which occludes wholly on the over-erupted unit can also rectify what is an extreme change in the occlusal plane.

The ‘local intrusion device’ concept can be developed further by considering the placement of a resin-bonded bridge prosthesis, which restores the edentate space at an increased occlusal vertical dimension designed to create adequate pontic space through the occlusal changes provided by the contact on the retainer wing.

### 10.6

### Open Bites

The presence or development of open bites (infra-occlusion) can be detrimental to both the function and aesthetics of the dentition. A lack of occlusal contact can be unsightly in the anterior dentition due to the spacing between the upper and lower teeth (Fig. 10.9). Similarly, a lateral posterior open bite may provide a further aesthetic issue where a unilateral difference between right and left can be just as unsightly for the patient due to asymmetry. These aspects of the smile may be worsened further in patients with high or wide smile arcs. From an occlusal perspective, the absence of posterior occlusal contacts from one or multiple units involved in open bites consequently means that the remaining functioning units are occlusally loaded more often or to a greater level (Fig.  10.10). Hence, establishing or re-­ establishing occlusal contact on such units has numerous benefits.

Due to the inherent space present between the teeth, the challenges in restoration provision are lessened. If direct or indirect adhesive restorations are to be provided, essentially making the teeth taller, the clinician needs to give attention to the new occlusal scheme design, the embrasure spaces, the interdental contact and the nature of the occlusal table. As the teeth are made taller, the crown–root ratio increases, and it may be prudent to consider a flatter occlusal anatomical plane to eliminate or minimise non-axial loading forces in dynamic function. When considering the restoration tooth margin interface, the clinician may consider creating a definitive preparation finish line if an indirect restoration is to be considered; however, a non-­ preparation approach may also be considered. Where direct restorations are provided, composite resin that is mechanically hard-wearing but also can be appropriately finished and polished is preferred for both functional and aesthetic reasons, where staining may develop. Ideally, the restoration should be confined to the occlusal table and have an identifiable, subtle margin for the purposes of future maintenance and/or replacement.
