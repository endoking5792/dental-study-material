In essence, the most amicable approach in managing this situation is the ‘reversal’ of the dentoalveolar compensation by way of manipulating occlusal contacts to induce a favourable change in tooth movements to create space for restorations.
This is largely achieved through a process of axial tooth movements by a combination of intrusion and extrusion of teeth.

The Dahl concept is a method of purposefully inducing physiological tooth movement that serves to create restorative space in worn dentitions in the presence of a lack of interocclusal space where dentoalveolar compensation has maintained functional teeth contact (Fig. 7.8). This is achieved through imposed changes to isolated arch occlusal contacts on anterior teeth with the provision of fixed restorations with the absence of restorations and occlusal contacts on posterior teeth.
Therefore, the OVD is solely increased by the restoration of the anterior teeth. Axial tooth movements will be induced, and the posterior tooth occlusal contact is later re-established by the compensatory dentoalveolar mechanism over a period of time.
The time for re-establishment to occur is dependent upon the amount of OVD increase. Occlusal contact re-establishment is a combination of the eruption of posterior teeth and the intrusion of anterior teeth that are in contact. The split between the processes is 60:40, respectively. For example, if the OVD is increased by 2 mm, re-establishment of occlusal contact would involve 1.2 mm of over-eruption and
0.8 mm of intrusion. This fairly crude calculation is not entirely accurate as 2 mm of change in occlusion anteriorly equates to approximately 1/3 of the amount posteriorly due to distance from the hinge action of the mandible.

The concept was first investigated by Dahl and colleagues in 1975 and developed further by clinicians in the United Kingdom from 1997 onwards to managing localised anterior TSL with a lack of interocclusal restorative space. This resulted in a series of clinical reviews and subsequent cohort studies that examined the utilisation of fixed ‘Dahl’ appliances provided through the restoration of worn teeth with adhesive composite resin restorations. During this period, clinicians were beginning to appreciate improvements in the predictability of adhesive bonding to tooth tissue.

### 7.3.4

### Restoration Design

To achieve axial intrusion of anterior teeth, the palatal portions of maxillary teeth restorations need to consider the vector of the forces applied and the angle of the long axis of the teeth. In general, the coronal portion of the maxillary anterior teeth are inclined buccally with the apical portion angled further palatally, this is in contrast to mandibular anterior teeth which are more perpendicular in their axis. To compensate for both of these aspects the palatal portion of the maxillary teeth restorations need to be designed with these factors in mind.

### 7.3.5

### Restoration Material Choice

The most efficient method of applying this technique is through the provision of adhesive restorations, where the volume of tooth structure allows. This largely involves the bonding of composite resin to the palatal aspects of affected maxillary anterior teeth, thus increasing the OVD. The worn incisal surfaces of the mandibular anterior teeth may also be treated in this manner. However, material choice is variable for the provision of the bite platform as further consideration is given to the nature of the tooth surface loss, where an attritive aetiology requires a more durable, harder occlusal surface to contact against in situations where bruxing or parafunction may be ongoing. For these clinical presentations, indirect restorative materials may be used, such as indirect composite resin, CAD–CAM resin-nano ceramic and metal alloy restorations.

### 7.4

### Direct Restorations

Where the TSL is minimal to moderate, such as incisal edge wear and Class V defects, direct composite resin restorations can be provided for the recreation of tooth morphology. A choice of a hybrid composite resin may be more favourable to provide greater resistance to further restoration wear and for an acceptable aesthetic result. Class V restorations can be restored with glass-ionomer cement as an alternative material.

Where more extensive restorations are planned, the advantages of the direct approach include efficiency of time and reduced financial cost as well as the serviceability of the restoration and any future subsequent revisions (Fig. 7.10).

The direct adhesive restoration of teeth requires a template to work towards to provide an optimal final functional restoration contour. This, in the main, is achieved through a diagnostic wax-up prescribed through diagnostic study casts, a facebow record and an appropriate interocclusal record. The diagnostic wax-up prescription is key to a successful definitive restoration as it forms the blueprint. Focus on details such as the incisal edge position, proportion ratio of the teeth, interdental embrasure spaces, design of the palatal occlusal platform, the nature of the contact points occlusal, and interdental and the marginal interface are immensely important (Fig. 7.9). The diagnostic wax-up constructed can provide patients with a preview of the proposed future treatment via mock-up with a temporary Bis-acryl material or can also be used to give the technician a guide for the construction of indirect restorations (Fig. 7.12).

### 7.5

### Indirect Restorations

The provision of indirect restorations for anterior teeth may allow improved functional and aesthetic control in comparison to the labour burden that multiple extensive restorations may provide, utilising direct techniques. The design of the adhesive indirect restoration needs to consider two aspects.
The first is the ability to successfully locate the abutment tooth and secondly to utilise maximise bonding surface area. A restoration lip over the junction between the palatal tissue and the incisal edge surface can make positive location easier. To reduce bonding or composite resin ingress into the gingival sulcus, 0.5 mm clearance will allow the clinician to visualise the restoration margin in this area and ensure the absence of overhangs and chronic gingival irritation (Fig. 7.9).

In situations where there is severe TSL, these features will not be defined enough to ensure adequate location, a restoration location jig may be used to ensure adequate and consistent seating at the cementation stage. A restoration wholly fabricated in composite resin can then engage all available surfaces in a positive manner.

### 7.6.1

### Direct Composite Resin

The following techniques utilise the diagnostic wax-up and transfer the proposed restoration shape directly to the mouth using a variety of silicone matrices. For the purposes of cleansability and ease of delivery, the technician needs to ensure that the interproximal areas are clearly defined and that the contact points between teeth are not too wide from a bucco-palatal aspect. The wider the wax-up is in this dimension, the bigger the contact point, the more difficult it will be to ensure a cleansable restoration and marginal integrity interproximally. Some clearance of the gingival margin area from the wax-up is also beneficial.

Once the teeth are isolated, they should be cleaned with prophy paste to ensure the absence of any plaque that can compromise etching and bonding. Bevelling the tooth margin can reduce the visibility of the restoration margin. A further stage of intra-oral sandblasting can improve the bonding potential of the tooth substrate.

### 8.1

### Introduction

The management of tooth surface loss (TSL) on posterior teeth, much in the same with anterior teeth, is heavily dependent on the aetiology of the tooth surface loss, whether it is ongoing and on the amount/volume of coronal dental hard tissue remaining. Intact posterior teeth manage loads by crushing food on a relatively flat

t.me/Dr_Mouayyad_AlbtousH

<!-- PDF page 266 -->

242

occlusal table. This, unlike anterior teeth, lends themselves to an adhesive approach.
Anterior teeth tend to shear food in function; this shear force can translate to the restoration tooth junction, resulting in adhesive bond failure. Molar and premolar teeth, in comparison, restored with an adhesively bonded cuspal coverage restoration will load the tooth-restoration complex in compression, which is tolerated much better than shear forces at the adhesive bond junction. As a result of this difference in loading, adhesive restorations may be more amenable in the posterior than the anterior dentition. This greater likelihood of compressive loading of the tooth lends itself to the greater expected loading due to the closer proximity to the masseter muscle and temporomandibular joint.

### 8.2

### Aetiology and Pattern of TSL

Tooth topography subsequent to erosive TSL can vary from patient to patient and is directly related to the nature and source of the erosion and its severity. A bulimic patient is likely to confer protection on the mandibular teeth through the tongue, which will result in the majority of stomach contents contacting the maxillary teeth.
This manifests clinically as asymmetric tooth volume, where the palatal surfaces are almost non-existent in severe cases and the buccal surfaces are entirely intact. Such an asymmetric shape may daunt the clinician due to the non-existent palatal aspect, which has limited potential to create resistance and retention form for indirect conventional cemented restorations. This lack of palatal tooth structure volume will result in a wholly short preparation if the tooth is going to be prepared for a conventional crown.

In contrast, patients with parafunction attritive tendencies essentially plane the tooth flat resulting in loss of all morphology and, in severe cases, revealing the underlying dentine. The topography of tissue in these cases may require greater thought, if not due to the non-axial forces that may or may not continue post-restoration.

Both TSL presentations can not only result in compromised tooth height for indirect conventional restoration but also compromised adhesive bonding potential due to the exposure of the underlying dentine, or more severely, tertiary dentine where the pulp previously resided, in the central portion of the tooth. Both conditions can also prospectively have an enamel margin for the restoration, but the bonding technique needs to be as optimal as possible due to the reduced amount of enamel substrate present. Dentine bonding, in severe cases, will be more likely and greater in volume than the enamel bond.

As teeth wear across their occlusal surfaces, they are likely to maintain occlusal contact through dentoalveolar compensation. This results in the need to plan the increase in occlusal vertical dimension due to the reduced volume of dentine mass between the opposing teeth pulps. ### 8.3

### Direct Restorations

Where the TSL is minimal, such as cupping on the occlusal surface, direct restoration can be achieved relatively easily. The cupped area is bounded by enamel, and the recreation of tooth morphology is likely to be straightforward with direct composite resin. These relatively early lesions within molar teeth need to be recognised early. Lesions wholly in enamel can be restored predictably prior to the lesion breaching into dentine. Once dentine is exposed, the rate of TSL progresses at a faster rate due to the inherent porosity and softness of dentine when compared to enamel. The lesions then broaden and widen as a result. ### 8.4

### Indirect Restorations

The indirect restoration of posterior teeth has historically been the accepted mode of treatment due to a lack of awareness of the ability or progression in the mode of direct restoration provision as previously described. Essentially, indirect restorations are more likely to be easier to deliver, especially in terms of management of the interproximal areas and labour burden that multiple extensive restorations may provide utilising direct techniques. Indirect restorations fabricated in a laboratory are likely to result in a more resilient restoration with superior physical properties and potentially better shape control with regard to the restoration design of the occlusal table for static and functional contact and interdental surfaces for contact points.

Choosing indirect over direct restoration provision is clinician-dependent based upon their individual knowledge of material selection, clinical skill set and experience. The choice may also be governed and influenced by patient finances. Clinicians can consider a variety of adhesively retained indirect materials, which vary with the benefits of differing aesthetic and physical properties. The planning, choice and execution of the adhesive restoration depend heavily on the individual nature of the presenting clinical case.

### 8.4.1

### Metal Alloy Onlays

The indirect restoration metal alloy material choice is confined to either nickel or cobalt chromium and type III gold alloy (60% gold content). When compared to nickel or cobalt chromium, gold alloy is kinder to opposing teeth from an abrasive aspect. When used in these situations, it can be cast into sections of 1–1.5 mm occlusal thickness, with nickel or cobalt chromium potentially less than this thickness due to their greater rigidity. However, with any material, the thicker the restoration, the stronger it will be.
