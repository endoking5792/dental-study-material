### 5.1

### Introduction

Operative procedures to manage carious cavities or the replacement of restorations that have been previously provided due to caries is a cornerstone of dentistry. The management of the carious lesion has evolved progressively over time, with the approach that less aggressive tissue removal is of greater benefit to the overall scheme of preserving pulp vitality and health. Unlike historical approaches requiring retention form incorporated into the cavity design, modern adhesive techniques aim to optimise the adhesive bond and seal without the need to grossly modify the residual tooth structure.

Tooth type, restoration class and size are significant predictors of difficulty in the provision of treatment, and with most clinical presentations, there often will be variants to the norm. Following a standardised protocol repeatedly will allow a clinician to develop confidence through their experience and will improve clinical efficiency and enjoyment of delivering these procedures.

### 5.2

### General Procedures

Prior to formal instrumentation, an occlusal examination should identify the intercuspal position contacts and any guidance movements that may be encroached upon by the new restoration. These need to be borne in mind during the restoration provision, as the rubber dam application will prevent ongoing assessment during instrumentation (Fig.  5.1). Without appreciation of the baseline static and dynamic occlusion from the outset, a carefully placed and anatomically contoured conformative restoration may need significant adjustment once the rubber dam isolation is removed and the occlusion checked and compared to the baseline record.

Adjuncts to cavity preparation include alumina blasting (using 50 micron alumina particles) the residual tooth tissue once the caries has been removed.
Unidentified remnant restorative material may also be removed during this process.

It is advised that ‘indirect air drying’ is undertaken by deflecting the air stream from the 3:1 syringe against an intraoral mirror towards the tooth such that any droplets will contact the mirror and not the tooth surfaces (Fig. 5.3).

### 5.3

### Class I Restoration

The Class I restoration is relatively predictable to deliver when considering the presence of an accessible enamel margin within the occlusal table. Once the tooth has been isolated and the cavity prepared and bonded, attention can be directed towards restoration placement and occlusal design.

In the Class I restoration, there are five bonded surfaces and one un-bonded surface, resulting in a C-factor of 5. Care must be made in restoring deep cavities with thin peripheral walls that shrinkage stresses do not have any profound negative effect upon the residual tooth structure.

Incremental application and curing of the composite resin material to reduce shrinkage stresses can be considered here. A flowable composite resin gingival base layer (up to 4 mm thickness) is placed to adapt to the cavity floor and block out the underlying dentine colour and light polymerised. Subsequent occlusal cuspal build-­up of the cavity is undertaken as each cuspal increment is placed and light polymerised for 10 s. Final tints and intensities may be added to the cuspal ridges and colour staining placed into the fissure pattern to allow harmonious aesthetic integration of the restoration into the tooth and the rest of the dentition (Figs. 5.7 and 5.8). As the restoration process is additive, with careful study of the static and dynamic occlusion and adjacent tooth anatomical form, the shape and contour of the restoration is built up with minimal occlusal adjustments and refinements required.

If the original occlusal anatomy of the tooth is still intact and not cavitated, a recording of this can be made prior to cavity cutting and caries access with a transparent silicone material or flowable composite material. The chosen material is applied to the surface with a microbrush immersed within prior to polymerising or curing. This is then later applied to the uncured final composite increment to shape the surface and recreate the previous anatomy prior to the final light polymerisation.
This ‘occlusal stamp’ technique will aid in the recreation of the original occlusal table anatomy within the composite restoration, with minimal occlusal adjustments required. It is described and depicted in Sect. 5.9.1.

### 5.4

### Class II Restoration

The Class II restoration is significantly more demanding on the clinician than the
Class I due to the proximal component of the cavity. Interdental pre-wedging with wooden or plastic interdental wedges can prise open the interdental contact to allow easier instrumentation in the interdental areas and protect the adjacent teeth from iatrogenic damage (Fig. 5.9). Wedges also serve to protect the rubber dam from tearing during cavity preparation (Fig. 5.10). The wedge placement should be just cervical to the deepest portion of the proximal box area. Where the interproximal contact is tight, the area can be relieved with an interproximal strip.

The interdental contact area of the affected surfaces needs to be identified prior to instrumentation. The cavity is initially cut in the fossa area and then is extended into the proximal area towards the interdental contact point. The outline form and base of the proximal box need to be smooth without any ledges. The contact point is to be fully broken in both buccal-palatal/lingual and occluso-gingival directions, and the enamel interdental box margins flared (Fig. 5.11). This will aid to place a matrix band with ease and, in addition, allows the convenient form of the final restoration for margin inspection in the long term.

The crucial aspect of restoration delivery is the management of the matrix band and ensuring fabrication of an adequate interdental contact point to the adjacent tooth, whilst ensuring the cavity is optimally restored.

Sectional matrix systems may have the advantage over traditional matrix bands and strips in that they are designed to allow delivery of anatomical restoration shapes. The metal matrix foils used are available in different sizes and are convex in form to allow for a natural anatomical contour of the restoration (Fig. 5.12). The size of the matrix foil should be chosen on the premise that it will be placed at the same height as the marginal ridge of the adjacent tooth and extends adequately to cover the base of the cavity in the gingival aspect. To avoid restoration gingival overhangs, the use of anatomically shaped plastic wedges approximates the matrix to the base of the tooth in the cervical area. A plastic wedge is optimally shaped to sit over the natural shape of the interdental papillae without over compression as is found with the triangular cross-sectional shape of wooden wedges (Figs. 5.9, 5.10, and 5.11). Plastic wedges also have the ability to be stacked upon one another to aid in the optimal seal of the foil to the tooth and to allow minimal finishing to the restoration. If it is found that the wedge compresses the matrix foil into the cavity and distorts its shape, it is possible to delay the placement of the wedge (‘delayed wedging’) and restore the first 2 mm at the base of the box first to allow the metal foil to then stabilise against the base restoration without shape distortion that will affect the interdental contour.

Under-contouring or over-contouring the restoration may result in stagnant areas.

### 5.6

### Class IV Restoration

The Class IV restoration is a common restoration provided for anterior teeth due to trauma resulting in an enamel dentine fracture or tooth surface loss, providing shortening of a unit. Depending on the presentation of dental trauma and the patient retaining the fragment, the fractured fragment may be adhesively rebonded back into place utilising a flowable composite resin material (Fig. 5.24).
If the fragment is not located, artificial replacement is indicated via restoration build-up.

The solitary Class IV defect on a maxillary anterior tooth is the most challenging of direct resin restoration even for a highly skilled experienced clinician for a variety of reasons. The task of mimicking and recreating shade, shape and the translucency with a synthetic material to the adjacent unit is demanding, especially in the high smile line or the aesthetically aware patient. This may provide a considerable test to the clinician, less so from being able to shape the restoration to resemble the correct morphology and more so, how the restoration will transmit light (Fig. 5.25).

There are numerous commercially available composite resin systems that allow material layering for the clinician to mimic natural tooth tissue as closely as possible to provide a seamless transition from natural tooth tissue. Resin systems offer a number of dentine and enamel shades of varying opacities and translucencies, and colour effects that the clinician has at their disposal. The points expressed here are
