### 4.2

### Treatment Planning

The shape and size of the spacing play a key role in the management of closure or slenderisation. Where a space is too large, restoration with an additive technique can possibly make teeth unnaturally oversized, especially if they are modified in isolation from adjacent units. An example of this is the closure of a large midline diastema, resulting in the proportion ratio (width:length ratio) of the central incisors becoming disproportionate (Fig. 4.11). In contrast, smaller areas of spacing may be considered negligible, and restoration may be considered less beneficial due to the ongoing restoration maintenance burden. In the former, orthodontics could be considered to reduce spacing to make closure with a restoration more amenable; in the latter, the space may be completed with orthodontics alone (Fig. 4.12). The management of spacing through adhesive bonding requires an appreciation of multiple factors. It cannot be crudely measured purely through the distance between the teeth.
The size of the teeth, their shape in addition to their gingival profile can make managing spacing challenging to achieve successfully.

Clinical- and laboratory-based diagnostic investigations can be performed to obtain more information on improvements to be made for the tooth proportion, shape and distribution of the spacing.

A chairside exercise when assessing spaces and tooth symmetry is the measurement of the spaces and tooth dimensions (Figs. 4.12 and 4.13). ### 4.3

### Key Clinical Factors

There should be consideration made of the key clinical presenting factors that affect the ability to successfully modify tooth form.

### 4.3.1

### Gingival Profile

The gingival profile plays an important role in the management of improving the form of teeth. Gingival profile is the superior determinant of shape of the tooth where the body and incisal areas provide the inferior determinants. The lip line, gingival arch and zenith profile frame the cervical portion of the tooth and so dictates the shape of the tooth out with of bonding or adhesion. It has a very specific role when dictating the form of adhesive resin bonding to close spacing between teeth.

A flat gingival profile reduces scope to make a tooth wider. This restricts the margin to widen as the tooth will become larger without an appropriate emergence profile and proportionally out of ratio with the tooth length. As a result of the flat profile, the tooth is square in appearance. Modification of the gingival profile is beneficial to space closure. Recreation of a gingival zenith through planned tissue removal creates an arc for natural widening of the tooth, which gradually progresses to the contact point (Fig. 4.25). The design of the arc depends on the envisaged change in width of the tooth, the size of the space and the length of the tooth. The gradient of the created arc also influences the nature of the widening and thus can make the tooth more triangular if the gradient is steep.

In patients where the smile line is low and the visible gingival margin is not shown, modification to the gingival profile may not be required to achieve a desirable aesthetic result (Fig. 4.26).

### 4.3.2

### Tooth Size and Shape

At the very basic level, the incisor tooth should be longer than it is wide. Taking the central incisor as an example, a desirable proportion ratio is approximately 80% (Fig. 4.23). Proportionally, this ratio is likely to vary depending on a number of factors, including the patient’s facial profile, the lips and smile and how these frame the teeth, the general appearance of the teeth and the gingival profiles. This ratio needs to be considered and tailored when teeth size and shape may be modified through augmentation with composite resin bonding or indirect adhesive restoration. If horizontal augmentation is significant resulting in closure of the interdental space, the negative effect on the tooth proportion ratio will be apparent, where the tooth will look too wide and square (Fig. 4.11). Hence, in situations where teeth are widened to close a diastema, length additions can be considered to maintain appropriate proportion ratios of the treated teeth (Figs. 4.22, 4.24, and 4.26). Length additions may be considered at the incisal edge (hard tissue addition) or at the gingival margin (soft tissue removal).

### 4.3.3

### Tooth Angulation and Position

In the aesthetic zone, maxillary teeth are generally inclined in a mesial direction where their long axes converge toward the midline. Variations in overjet and overbite complicated by tooth rotations may make restorative tooth modification via adhesive restoration augmentation challenging. It should be said from the outset that in cases where the teeth angulation and position correction is extensive, orthodontic treatment is advised to aid an optimal treatment aesthetic and functional outcome.

In situations where the patient would like a space closed, proclined maxillary anterior teeth exhibiting interdental spacing (Fig. 4.10) are more difficult to manage than those that are otherwise positionally correct or retroclined. Interproximal additions of composite resin material will make the teeth wider, closing the space, but simultaneously making the teeth more prominent. Making the teeth wider will result in more light reflection on the buccal surface, increasing their already prominent appearance. In contrast, retroclined teeth are inherently less likely to present with spacing due to a higher likelihood of crowding. The crowded dentition can provide an appearance that is considered unsightly by patients. Differences in bucco-palatal positioning results in significant differences in the manner with which neighbouring teeth reflect light. The further palatal or lingual a tooth resides, the greater the shadow cast on the buccal surface by the adjacent teeth and soft tissues such as the lips. These appearances may be compounded by inconsistent incisal edge profiles if teeth are angled towards the palatal or lingual.

The appearance of retroclined teeth can be modified by adhesive bonding of direct or indirect composite resin veneers to the facial surfaces, creating the appearance of teeth that are in line or more harmonious, making an aesthetic improvement (Figs. 4.31, 4.32, 4.33, 4.34, and 4.35). However, the restoration gingival emergence profile and contact point areas are likely to be changed and less ideal than the norm. The clinician needs to ensure the cleansability of the contact areas and ensure an adequate embrasure space is created for long-term restoration maintenance.

### 4.4

### Managing Spacing and Black Triangles

In isolation, spacing that is evenly distributed may be easier to manage than spacing that is wholly confined to a single interdental space. For example, an anterior tooth that has a distal interdental contact with a 2 mm mesial space may be more difficult to manage than one where there are two spaces of 1 mm on each interdental side.
This division of space allows the clinician to maintain some aspect of symmetry of the tooth unit with augmentation on either side. Where the space is wholly on one aspect, the augmentation to one side may result in difficulty in achieving a symmetrical result. This is due to the gingival zenith not coinciding with the mid-point of the incisal edge. The clinician in this instance may consider the option for orthodontic treatment or augment the space and not close it completely to avoid an unsightly appearance. Where the space is large in these instances, the clinician can decide to augment the adjacent tooth to the space and split the spacing between the two adjacent tooth units to avoid the asymmetry (Fig. 4.36). This, however, increases the restoration maintenance burden of the patient for the two teeth.

Where multiple spaces reside, the challenge to close spaces whilst maintaining symmetry is more challenging. If the generalised space distribution is asymmetric, merely closing spaces may not result in an entirely acceptable aesthetic result. ### 4.5

### Adhesive Technique

To aid treatment planning and visualisation of the planned treatment, analogue or digital diagnostic cast waxing of the proposed tooth form is carried out. The new tooth form offers improvements upon the aesthetic and functional contours of the existing tooth shapes.

The information from the diagnostic wax-up may be transferred or delivered to the teeth in a provisional or definitive way via a PVS stent with the use of either
Bis-acryl or restorative composite resin materials. If the latter material is used, a translucent PVS stent is used to allow light curing.

For both methods, the intra-oral transfer as a concept is similar with slight differences being in the detail of application. The methods are further described below.

### 4.5.1

### Prototype Provisional Try-In

This technique allows for the transfer of a Bis-acryl material to the teeth in a provisional manner to trial the aesthetic and functional changes for a short period of time (hours or days). The teeth are not adhesively treated with the material being engaged by ‘shrink fit’ into embrasure spaces and undercuts.

If a longer trial time is required (up to 8 weeks), the buccal surface of the teeth may be adhesively treated by spot etch and bond application to allow the Bis-acryl material to ‘shrink fit’ and adhesively bond to the teeth for improved retention and durability. The patient must be warned that the material in non-bonded areas may peel off and is likely to fracture due to the brittle nature of the Bis-acryl material.

### 4.5.2

### Prototype Definitive Restoration

If the tooth trial is to be placed for a longer period of time (6–12 weeks) or the teeth are to be restored in a definitive manner with restorative composite resin material, a translucent PVS stent is used to deliver the material to the teeth to aid in the curing of the material.

The receiving tooth surfaces may be partially or fully adhesively prepared by etching and bonding procedures. The amount of surface coverage for adhesive treatment (bonding area) is dependent upon the amount of time the functional assessment is required. More surface coverage is required for longer time spans, and full surface coverage is required for definitive restoration. It is also advised that for definitive restoration, if multiple adjacent teeth are being treated, then alternate teeth are treated simultaneously to prevent restoration fusion.
