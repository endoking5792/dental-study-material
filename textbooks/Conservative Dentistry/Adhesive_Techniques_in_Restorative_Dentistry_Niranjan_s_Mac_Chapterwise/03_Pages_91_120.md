### 3.3.3

### Vital Tooth Bleaching

Vital tooth bleaching treatment is an effective non-invasive method of improving tooth colour. It may be utilised as a sole course of treatment or prior to restorative treatment with adhesive restorations to improve upon the appearance of teeth that have aesthetic indications to be managed further (Figs. 3.23 and 3.24). It may be carried out in two ways: ‘in-office’ bleaching allows the clinician to bleach the teeth immediately in a single appointment for the patient. The soft tissues are isolated using a combination of lip retractors, cotton gauze and light polymerised resin. The teeth are cleaned, and the bleaching agent gel is applied directly to the teeth and activated using an activation blue spectrum light. The gel is replenished in 3–4 cycles of 15 min. This procedure has the advantage that the results are immediate for the patient in a short period of time (Fig. 3.25). The ‘home bleaching’ process allows the patient to carry out the process themselves at their discretion on instruction from the clinician and requires multiple appointments. The bleaching process may be carried out during the day or at night whilst the patient is asleep. This requires the production of a bleaching tray (bleaching agent gel carrier), which is fabricated from intra-oral impressions or digital scans (Fig. 3.26). The laboratory prescription needs to identify key areas, including the teeth planned for bleaching, the design and material of the bleach tray. Generally, bleaching trays are constructed from thermoplastic ethyl-vinyl acetate, and the general minimum thickness should be
1 mm. At the current time, there is no definitive evidence to show that the presence of bleach reservoirs (spacers) is more or less beneficial than in the absence of reservoirs. As the bleaching gels are viscous, the likelihood of material exchange between the gel approximated on the surface of the tooth and the main body of the reservoir is unlikely, as such, it may seem a waste of gel material to prescribe this. ### 3.3.4

### Non-vital Tooth Bleaching

Non-vital tooth bleaching treatment encompasses the exact same ethos as the home bleaching method described for vital teeth, the difference being that the internal aspect of the tooth will be treated in conjunction with the external part. It has been described as the ‘inside-outside’ bleaching technique as it applies a two-fold application of the bleaching agent into the root canal system in addition to the external surface of the tooth via a bleaching tray (Fig. 3.28). The required clinical stages for this treatment are depicted diagrammatically (Fig. 3.29).

Prior to considering ‘inside-outside’ bleach treatment, optimal root canal treatment should be completed. During endodontic instrumentation and irrigation, the tooth may lighten as a result of antibacterial disinfection treatment with sodium hypochlorite. Chemo-mechanical debridement followed by GP obturation should be concluded with a well approximated coronal restoration in the access cavity under normal circumstances, where teeth are not discoloured. Where bleaching is planned, the clinician may decide to progress to prepare the tooth to receive bleach without a period of monitoring of the endodontic outcome. This may be more of a consideration where the general restorative and endodontic prognosis of the tooth is good in the absence of a periapical lesion.

The non-vital tooth is likely to be the most discoloured unit in comparison to its vital neighbours, and as such, this tooth needs to be treated first, prior to bleaching adjacent teeth. ### 3.3.5

### Treatment Management

The patient should be encouraged to take a lead on how they manage their bleaching treatment. Self-assessment of progression provides the patient with the impetus to either continue or to cease treatment, depending on improvements in colour, but also the development of any tooth sensitivity. Once the bleaching treatment has stopped, the patient should be made aware that there will be some rebound or relapse in colour approximately 2 weeks after. This may be due to residual free radicals remaining, affecting the optical properties of the tooth, and the patient should be reassured that the tooth shade is merely stabilising during this period.

### 3.3.6

### Treatment Complications

Vital tooth bleaching can present with local adverse effects such as tooth sensitivity and gingival/mucosal irritation. Utilising hydrogen peroxide as the bleaching agent over carbamide peroxide results in a faster process but also likely to result in a greater risk of adverse effects to the patient.

A complication issue more specific to non-vital tooth bleaching is the development of external cervical root resorption (ECRR). This condition can be very difficult to treat and in some cases, can render the tooth unrestorable. The presence of hydrogen peroxide within the tooth and diffusing towards the root surface via dentinal tubules has been identified as a possible mechanism causing the inflammatory resorptive process. The more patent the dentinal tubules, the increased amount of diffusion. If the tooth has been previously traumatised, cementum may be absent in the cervical region, especially where a luxation or avulsion injury has occurred, making the tubule patent through and through. An association may exist with patients with immature teeth, and so wider tubules and less than fully developed root walls provide a portal for hydrogen peroxide movement.

There is limited evidence in the dental literature illustrating a significant clinical effect of bleaching on the enamel tissue. Some studies illustrate loss of the aprismatic layer of enamel whilst others have shown decrease in surface hardness, the actual clinical relevance of these changes has not been entirely quantified.
