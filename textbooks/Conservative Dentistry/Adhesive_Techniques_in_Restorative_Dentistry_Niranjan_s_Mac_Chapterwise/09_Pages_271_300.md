### 8.4.2

### Tooth Coloured Onlays

The indirect restoration tooth coloured material choice are composite resin, resin nano-ceramic or all-ceramic materials. High-strength lithium disilicate glass-matrix ceramic materials are the preferred choice of the all-ceramic material selection.

Resin-based materials can be made into sections of 1.5–2 mm occlusal thickness with lithium disilicate potentially made less than this thickness due to its greater strength.

The designs of the preparations for these restorations take into account the same two aspects as required for the metal alloy restorations, location and maximising bonding surface area. However, the creation of defined grooves or slots on axial wall areas is not advised for these materials.

### 9.1

### Introduction

Resin-bonded bridge (RBB) restorations are an efficient and predictable method of restoring edentulous spaces. Since their inception in the 1970s as a method for splinting periodontally involved teeth, the technique has gradually evolved due to improvements in adhesive resin technology and knowledge on failure mechanisms.
In the 1980s to early 1990s, the technique came under scrutiny due to a number of studies documenting poor survival rates. The initial popularity was stunted as failure rates were less than acceptable, and as such, the technique fell out of favour. In

In addition, and in keeping with certain prosthodontics ethos’ from this era, the RBB may also have suffered from over-engineering where multiple abutments were engaged (Fig. 9.1) when a more simple design would later be revealed as more successful. Fortunately, a cohort of further studies maintained the excellent promise shown during this period. This culminated in the publication of a long-term follow-
­up of 832 resin-bonded bridges by Djemal et al. in 1999. This study outlined specific requirements relating to the design of the bridgework whilst also emphasising the need for an optimal bonding technique. Single pontic single abutment bridges provided the greatest survival time, with bridges on anterior teeth, and this was greater than on posterior units. A sound enamel substrate surface was shown to be another significant variable. This was subsequently followed by a study by King et al. in 2015, illustrating excellent survival over 15 years, especially with single-­ unit single cantilever designs. Some important proven points from this landmark study were that a lack of abutment preparation resulted in greater longevity than prepared abutments and that operators with greater experience generally exhibited longer restoration survival. History shows that a lack of understanding or apathy towards ‘good adhesive bonding’ has resulted in less than expected outcomes. These results could be considered timely when considering the emerging complication rates of both single-unit implants and implant-supported bridgework.

### 9.4

### No Tooth Preparation

As a concept, a ‘no tooth preparation’ bridge design may initially be counterintuitive to most. With a lack of any abutment preparation, there is an absence of restorative space to accommodate the thickness of the bridge retainer and a lack of resistance and retention form with a total reliance on micromechanical and chemical adhesion of the retainer, cement lute and hard dental tissue substrate. The evidence for such an approach is established largely through two British studies based in secondary care specialist units. Djemal and King and colleagues in 1999 and
2015, respectively, demonstrated predictable success rates with this approach. This may be explained as the abutment surface is unblemished, resulting in a totally enamel substrate adhesive bond which is significantly superior and more predictable in comparison to a dentine substrate adhesive bond.

The evidence also points towards the single retainer pontic cantilever design being the most successful in most anterior and posterior situations (Fig. 9.8). The clinician may also consider abutments on either side of multiple pontics (fixed– fixed) to provide greater bonding surface area as well as support from two separate teeth (Fig. 9.9). The favoured material for the resin-bonded bridge prosthesis framework is a non-precious metal alloy of nickel chromium, largely due to its inherent rigidity, predictable bonding surface, ease in casting and its economic benefit. With any choice of framework material, the retainer wing design should be unperforated and engage a maximum surface area across all available abutment tooth surfaces (mesial, distal, incisal/occlusal and cervical/lingual/palatal) to provide optimal retention. If the fissure patterns are deep, the clinician may find it difficult for the bridge to seat. In these circumstances, technical colleagues could block out the fissure areas with a die spacer.

Other than improved adhesive potential, increased surface area coverage of the abutment tooth also plays a role in the manner in which the resin cement is loaded.
Cements loaded in compression are less likely to result in failure than those exposed to shear or tensile forces. If the abutment tooth has an occlusal contact transition from retainer wing to tooth surface, this is likely to result in shear stresses being translated during lateral movement to this junction, and there is an increase in the risk of the restoration debonding via resin cement failure. To reduce these stresses, the greater the retainer wing extends in coverage across functional surfaces, the less risk there is of functional occlusal contact guidance moving across such a transition.

Metal alloys bend and flex due to their physical ductility, whereas the inherent brittle nature of ceramics allows them to catastrophically fracture through their weakest section (typically the connector). The degree to which this manifests depends on the dimensional size of the prosthesis framework itself. The longer and narrower the framework, the greater the potential for flexure and fracture. In contrast, thicker and shorter frameworks will have less potential for flexure and fracture due to the increase in stiffness. This phenomenon is relevant to the framework design of the prosthesis. The more flexure, the greater the risk of prosthesis debond or fracture as the prosthesis flexes away from the abutment tooth. ### 9.8

### Adhesive Technique

The adhesive technique for resin-bonded bridge cementation does not vary a lot from direct resin bonding in that management of moisture in the operative area is key to restoration success. One other aspect of note is the plaque status of the abutment at fit. Commonly, plaque and biofilm may be present around the cervical margin of the tooth compromising the bond as this substrate area will not be adhesively prepared optimally. The abutment tooth should be cleaned with a prophylaxis and ultrasonic scaler prior to adhesive treatments to the recipient substrates. One may be hesitant to remove the surface contaminants in these areas just before adhesive bonding procedures, as gingival bleeding may prevent and potentially contaminate adhesive cementation at that visit. To avoid such circumstances, instilling appropriate oral hygiene measures in good time before bridge provision would be the best pathway.

Where challenges may arise is from the ability to successfully isolate the abutment to allow location of the retainer wing, which is likely to be in close proximity to the gingival margin and the pontic contact to the edentulous ridge, which can potentially make rubber dam isolation challenging. The importance of the bridge fitting passively onto the abutment at the diagnostic try-in stage, as well as the pontic onto the edentulous ridge site, should not be assumed but assured very early on in the treatment process (Fig. 9.24).
