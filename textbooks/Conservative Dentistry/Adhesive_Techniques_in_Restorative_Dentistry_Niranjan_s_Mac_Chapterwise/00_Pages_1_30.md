## Foreword

It is with great pleasure and privilege to write a foreword to this new book, Adhesive
Techniques in Restorative Dentistry, by Drs Aws Alani and Mahul Patel. This book represents the paradigm shift in Restorative Dentistry, Prosthodontics and Operative
Dentistry during the last 20 years. Contemporary adhesive approaches to tooth restoration provide a predictable and sensible option that is minimally invasive compared to the more traditional approaches of conventional tooth preparation and retention.

They have methodically delivered 10 chapters spanning various patient presentations, from tooth surface loss to tooth replacement, illustrating the text with step-by-­ step photographs. The authors, Drs Aws Alani and Mahul Patel, are diverse and different in their backgrounds and training, but are united by their expertise, knowledge and technical skill. ## Preface

The purpose of this textbook is to highlight, describe and depict current adhesive techniques used to manage and treat common clinical conditions across the breadth of Restorative Dentistry. This book is an essential purchase for undergraduate and postgraduate students where modern operative techniques and contemporary restorative dentistry methods are taught. It is aimed to be used as an aide memoir instruction guide for both general and specialist practitioners working in primary and secondary care environments to develop and enhance existing clinical skill sets.

Achieving clinical outcomes whilst delivering minimally invasive techniques is supremely beneficial as teeth and their associated structures are irreplaceable.
During our times opinions have swayed and new technology has replaced the old.
Despite so much change one entity has remained true, adhesive dentistry applied optimally and with understanding, works. The delivery of treatment in this manner requires ingenuity and presence of mind through processing material science, dental anatomy and self-discipline. Clinical skills in Dentistry are won through the continual cycle of practicing, implementing and reflecting. The cases and knowledge captured in this text are the culmination of collective 40 years of experience and practice that have been incrementally built continually since our first phantom head sessions in the late 1990s as Dental Students at King’s College London. Distinction is achieved through contemplating the last restoration, critiquing oneself, and saving those sentiments for the next. This notion was classically personified by
Aristotle:

Excellence is an art won by training and habituation. We do not act rightly because we have virtue or excellence, rather we have those because we acted rightly. We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence is not an act but a habit.

We would like to thank all our colleagues we have met through this great profession and more specifically those that trained and supported us through our journey to reach the level of knowledge and experience we enjoy today. ### 1.1

### Introduction

The perception of what may constitute a ‘healthy’ dentition is likely to be subjective and differ from patient to patient and dentist to dentist. At the very basic level, patients may expect, rather than aspire to, a dentition that is pain-free, functional and aesthetic. Meeting these expectations, despite their seemingly fundamental nature, may seem daunting to the clinician, depending upon the individual clinical challenges that present. The solutions to the clinician may be very apparent, but what may be more testing is the ideals and aspirations of the patient.

The paternalistic approach to oral care has progressively ebbed away from everyday practice due to the rapid increase in patient knowledge subsequent to the digital information revolution. Patients tend to question more readily, wanting to know the process, outcome and longevity of what we provide. Almost concurrently, the increase in dento-legal litigation has tempered clinicians to develop strategies to reach or even surpass patient expectations but achieving these utilising methods that minimise damage or reduce patient exposure to risk. The well-informed patient will be aware of technological advances and understand the positive repercussions of treatment. This ‘knowledge’ will not be to a professional level but to a degree that will result in an impetus to seek involvement in clinical decision-making, the outcome of which may be scrutinised by the patient to a greater level than ever before.
This increased expectation, coupled with the potential legal repercussions, has resulted in the development of more innovative operative techniques to solve common clinical problems.

The philosophy of attaining successful clinical outcomes through ‘maintaining health of what remains’, as opposed to ‘destroying to achieve a result’, is likely to be the preferred aim of modern contemporary dentistry for both clinicians and patients.

### 1.2

### Patterns of Need

The management of plaque-associated disease is the established core of the dental profession. Repercussions of caries or periodontal disease require comprehensive management from the optimisation of oral hygiene measures right up to the unfortunate need for tooth extraction. Patterns of need have changed due to the increased prevalence and reporting of non-plaque-associated disease processes and conditions such as tooth surface loss, congenital tooth malformations and dental trauma.
Other emerging pathologies, such as cracked tooth syndrome, are challenging due to inconsistent signs and symptoms and the need to manage a sensitised vital pulpal complex with compromised dental structural integrity. If teeth lose pulp vitality, from cracks or caries, the planning for root canal treatment should embrace the need for an optimal coronal seal and cuspal coverage strategy at the outset of endodontic treatment. If teeth prove to be unrestorable, patients are less inclined to accept extraction as the remedy for their problem, however absolute a solution that may be, largely due to the social stigma as well as physical and mental co-morbidities associated with partial edentulism. Contemporary solutions for tooth replacement, such as dental implants, do carry significant morbidities during provision and have emerging biological and mechanical complications, the remedies for which are not forthcoming. Alternative tooth replacement methods must be explored.

The twenty-first-century patient is also more likely to suffer and report mental health issues. These can manifest in their opinions and requests for treatment.
Patients with body dysmorphia may be convinced of the need for wholesale cosmetic changes to otherwise intact, healthy teeth. Managing these conditions whilst

With this plethora of problems, plaque-associated or not, physical or mental, the ‘value’ in treatment should focus on how we can achieve patient satisfaction and health without undue damage. Patients may realise that treatment that preserves tooth health whilst improving function and aesthetics is more valuable than any other mode of provision.

### 1.3

### The Need for Change

Traditional prosthodontic modes of indirect extra-coronal restorations focus on the attainment of resistance and retention form through a radial preparation that is near parallel or at least has a total occlusal convergence angle of 6°. This approach is
‘subtractive’ in its nature as it requires the removal of an increased volume of tooth tissue for the provision of the restoration. The genesis of these ideals occurred during a period where restorations were cemented to prepared abutment teeth with weak luting cements. This approach may result in a risk of pulp vitality loss. Heat generation or pulp exposure will compromise the pulp, making the need for root canal treatment more likely. It is known that loss of pulp vitality and the subsequent root canal treatment weaken teeth mechanically. Successful endodontics is also technique sensitive and brings co-morbidities to the patient, such as further pain and prolonged treatment. With the cervical extension of extra-coronal restorations, marginal fit becomes crucial to avoid positive or negative ledge creation, which aggregates plaque deposits and is a prelude to caries, gingivitis or periodontal defects. It would therefore seem a natural progression to reduce or even omit risks to patients by providing predictable restorations that do not compromise tooth tissue or threaten pulp or gingival health.

### 1.4

### Survival—Tooth Versus Restoration

Research examining the longevity of restorations is popular as it provides clinicians with a tangible time period for the length of service of restorations. These research threads may focus on restoration survival as opposed to tooth survival, or the endpoint of restoration failure as opposed to tooth extraction. Tooth survival when linked to types of restoration would be more relevant to the patient and clinician.

These factors need to be considered more generally when considering the patient and the need for further treatment. The placement of a restoration for a patient of
20 years of age is likely to require replacement at least, at a conservative estimate, six times during the lifetime of the patient if it is assumed that the tooth is retained and not extracted. This is on the assumption that by the sixth replacement, there is adequate tooth tissue available to seal the remaining structure and manage occlusal loads without catastrophically failing. If the initial preparations remove significant tooth tissue or the disease process, such as extensive caries, leaves minimal volume

This then progressively increases the risk of tooth loss as less and less tissue remains. Fundamentally, plaque-associated disease threatens tooth retention, as can overtly aggressive dental intervention.

### 1.5

### The 3 A’s of Adhesive Dentistry

Traditional prosthodontic approaches to the assessment of teeth prior to restoration may consider the volume and height of the tooth to deliver either an intra-coronal or extra-coronal restoration, depending on clinical case presentation. This is also true of the adhesive approach to treatment, which wholly depends on the bonding surface area. The key to successful adhesion is the presence of an optimal bonding substrate, its subsequent isolation and observing an adhesive bonding protocol. This largely translates to the presence of enamel, ideally, throughout the prospective margin of the restoration, the ability to isolate the tooth and proficiency in a bonding sequence.

Tooth tissue is physiologically responsive to its environment. At the higher level, this may involve the utilisation of growth factors present within the pulp or dentine to respond to bacterial invasion. More routinely, teeth can adapt to occlusal loading through bending and deforming largely due to the elasticity of dentine and the ability of the periodontal ligament to manage applied forces. Unfortunately, dental materials are yet to be able to mimic natural tooth tissues in this way without the combined use of dental adhesives. In addition, minimising tooth tissue removal during restoration is likely to retain many biomechanical advantages.

The key advantage of adhesive dentistry is that it can be provided on both a ‘subtractive’ and on an ‘additive’ basis, nullifying the need to remove tooth tissue and the co-morbidities that may bring. Without a significant volume of tooth tissue removal, treatment is provided ‘atraumatically’; any initial pulp insult through caries or tooth surface loss is not worsened or amplified by treatment. Gingival health is also maintained where restoration margins are kept equi- or supragingival, or the gingiva is retracted during the procedure without the creation of a positive ledge profile.

‘Additive’ as opposed to ‘subtractive’ restorative dentistry is more technique sensitive and requires improved operator skill and a greater appreciation of restorative material science, knowledge of occlusion and the physiology of tooth movement.
Where indirect restorations are essentially designed and constructed by technical colleagues through information provided to them, the direct adhesive additive approach puts more onus onto the clinician to formulate restoration design and manage its delivery through various chairside clinical stages. In essence, the dental surgeon can now interplay the roles of both technician and clinician.

Patients may analyse the treatment we provide through photographs of themselves, putting greater pressure on clinicians to deliver aesthetic outcomes. Recreating tooth tissue, the small nuances, from surface texture to colour and shape, can be challenging and humbling. Conventional restorations, provided in a wholesale manner for multiple adjacent anterior units, remove the onus of reproducing natural tooth appearance from the clinician onto technical colleagues. In comparison, the techniques and cases showcased in this book have been wholly designed and delivered through the hands of the clinician who can better appreciate the features of the natural dentition as they reside during the clinical appointment. ### 1.6

### Business Model

One of the main keys to business is customer satisfaction, the ability to satisfy patients to a degree that they return to your clinic rather than to another office and attract their family and friends to attend for your clinical service. This is a key determinant of business growth and profitability. What could be more convincing to family and friends than an improved smile, ‘whiter’ and more uniform without gaps, that did not require local anaesthetic or ‘drilling’? These positive aspects of ethical minimally invasive aesthetic dentistry are huge selling points over other modes.
This provides the satisfaction to the patient or ‘value’ attached to the treatment they are being provided with. ‘Value’ may not only be provided when comparing the ratio between the monetary outlay to the treatment provided. With healthcare and more often invasive procedures, the biological cost of treatment triangulates the inter-relationship. The patient is not simply purchasing a direct adhesive restoration or a tooth bleaching treatment; they are choosing self-preservation and self-­ maintenance. The business advantages for the clinician are apparent in more ways than one.

Through the additive adhesive approach, restoration renewal can be relatively predictable and consistent, provided the highly biologically ‘valuable’ enamel is maintained. However, the latter cannot be said with any guarantee for the provision of replacement conventional restorations. Hence, the business model is based on the selling points of minimal invasive treatment provision from the outset, but also the predictability in inevitably replacing the restoration and the future treatment fees attached to this.

Consent is the permission granted in full knowledge of the possible consequences of treatment. This process is ongoing and active throughout treatment provision, during which the patient can withdraw consent at any time. With the majority of any operative dental treatments, the physical process of instrumentation removal of tooth tissue produces some form of morbidity or damage. For example, the repercussions that follow from the provision of a subtractive approach may be potentially multiple, with varying consequences and levels of tooth-related or soft tissue-related damage. These risks need to be balanced against the biological, functional and aesthetic improvements the patient may gain from the restorative procedure. ### 2.1

### Introduction

The success of any adhesive dentistry technique is built upon a good foundation understanding of restorative dentistry and how this relates to the manipulation of dental materials and their adhesive adjuncts, coupled with the management of the residual tooth bonding substrate. Any adhesive restoration, be it a direct composite resin restoration or the replacement of missing teeth with indirect resin-bonded bridges, can only be as good as the procedural technique and optimisation of the materials utilised to retain it. A nonchalant attitude to adhesive bonding protocols, from lack of moisture control, not following manufacturers’ recommendations on how to manage adhesive materials or the assessment and control of the occlusion, cannot be excused when the treatment outcomes are poor. The development of adhesive technology and understanding of factors required to gain predictable clinical outcomes should be observed by clinicians to establish greater predictability and result in increased confidence in the treatment modality provided. The foundation of good adhesive practice is based upon following recommended evidence-based treatment protocols and applying them to the presenting clinical case as best as possible.

### 2.1.1

### The ‘Bonding Stack’ Concept

The success of any adhesive bonding process is reliant on every step being delivered as optimally as possible. Any weak or sub-standard steps in the process are likely to result in a deficient restoration or one that is more likely to fail in an undefined shorter time period. The hydrolytic stability of the bond at the interfacial layer is subject to degradation over time by the process of ageing; therefore, care must be taken from the outset to carry out this procedure in dry conditions to eliminate moisture contamination. The ‘bonding stack’, from management of the operative environment, optimising substrate treatment up to and including the adhesive techniques and occlusal control can ‘collapse’ if one stage or process is compromised. The onus for clinicians should be that of instilling good adhesive bonding protocols for the treatment they provide and ensuring that this is integral to the provision of the restoration.

### 2.2

The larger the dental hard tissue surface area available for adhesive bonding, the improved retention of the restoration. The opportunity to increase the surface area of enamel or dentine availability for bonding can take the form of soft tissue manipulation or removal prior to the adhesive procedure.

Soft tissue gingivectomy in clinical situations where there is residual periodontal pocketing can be achieved by various different instruments and techniques. These include a surgical scalpel blade, dry gingival curettage with a diamond bur or a soft tissue removal bur or the use of an electrosurgery unit. These methods aim to sculpt
