NOBLE VS BASE METALS
The metal restorations are generally fabricated from
either noble metals or base metals. ‘Noble’ refers to
metals with marked resistance to oxidation and
chemical reaction. Sometimes, the term ‘precious’ is
also used. Precious usually refers to economic value.
‘Base metal’ refers to the metal elements that are
chemically reactive to their environment. Recently,
titanium has also been used for casting.
Noble metals can be gold based (gold, silver,
copper along with platinum, zinc, iridium) or
palladium based (palladium, silver, gold along with
zinc, indium, iridium). The base metals are mostly
cobalt based (cobalt, chromium, tungsten along with
copper, nickle and gallium) or nickel based (nickle,
chromium, iron along with boron, copper and
manganese).
Taggart (1907) was credited for describing casting
procedures in cast restorations.

metals are summarized in Table 16.1.
Amongst cast metals, gold alloy is most common.

Indications of Cast Restoration

efficient in replacing lost tooth structure and also
for supporting remaining tooth structure (Fig. 16.1

Fractured teeth/marginal ridges are also
indications for cast restorations.

contact, contour, marginal ridges and embrasure
areas, which are vital for health of the perio-
dontium (Fig. 16.2).
3. Modifying contacts and contours (superior control

reshape the deviated contact, contour and
embrasures.
4. Correction of occlusion: If any change for occlusal

loss; need cast restoration.

7. Subgingival lesions: In cavities with subgingival

restorative material is the cast gold.
8. Fracture lines: Fracture lines in enamel, especially
in teeth having extensive restorations are ideal for
cast restorations (full crowns, onlays).

CAVITY DESIGN FOR CAST RESTORATIONS
The factors that influence design of the cavity are:
• Length of the clinical crown
• Anatomic characteristics of the occlusal, proximal,
buccal and lingual surfaces
• Position of the tooth in the arch
• Occlusal and proximal relationships
• Esthetic issues
• Relationship and condition of the soft tissues
• Extent and location of the carious lesions
The conventional Black’s principles are followed
for preparing cast restorations.
1. Outline Form
a. External Outline Form
The external outline form for the gold inlay is, by and
large, similar as that for amalgam cavity. The
placement of bevels and taper make the outline form
slightly wider for cast restorations. The beveled
cavosurface margin is placed in sound tooth tissue
(Fig. 16.4A to  G).
b. Internal Outline Form
The amount of taper varies with depth of the
preparation. The taper should not be visible to the
eye. In shallow preparations, parallelism enhances the
resistance and retention form of the preparation. Deep
cavities require taper to facilitate seating of the
restoration.
The pulpal floor is positioned 1.75 to 2.0 mm into
dentin below the central groove (0.5 mm into DEJ).
The cervical floor should be in sound tooth tissue.
The cervical floor is positioned after caries removal
(Fig. 16.5A). The bevel is placed to establish the
cervical finishing line (Fig. 16.5B).
Line angles in both the occlusal and proximal
portions of the preparation should be well defined.
The axiopulpal line angle is slightly rounded
(Fig. 16.6A). The flare of the proximal walls should
form axioproximal angles of 100° to 110°.
2. Resistance and Retention Forms
Lateral/tangential forces may cause displacement of
the restoration. Frictional retention can be achieved
by length of dentin and enamel walls grasping the
restoration (intracoronal retention). The cement bond
alone will not provide retention for the casting.
Cement provides only a moderate mechanical lock
between the minute irregularities of the cavity walls
and the casting surface.
9. Oral hygiene: The tooth–cement–cast interphase
is the weak link in cast restorations; patients
performing adequate hygiene measures are
indicated for cast restorations.
Contraindications of Cast Restoration
1. Developing and deciduous teeth: Young dentition with
large pulp chambers are contraindicated for cast
restorations.
2. High plaque/caries indices: Patients with rampant
caries and poor oral hygiene should not be given
cast restoration.
3. Occlusal disharmony: Cast restorations should not
be used in patients with severe occlusal interference
or other defects in the stomatognathic system.
4. Dissimilar metals: Gold-based castings are avoided
in patients already having silver restorations.
5. Where esthetics is prime consideration, because
metallic restorations display metal color.
Advantages of Cast Restoration
• Compressive strength, tensile strength and shear
strength of alloys used for cast restorations are far
greater than those of amalgam.
• Cast techniques reproduce precise form and minute
detail; maintain the detail even under functional
stresses.
• Gold cast restorations are not affected by tarnish
and corrosion processes in the oral environment.
• Cast restorations provide fewer voids, less internal
stresses, and maximum bonding between the
component phases.
• Finishing and polishing is better.
• No volume change after placement.
• Gold castings have a coefficient of thermal
expansion (12 × 10–6/°C); approximately similar to
that of tooth structure.
• Better control of contacts and contours.
Disadvantages of Cast Restoration
• Being a cemented restoration, several interphases
will be created at the tooth–cement–casting
junction. Leakage under a cast restoration is
alarming; more pronounced gingivally.
• Cast restorations necessitate extensive tooth
involvement, which may create hazards for the
vital dental tissues.
• The cathode nature of cast gold alloys towards
amalgam may lead to galvanic deterioration of
amalgam, if these two restorations are placed
adjacent to or opposite to each other.
• Cast alloys usually have a high abrasive resistance,
more than that of tooth enamel (may abrade the
opposing tooth and create occlusal imbalance).

Factors Affecting Retention of Cast Restorations
The factors affecting the retention of cast restorations
are:
a. Magnitude of the dislodging forces: Generally,
sticky foodstuffs act as a pulling force. The
quantum of vertical and oblique forces also tends
to dislodge the restoration. The magnitude of the
dislodging forces depends on the stickiness of food,
occluding and lateral movement of jaws, the
surface area and texture of restoration being pulled.
b. Geometry of the tooth preparation: Retention of
cast restorations depends on the geometric form
of the preparation, rather than on the intervening
cements. (Cements act by increasing the frictional
resistance between tooth and the restoration.)
Cement is effective, only if the restoration has a
single path of withdrawal, i.e. the tooth is prepared
to restrain the free movement of the restoration.
The geometrical forms generally responsible for
retention are:
i. Taper: A basic requirement for the intracoronal
cast gold restoration is that the cavity walls must
diverge from the floor of the preparation
externally. A similar requirement for all
extracoronal preparations is that walls must
converge from the cervical to the occlusal
surface. This is the concept of taper. Removal
of wax pattern and insertion of casting is
facilitated by the taper.
Each intracoronal and extracoronal preparation
has a line of draw that describes the path of
insertion and removal of the casting, which
determines the axis of taper (Figs 16.8A to C
and 16.9A, B).

TYPES OF MARGINS IN A CAST RESTORATION
Various types of margins are tried in a cast
restoration; however, bevel, chamfer and the shoulder
are commonly used margins (Fig. 16.13 A to C). The
difference between chamfer and shoulder is that
chamfer is less than 0.5 mm wide and is rounded;
while, shoulder is more than 0.5 mm wide and sharp.
The chamfer is given where the metal is malleable;
whereas, the shoulder is given where the metal is not
malleable.
iii. Stress concentration: It is established that the

are not uniform throughout the cement, but are
concentrated around the junction of the axial
and occlusal surfaces.
iv. Type of preparation: The retention of a complete

Adding grooves to a preparation with a limited

increased significantly. However, where the
addition of a groove limits the path of
withdrawal, retention is increased.
c. Roughness of the surfaces being cemented: When
the internal surface of a restoration is smooth,
retentive failure occurs through the cement–
restoration interface. The retention can be increased,
if the inner surface of the restoration is roughened
(inner surface is roughened with 50 m alumina
particles).
d. Materials being cemented: The more reactive the
alloy, the more will be adhesion with the luting
cement. (High reactive base metal alloys are better
retained than less reactive gold alloys.)
e. Type of luting agent: The adhesive resin cements
are most retentive as compared to other cements.

Bevels
The weakest link in any cast restoration is the tooth–
cement–cast interphase. An accurate wax pattern and
reproduction in gold casting may not assure well-
adapted margins to the tooth surface. This peripheral
marginal anatomy of the preparation is called
‘circumferential tie’, created by placing bevels along
the cavosurface margins.
The enamel must be supported by sound dentin,
and enamel rods along the cavosurface margin should
be continuous with sound dentin.
Bevels reduce the error factors (space between cast
and tooth substances) at the margins compared to
their internal dimensions, depending on the bevel’s
angulation. Bevels indirectly improve retention form
for a cast restoration; bevels decrease the direct
frictional component between the tooth and the
casting.
The types of bevel are:
a. Partial bevel: It involves part of the enamel wall.
Such type of preparation is indicated in direct filling
gold restorations (Fig. 16.14A).
b. Short bevel: It involves the entire enamel wall. This
type of bevel is preferred in cast gold restorations
(Fig. 16.14B).
c. Inverted bevel: It is given on the labial shoulder of
metal ceramic crowns to effectively improve the
esthetics at the margins (Fig. 16.14C).
d. Reverse bevel: A reverse bevel is placed at the
dentinal portion of the cervical wall towards the
axiogingival line angle (Fig. 16.14D). The hydrostatic
pressure during cementing a cast restoration can
produce a rotational displacement of the casting
with flat gingival walls. This effect is resisted by
the reverse bevel resulting in even seating of the
cast restoration.

FLARES IN CAST RESTORATIONS
The requisite flares in cast restorations are:
a. Primary flare: As per the old concept of inlay cavity
preparation, the proximal walls were divided into
two halves. The axial half was placed at 90° to the
axial wall and proximal half had an angulation of
45° to the axial half. This proximal half that helped
to bring the proximal wall into self-cleansing areas
was known as the primary flare. It was believed
that the axial wall should be at 90° to the proximal
walls to provide the retention and resistance form
to the cavity, but later it was found that flaring of
the walls right from axiofacial or axiolingual line
angles till the cavosurface margin did not alter the
retention and resistance form. This implies that the
flaring of lingual wall and buccal wall of proximal
box from the axial wall (axiolingual and axiofacial
line angles, respectively) to the proximal
cavosurface margins is known as primary flare (Fig.
16.15A, B).
Functions: The function of the primary flare is to
keep the proximal buccal and lingual walls in the
self-cleansing areas.
b. Secondary flare: The secondary flare is a flat plane
superimposed peripherally to a primary flare. It is
as good as the occlusal bevel given to create a
circumferential tie in gold inlays to take the
advantage of malleability of gold alloys (Fig. 16.15C).
Functions: A secondary flare creates the obtuse
angulation at the proximal cavosurface margin;
created exclusively for gold inlay preparations
(malleability of gold facilitates adaptation of
restoration to proximal cavity walls).
The modified preparations, such as box only
preparation, slice preparation and modified flare
preparation, are no longer used.

Tucker’s Technique
Dr. Richard Tucker developed a conservative cast
gold inlay preparation technique. The cavity
preparation sequence is as follows:
After placement of rubber dam, the existing
restoration, if any, and carious tooth structure are
removed.
The occlusal box is prepared with straight fissure
bur and pulpal floor is placed to a depth of 1.5 to
2.0 mm. A taper of 3° to 5° is uniformly maintained.
The proximal box form is established and blended
with the occlusal preparation. The buccal and lingual
walls must be extended sufficiently (1.0 mm) beyond
the adjacent tooth. The proximal walls have slight
flare (45°) that eliminate unsupported enamel rods.

walls are finished. The bevel should be definitive and
smooth, but not too wide. Tucker’s gingival margin

Inlay vs Onlay
An inlay is primarily an intracoronal cast restoration
that is designed mainly to restore occlusal and
proximal surface(s) of posterior teeth without
involving the cusps. An onlay is a combination of
intracoronal and extracoronal cast restoration, when
one or more cusps are covered. A full veneer crown
is an extracoronal cast restoration, where all cusps
are covered.
Cusp capping or onlays are usually indicated
where one or more cusps, which are weakened by
caries or trauma, are to be covered occlusally. The
cast restorations are suitable especially when
functional cusps are to be capped. It is partly an
intracoronal and partly extracoronal type of
restoration. If the cavity involves more than half of
the cusp inclines, cuspal coverage is mandatory.

FABRICATION OF CAST RESTORATIONS
Fabrication of cast restoration involves various
procedures. These procedures are equally important
as that of cavity preparation for the success of the
treatment.
The procedures are as follows.
A. Impression Technique
In dentistry, impression is the negative replica of teeth
and surrounding tissues. From this negative form, a
positive reproduction or cast is made. The cast must
exactly duplicate the preparation in order to have the
precision fitting castings.
Several types of impression materials are available
for use in the fabrication of cast restorations.
The impression can be taken on prefabricated
tray or the tray can be fabricated outside the oral
cavity.
Selection of Impression Tray
For reversible hydrocolloid, stock metal tray is used.
For rubber base materials, stock or custom trays can
be used (Figs 16.19 and 16.20).
Custom trays are usually preferred because they
allow a more uniform, thin layer (2.0–3.0 mm) of the
impression material. A uniform thickness of

b. Hygroscopic Expansion (Low Heat) Technique
This technique involves the immersion of the metal
ring with investment into a warm water bath set at
37°C or 100°F.
The technique compensates shrinkage by three
mechanisms:
i. 37°C water bath expands the wax pattern.
ii. Water entering the investment provides greater
volume into which gypsum crystals can grow and
provide some hygroscopic expansion.
iii. Thermal expansion at 468°C (burnout temperature
for hygroscopic setting expansion).
The amount of hygroscopic expansion can be
controlled by:
• Water/powder ratio: The higher the water/powder
ratio, the lower the amount of expansion.
• Time of immersion in the water bath: The longer the
delay before the investment is immersed in the
water bath, the less is the hygroscopic expansion.
• Controlling the amount of water added during the
setting.
Hygroscopic expansion can be achieved by two
ways:
i. Water bath technique: A definite proportions of
water and investment are used. Investment must
be slow setting to allow sufficient time to expand.
The wax pattern is invested. Both ends of the ring
are sealed and it is placed in the water bath at 45°C
until the investment has completely set. The wax
is then burned out and the metal is casted.
ii. Controlled water technique: The amount of
hygroscopic expansion is directly related to the
amount of water available to the setting
investment; adding a specific amount of water to
the setting investment, a specific amount of
expansion results. The controlled water technique
is based on this principle.
For this technique, an investment, especially
compounded to provide high hygroscopic
expansion, is used.
The investment is mixed and poured into the
rubber ring with supporting metal ring in place.
Immediately after investing, the metal ring is
removed so as to avoid restriction of the expansion
of investment. The investment is flushed with top
of the rubber ring and the reservoir collar inserted.
The syringe is filled with water and the water is
carefully dispersed into the reservoir collar.
Generally, 1.1 mL water is added; however, less
water (0.6 mL) can be added, if it is warm.
The investment is allowed to set; after setting, the
rubber ring is removed and the investment mold
without the support of a metal ring is subjected to
burnout and the alloy is cast into the mold.
In the hygroscopic technique, the ring is heated
around 460°C (thermal expansion of the investment
is not desired).
Cleaning the Casting
After the casting has been completed, the ring is
removed and quenched in water.
Advantages of quenching are:
• The noble metal alloy is left in an annealed
condition for burnishing, polishing, etc.
• When water contacts the investment, it undergoes
vaporizing; subsequently, produce cracking of the
investment into small pieces. This simplifies
cleaning the casting.

FINISHING AND POLISHING
After casting, the gold alloys get discolored due to
contamination/presence of sulfide in the investment
material. Pickling procedure is carried out to remove
the contaminants. The solutions used for pickling is
50% hydrochloric acid. Solution of sulfuric acid is
considered advantageous. The best method of
pickling is to place the casting in a test tube and pour
acid over it. It is preferred to heat acid, but boiling
should be avoided. After pickling, the acid is poured
off and the casting is removed. The pickling solution
should be renewed frequently, because it is likely to
get contaminated. Casting should not be held with
steel instrument, as this may contaminate the pickling
solution and casting.
Another procedure to be avoided is heating the
casting and then dropping it in the pickling solution.
This may melt delicate margins of the casting or the
casting may be distorted. After pickling, the casting
is washed in running water.
The casting retrieved from the investment is rough.

used, viz. silicon carbide, emery, aluminium oxide,
garnet, sand, tripoli, rouge, etc. The outer surface of
the casting is smoothened with these abrasives using
progressively small particle sizes.
Finishing may leave microscopic scratches on the
casting. These scratches are eliminated by sequential
polishing, producing microcrystalline surface layer,
known as ‘Bielby layer’.

a. Seating
The discrepancies in the prepared tooth and the
castings coupled with cementing pressure may lead
to rebound of restoration. These discrepancies are to
be compensated for proper seating of the restoration.
The discrepancies can be minimized by coating the
die with die-spacer, and/or relieving the die or
etching the casting.
In seating the restoration, the features of luting
cement, which plays important role, are:
• Rheology of cement
• Working time
• Film thickness
• Geometry of gap
The failure usually occurs at cement–tooth interface
(Fig. 16.38). The flow of cement is the key factor for
successfully cementing the restorations. To achieve
sufficient cement flow, the seating of restorations
should be fast (within a few seconds) while the cement
is sufficiently fluid. The flow of zinc phosphate,
polycarboxylate and ethoxy-benzoic acid cements is
sufficient under moderate pressure. The resin cements
are less satisfactory in this respect. Zinc oxide eugenol
cement generates the lowest hydraulic pressures
during seating, followed by polycarboxylate cement.
Zinc phosphate cement exhibited the greatest
hydraulic pressure. The seating force should be
maintained at least for three minutes without any
interruption while the cement is setting (Fig. 16.39A,
B).
Working time should be adequate so that the
hydraulic action takes place before setting of cement.
Mixing on a cool glass slab solves the problem to some
extent. The excess cement is to be removed. To achieve
easy removal and minimizing the hydraulic pressure,
a sort of path is created, known as venting. It is
confirmed that venting is a satisfactory method of
achieving minimal film thickness under cast
restorations; it also minimizes the hydraulic pressure.
b. Retention
It is established that almost all luting cements are fair
in compressive strength and poor in tensile strength.
The luting agents do not help in retention directly;
however, better tensile strength of the cement may
aid in retention. The retention of the restoration
mainly depends upon the following features:
• Geometrical design of the tooth preparation, which
in turn influences the stress distribution.
• Bonding efficiency of the luting cement to both the
tooth and the restoration.
• Durability of the cement (resistance to mechanical
breakdown and dissolution).
It is recommended that the cement with a high
tensile strength be used to cement crowns and long
span bridges, since shear stresses in marginal area can
exceed the strength of low strength cement.

b. Porosity Caused by Trapped Gases
i. Pinhole porosity.

The metal absorbs gases when it is in molten state.

Larger spherical porosities are caused by gas

c. Subsurface Porosity
The exact reason for subsurface porosity has not been
established. It may be due to the simultaneous
nucleation of solid grains and gas bubbles at the first
moment that the metal freezes at the mold walls. This
can be diminished by controlling the rate at which
the molten metal enters the mold.

Porosity produced due to air entrapped on the inner
surface of the casting; occurs if for some reason gas is
not vented from the mold.

rapidly. The water/powder ratio of the investment
