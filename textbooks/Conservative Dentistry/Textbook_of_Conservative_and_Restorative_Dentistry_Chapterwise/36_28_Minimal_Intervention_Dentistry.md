In the recent past, scientific developments in
cariology, dental materials and diagnostic
armamentarium have revolutionized operator’s
approach in managing caries. The previous surgical
approach requiring removal of diseased portion of
tooth along with extension of cavities to the areas
prone to caries is no longer used. The ‘extension for
prevention’, which was commonly taught has been
changed to ‘prevention for extension’. The tooth
weakened by extensive surgical approach leads to
frequent retreatment and replacement, subsequently
resulting in more complex treatment modalities and
even extraction. The emerging technologies have
paved the way for preventive aspects vis-à-vis
remineralization of the existing lesions. Though this
concept might not be fully practical, however, the
early trials are promising. Alternatively, the treatment
of the existing lesion should include minimum
involvement of the healthy tooth structure.
The terms ‘minimal intervention dentistry’ and
‘minimal invasive dentistry’ are used interchangeably,
although they describe different clinical concepts.
Minimal intervention dentistry implies patient care
that deals with the cause of dental disease and not
just the symptom. Minimal intervention is based
entirely on prevention and control of oral disease;
whereas, minimal invasive dentistry embraces
conservative restorative procedures (Flowchart 28.1).
The excavation of caries is performed with the
objective of preserving not only sound tooth tissues
but also that tissue, which has potential to
remineralize. The use of adhesive biomaterial is
preferred. Minimal invasive dentistry is merely a
component of minimal intervention treatment (care)
plan. The terms, ‘ultraconservative’ and ‘microdentistry’
are synonymous with minimal invasive dentistry, that
implies use of magnification aids and other  devices,
such as, chemo-mechanical, air abrasive, sono-
abrasion and laser systems for management of caries.
Core Principles
The basis of minimal invasive dentistry is to deviate
the clinical focus from restoring the teeth damaged
by caries to the prevention of disease in its early
stages.
The core principles of minimal invasive dentistry
are:
i. Recognizing risk factors: To assess the potential
caries risk factors, may undertake some diagnostic
tests.
ii. Minimizing risk factors: The risk factors are
minimized by reducing cariogenic diet, altering
fluid balance and managing pH of the oral
environment.
iii. Regeneration/remineralization: The incipient lesions
are arrested by remineralizing agents, such as
fluorides, casein phosphopeptide-amorphous
calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP).

COMPONENTS OF MINIMAL INVASIVE APPROACH
The components of minimal invasive approach
include the following:
I. Early diagnosis of caries
II. Caries risk assessment (young and old)
III. Strategies to arrest existing carious lesions
IV. Remineralization of the existing lesions
V. Restoring cavitated lesions: Minimal invasive

VI. Caries inhibition by resin infiltration
VII. Repair rather than replacement of defective

I. EARLY DIAGNOSIS OF CARIES
The early diagnosis involves detection of carious
lesions at initial stages and also determine the caries
activity. Caries activity must be evaluated by
monitoring the lesion over time. Various caries
activity tests are available. Indices for progress of
caries activity are also used; however, improved
versions are required to analyze the progress of the
caries. (For details, refer author’s book ‘Dental caries’:
Vimal K Sikri.)
Various diagnostic aids are being utilized to detect
caries. Some are preferred for detecting occlusal caries,
while others detect proximal or smooth surface lesions
in a better way. The emerging technologies in
diagnostic armamentarium include electrical
conductance methods, quantitative laser fluorescence,
tuned-aperture computed tomography and optical
coherence tomography (For details refer Chapter 9).
Radiographs, especially bitewing, are also helpful in
analysing progress of caries; however, not useful in
detecting early caries.
There is still a need for research to increase the
accuracy of diagnostic methods. The on-the-spot
assessment of caries activity is required to effectively
perform minimal invasive procedures.
II. CARIES RISK ASSESSMENT (YOUNG AND ADULTS)
Risk assessment helps professionals supervise
individual’s oral health. It is a process that attempts
to identify those individuals who are at greater risk
of caries attack. These features are helpful in early
treatment of caries and also the potential of reversal
phenomenon.
The patients are categorized as ‘low risk’,
‘moderate risk’, and ‘high risk’, depending upon the
risk of development of caries.
Low risk
• Good oral hygiene
• No new caries in the last couple of years
• No susceptible pits and fissures
• Visiting dental clinic regularly
Moderate risk
• Oral hygiene fair
• One caries lesion in last year
• Susceptible pits and fissures
• Irregular visit to dental clinic
• Undergoing orthodontic treatment
High risk
• Poor oral hygiene
• Two or more than two carious lesions in last year
• Susceptible (deep) pits and fissures
• Inadequate salivary flow
• Rarely visit dental clinic
• Inappropriate and frequent bottle feeding
The procedure involves examining risk factors that
may negatively impact individuals oral health, and
protective factors that promote oral health. Using risk
assessment, the operator can make specific preventive
and treatment recommendations to improve overall
oral health. This also contributes to efficient
management of case by eliminating unnecessary
interventions.

Risk Factors in Caries Initiation
The risk factors for the initiation of caries can be
physical, biological, environmental, and behavioral.
Other factors include number of cariogenic bacteria,
compromised salivary flow, poor oral hygiene and
frequent consumption of cariogenic food.
The important factors are as follows:
a. Host (Tooth) Factors
The host factors may influence the initiation of caries
in many ways. First, some areas of the tooth, such as
pit and fissures, tooth irregularities, etc. are prone to
biofilm accumulation. Second, quality and quantity
of saliva, buffering capacity, flow, antimicrobial
activity and also clearance from the oral cavity.
b. Biofilm (Bacterial Stagnation)
Many biofilm micro-organisms have acid producing
and acid tolerating properties, including species from
the genera Veillonella, Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium,
streptococci, Actinomyces spp., etc.
Lower pH in biofilm may not result in progressive
net mineral loss. The prolonged acidification of
biofilm may result in mineral loss and subsequent
caries. The biofilm environment, which favors more
acid producing micro-organisms, facilitates initiation
of caries.
c. Dietary Factors
The dietary sugars are considered as the main
causative agents in the initiation of caries. Diet high
in fermentable carbohydrates favor a low biofilm pH,
while diet high in proteins and fats favor a neutral
biofilm pH. High-protein diets increase the urea
concentration of saliva, which can be converted by
ureolytic bacteria to ammonia; this raises the biofilm
pH and is associated with decreased caries risk.
Other dietary factors, such as type of carbohydrate,
retentiveness and presence of protective factors in
food (calcium, phosphate and fluoride), are important.
Complex carbohydrates (starches) are considered less
cariogenic than the simple sugars (sucrose, glucose,
and fructose) with sucrose being the most cariogenic,
because of its ability to produce extracellular glucans.
Amino acids and proteins have the potential of being
‘anti-caries’, and have significant caries-inhibitory and
cariostatic effects.
i. Effect of minerals: Trace elements (pre-
eruptively/post-eruptively), when incorporated
in enamel, can affect remineralization process and
influence the metabolism of cariogenic micro-
organism. The elements, which promote caries are
Se, Mg and Cd; while, Mo, V, and Sr, are mildly
cariostatic.
ii. Effect of vitamins: Vitamins A, C, and D are
essential for proper calcification of tooth structure.
The vitamin–caries relationship is mediated
primarily through acidogenic micro-organisms.
Vitamin B is necessary growth factors for most of
the oral acidogenic flora. Thiamine and nicotinic
acid may promote acid production by microbial
action.
d. Micro-organisms
High proportion of mutans streptococci is essential
for initiation of caries. Streptococcus mutans produces
high levels of acid from sugars, is acid tolerant and
firmly adheres to tooth surface. Lactobacilli and other
gram-positive bacteria, commonly isolated from the
oral cavity, are high acidogenic, but they have low
affinity to the tooth surface. (Lactobacilli and gram-
positive bacteria are more related to advancing caries rather
than initiating caries.) The acid produced during the
metabolic activities cause demineralization, which
increases porosity in the enamel and decreases
translucency of the surface color.
Caries risk factors: General features
Physical
• Variations in tooth enamel, deep pits and fissures,
anatomically susceptible areas
• Gastric reflux
• High mutans streptococci count
• Previous caries experience
• History of baby bottle tooth decay
Behavioral
• Feeding bottle used at night for sleep; or ‘at will’ while

• Taking frequent snacks
• Inadequate oral hygiene
• Eating disorders, including self-induced vomiting

Socio-environmental
• Inadequate fluoride
• Poor family oral health
• High parental levels of mutans streptococci
Disease or treatment related
• Cariogenic carbohydrate diet
• Disturbed salivary flow due to medication/ irradiation
• Orthodontic appliances

IV. REMINERALIZATION OF THE EXISTING LESIONS
The possibility of arresting and even reversing the
mineral loss associated with initiation of caries has been
established. Depending upon the oral environment,
the tooth alternatively loses and gains calcium and
phosphate ions. This cyclic loss and gain forms the basis
of demineralization and remineralization (Flowchart
28.2). The pH of the environment surrounding the teeth
is an important factor for this process.
Taking advantage of the tooth’s ability to
remineralize, the balance can be tipped in favor of
remineralization by altering the oral environment.
The phenomenon of alteration includes:
• Decreasing the frequency of intake of refined
carbohydrates
• Following optimum plaque control measures
• Maintaining salivary flow
• Motivating and educating the patient
Ideal Requirements of a Remineralizing Agent
• Diffuse into subsurface, deliver appropriate
amount of calcium and phosphorus (should not
diffuse excess of calcium)
• Should not encourage calculus formation
• Should be effective in xerostomic patients
• Reinforce the remineralizing component of saliva
• Should be effective in acidic pH
• Should follow simple/easy technique
A. Remineralization of Enamel Lesions
The materials used for enamel remineralization are
as follows.
a. Fluorides
The fluorides, both as systemic and topical application,
increase caries resistance. The action of fluorides is
summarized as: Increased enamel resistance,
remineralization of incipient caries, controlling micro-
organisms and increase in rate of maturation.
Fluorides present in oral environment alter the
continuously occurring dissolution at the tooth–oral
fluids interface.
Topical fluorides encourage:
• Remineralization at the crystal suface by making
them acid resistant.
• Inhibition of bacterial enzymes.
Fluorides facilitate formation of fluorapatite, which
is deposited at the surface layer, while hydroxyapatite
is dissolved from the subsurface. Fluorides have their
limitation in providing calcium and phosphate ions
from oral biofilm to the demineralized enamel. Also
the effect of fluorides is limited in pit and fissure

b. Casein Derivatives: Casein Phosphopeptide-
Amorphous Calcium Phosphate (Recaldent)
Casein is the predominant phosphoprotein in bovine
milk and accounts for almost 80% of its total protein.
The calcium phosphate is present as an alkaline
amorphous phase along with calcium phosphate,
referred to as casein phosphopeptide-amorphous
calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP). They have the ability
to stabilize high concentrations of calcium and
phosphate in metastable solutions. CPP-ACP is a
useful cariostatic agent for the control of dental caries
as it contains calcium and phosphate in a bioavailable
form. CPP-ACP can be used as an:
• Adjunct preventive therapy to reduce caries in high
risk patients
• Reduce dental erosion in patients with gastric reflux
• Remineralize enamel involving initial white spot
lesions
• Minimize orthodontic decalcification
• Potent desensitizing agent
CPP-ACP binds readily to the surface of tooth; the
bound CPP-ACP buffers the free calcium and
phosphate ions and increases level of calcium
phosphate in plaque. This saturation inhibits enamel
demineralization and enhances remineralization.
CPP-ACP is pH responsive. Increase in pH increases
the level of bound ACP; stabilizes free calcium and
phosphate in plaque; subsequently, inhibiting acidic
activities in plaque. CPP-ACP influences the behavior
of plaque through:
• Binding to adhesin molecule of S. mutans and
impairing their incorporation into plaque.
• Elevates plaque-calcium ion level; inhibiting acidic
activities of plaque (plaque fermentation).
• Suppress overgrowth of aciduric species, where
fermentable carbohydrates are in excess (provide
protein and phosphate buffering of plaque pH).
Casein derivatives added in toothpastes, dentifrices
and gum/lozenges have shown promising results,
inhibiting caries and enhancing remineralization. CPP-
ACP has also been used with fluoride for better results
(CPP-ACFP: amorphous calcium fluoride phosphate).
c. Tricalcium Phosphate (TCP)
Tricalcium phosphate, precisely -tricalcium
phosphate (TCP), substantially enhances levels of
calcium in plaque and saliva. The commercial
preparations are Cerasorb, Bio-resorb, etc. -TCP is
less soluble than -TCP; less likely to provide bio-
available calcium. The common problem using TCP
is the formation of calcium–phosphate complexes
(with fluorides, formation of calcium fluoride), which
would inhibit remineralization by lowering the levels
of bioavailable calcium and fluoride. TCP can be
combined with titanium dioxide (or any metal oxide)
to limit the interaction between calcium and
phosphate, making the material more stable in oral
conditions. When TCP moistened in saliva contacts
the tooth surface, the protective barrier breaks down,
facilitating availability of calcium, phosphate and
fluoride to the tooth; subsequently, enhancing
remineralization.
Dicalcium phosphate dihydrate (DCPD) slurries
are effective in preventing plaque pH drop and
allowing higher degree of saturation for an extended
period of time.
d. NovaMin (Bioactive Glass)
NovaMin is a bioactive glass ceramic (calcium sodium
phosphosilicate) material, when exposed to aqueous
medium, provides calcium and phosphate ions that
form hydroxy-carbonate apatite (HCA) with time.
NovaMin is able to fill the small surface defects in
enamel and minimize development of erosion from
acidic foods and beverages; also allows remineraliza-
tion of these defects. NovaMin has been incorporated
into dentifrices/gels (oravive revitalizing paste). A
few studies have observed low availability of calcium
and phosphate from NovaMin.
e. Enamelon
Enamelon consists of unstabilized calcium and
phosphate salts with sodium fluoride. Enamelon
toothpastes have shown promising results in
reduction of white spot lesions and remineralization
of tooth enamel.
A technical problem with Enamelon is that calcium
and phosphate are not stabilized, allowing these two
ions to combine into insoluble precipitates before
coming in contact with oral fluids and enamel.
f. Xylitol (Sugar Alcohol)
Xylitol (sugar alcohol), used as a substitute for sugar,
occurs naturally in plants.
Xylitol has the ability to:
• Make plaque less adhesive
• Reduce S. mutans levels
• Reduce plaque formation
• Neutralize plaque acids by decreasing the
production of lactic acid

B. Remineralization of Dentinal Lesions
As the carious lesion reaches the dentin matrix, it
progresses rapidly than enamel lesion. The dentinal
lesions show different zones of mineral contents and
optical reflections. These lesions are remineralized by
spontaneous incorporation of calcium, phosphate and
fluoride ions from oral fluids or by external sources.
Remineralization of dentin implies precipitation of
mineral between collagen fibrils. This precipitation of
mineral into demineralized dentin matrix may provide
an increased minimal content, but do not chemically
interact with organic compounds of the matrix.
The overall mechanical properties do not change.
The preservation and stability of dentin collagen is
essential during remineralization process, because it
acts as scaffold for mineral deposition.
Plant extracts, polyphenols, green seed extract,
contain proanthocyanidin (potent antioxidant and
anti-inflammatory), etc. are used as dentinal
remineralizing agents. These substances accelerate the
conversion of soluble collagen to insoluble collagen
during collagen synthesis. Polyanthrocyanidin also
inhibits the enzymatic activity of glycol transferase;
subsequently, inhibits caries progression.
V. RESTORING CAVITATED LESIONS: MINIMAL
INVASIVE APPROACH
It is established that by focusing on infection control
measures coupled with application of remineralizing
agents, the restoration placement can be reduced to
approximately 50%.
As the clinician detect a white spot lesion, efforts
should be to educate and motivate the patient
regarding its contributing factors and also the protocol
for control. Uncontrolled demineralization leads to
cavitation, which further encourages growth of oral
microflora (mainly facultative anaerobes). Restoration
should be avoided, especially in following situations,
which may show evidence of dental caries:
• White spot lesions or hidden lesions, which are
non-cavitated (especially when confined to enamel).
• Secondary caries along the restoration-tooth
interface; preferably be managed using topical
remineralizing agent.
• Root surface lesion, even if cavitated, should be
managed using topical remineralizing agents
(Inaccessible lesions do not fall under this category).
A classification system, termed ICDAS II
(International Caries Detection and Assessment
System) can be applied to individual tooth surfaces
(Table 28.1).
ICDAS II system distinguishes between sound
teeth surface, first visual change in enamel (white
spot), distinct change in enamel (white lesion), micro-
cavitation and cavitation of different depths. (Details
of ICDAS, explained in author’s book: Dental Caries—
Vimal K Sikri.)
Cavitation makes plaque control difficult especially
at the affected site; therefore, surgical approach is
more reliable. The newer techniques of tooth
preparation allow much more conservative
preparation designs than those used earlier.
The classification conceived by Mount and Hume
(Chapter 2) based on site and size of the lesion is
considered the basis for minimal cavity preparation,
though the advanced and extensive lesions do not fall
under such regimes. Secondly, the classification is
subjective; the size of the lesion is not well defined.
The tooth can be restored by one of the following
modalities utilizing minimal invasive approach.

i. Tunnel Preparation
The tunnel preparation is performed by accessing the
proximal carious lesion from the occlusal surface,
while preserving the marginal ridge.
When caries is present only on proximal side,
usually the access is gained through occlusal side.
Conventionally, after making occlusal dovetail and
breaking the marginal ridge, proximal box is prepared.
Such restorations lead to more of tooth loss and
jeopardize the integrity of marginal ridges. In tunnel
preparations, the access is gained through occlusal
side without involving the marginal ridge.
Tunnel restorations were designed with the idea
of preserving the marginal ridge during posterior
proximal restorations. For convenience, the tunnel
restorations are divided into three types, viz. internal,
partial, and total (Fig. 28.4). The ‘internal tunnel’
implies touching the lesion; the main objective is to
help remineralization. This is also known as
‘terminated tunnel’ preparation. In partial tunnel
preparation, the enamel is smoothened around the
periphery and the remaining lesion can remineralize.
In ‘total tunnel’ preparation, all demineralized enamel
is removed.
The adjacent tooth is protected with matrix bands
before cavity preparation. The access is gained
through the mesial/distal pit depending upon the
side involved. A small round bur is pierced through
the pit, directing the bur at 45° towards the proximal
contact area (Fig. 28.5A, B). Once the lesion is
approached, the access can be slightly widened using
tapering fissure burs. The carious lesion is thoroughly
excavated by moving the bur in occlusal and gingival
directions. Till the completion of the cavity prepara-
tion and the restoration, the band is kept stable so as
not to injure the adjacent tooth and the underlying
gingiva. Various studies conducted on tunnel
restorations could not show promising results. The
strength of the marginal ridge is not maintained. The

g. Laser Cavity Preparation
Lasers are being widely used for cavity preparation.
The Er:YAG and Nd:YAG lasers have been used to cut
dental hard tissues; whereas, CO2 lasers facilitate
sealing of fissures. Laser treatment reduces the number
of carious bacteria and also volatilizes the water in the
caries. With the use of lasers, the effect of vibrations,
pressure and unfavorable temperature changes
associated with the use of rotary cutting instruments
is eliminated. The cavity cutting by lasers is clean with
minimum debris and smear layer. The morphology of
enamel prisms and dentinal tubule is preserved. Lasers
facilitate selection removal of caries. Preparations are
similar to those made with air abrasion. Adhesive
materials are preferred for restoration.
The erbium yttrium-aluminum-garnet (Er:YAG—
wavelength 2.78 mm) and the erbium-chromium—
yttrium-scandium-gallium-garnet (Er,Cr:YSGG-
wavelength 2.94 mm) lasers are currently used in cavity
preparation and caries excavation. The mechanism by
which enamel and dentin are removed consists of
explosive subsurface expansion of water interstitially
trapped in the dental hard tissues. During irradiation,
the water molecules absorb the incident radiation,
causing sudden heating and water evaporation. As a
result, a high stream pressure is formed, inducing a
violent expansion and ejection of dental hard tissues.
Er, Cr:YSGG laser system delivers photons through air-
water spray straight onto the target tissue. The
microexplosive forces turning into water droplets,
contribute to the hard-tissue removal. Laser ablation
provides more comfort to the patient due to the absence
of vibration and a lower pain sensation. It has definite
role in the prevention of recurrent caries.
Laser cavity preparation: Major drawbacks
• Time consuming; the time required for a complete
excavation is twice that with rotary instruments.
• Can induce irreversible chemical and structural
alteration of dentin and even damage the pulp.
• Produces an undefined and irregular excavation
pattern in dentin.
• Irregular dentin surface left after laser ablation hinders
tactile sensation.
• Laser may change the organic matrix component of
dentin, thereby impairing the adhesive treatment.

Restorative Materials
Adhesive dental materials make it possible to conserve
tooth structure, because they do not require the
inclusion of mechanical retention features. Examples
of such materials are glass-ionomer cements, resin-
based glass-ionomer cement, composite/dentin
bonding agents, and the bonded amalgam.
i. Glass-ionomer Cements
The advantages of glass-ionomer cement (GIC)
include, adhesion to tooth structure and release of
fluorides. They perform well in low stress areas. Set
GIC is ‘rechargeable’; can take up fluoride from the
environment, which is provided by exposure to
fluoride varnishes and fluoride containing toothpastes.
The disadvantages include, technique sensitivity
and the opaque nature. The handling properties and
opaqueness of the material can be overcome by
adding resin to the material resulting in resin-
modified GICs, which are easier to place, are light
cured, and have improved esthetics.
ii. Composite Resins
The cavity preparation for composite mainly depends
upon the surface area of the tooth, which can be
utilized for bonding. Cavity preparations designed
to conserve maximum enamel can eliminate the need
for macromechanical retention. Bonding, both to
dentin and enamel is required for composite
restorations. Various types of composites are available
for use in anterior and posterior teeth. Flowable
composites with lower filler loading have been
developed, which are preferred in inaccessible areas.
These materials have low modulus of elasticity and
higher polymerization shrinkage, therefore, not used
in routine.
iii. Bonded Silver Amalgam
The bonded silver amalgam was introduced with an
idea to improve the bonding of amalgam to the
cavity walls. A chemically active, resin-based luting
material is placed along the cavity wall and at its
base before condensing the silver. Though the
adhesion is definitely improved, the other physical
properties might be compromised, since proper
depth required for silver is not provided. The
thickness of the oxygen inhibited layer in dentin
bonding agents may create difficulty in bonded
amalgam.

• Marginal leakage is checked.
• Conservative approach is maintained.
• Sealed and bonded restorations prevent further
progress of caries.
Disadvantages
• Increased cost
• Proper strength may not be achieved.

caries lesions; even though the resins available at that
time might not penetrate effectively deep due to their
high viscosity (Higher the viscosity lesser is the
penetration coefficient).
Researchers continued to develop resins of low
viscosity capable of penetrating deep into the pores
created by demineralization. The resins exhibiting
rapid penetration abilities were termed as ‘Infiltrants’.
The infiltration depth of these resins was evaluated
clinically. The infiltration of ‘low viscosity’ resins into
natural tooth surface was difficult; might be due to
presence of pseudo-intact layer covering the porous
structure hampering the infiltration. The surface layer
is formed by precipitation of minerals on the outer
surface and has a much lower pore volume compared
to the lesion body, thus inhibiting penetration of the
resin.
Conventional phosphoric acid etching was not
effective as the penetration of resins remained
superficial (phosphoric acid affected only 25 m of
surface). 15% hydrochloric acid for two minutes was
tried as an alternative etching protocol. It removed
58 m of demineralized/normal enamel surface.
Etching with 15% HCl gel for 90–120 seconds was
found to be more effective than 37% phosphoric acid
gel (Hydrochloric acid in concentration of 18% has
been used in dentistry for microabrasion). It is
observed that the erosion depth with hydrochloric
acid is more than twice as compared to phosphoric
acid when etched for two minutes. HCl penetrates
into deepest part of the lesion, preventing further
attacks. The addition of ethanol after etchant
application significantly increases the penetration of
infiltrant due to decrease of viscosity and contact
angle. Better penetration was observed using
combination of TEGDMA and HEMA as infiltrant
along with using 20% ethanol.

Advantages
• Improved esthetics
• Occlusion of microporosities (up to certain depth)
• Obturation of deeply porous demineralized areas
• Arrest/retardation of lesion progression
• Minimizes risk of secondary caries
• Mechanical stabilization of demineralized enamel
• Preservation of tooth tissue
• No risk of postoperative sensitivity
• Pulpal reactions are negligible.
• Restorative intervention can be delayed.
• Time and cost-effective
• Reduced risk of gingivitis and periodontitis
