Sterilization and Infection Control
The importance of aseptic techniques using sterilized
instruments, disinfectant solutions, etc. coupled with
other barriers, has long been emphasized. Unless the
instruments and equipment are properly sterilized, a
variety of saliva and blood-borne pathogens can easily
travel from patient-to-patient. Thus it becomes
mandatory for dental professionals to employ
effective methods of sterilization and infection control
to deliver quality healthcare.
TERMINOLOGY
Sterilization: Sterilization is defined as “the process
by which an article, surface or medium is freed of all living
micro-organisms either in vegetative or spore state”.
Disinfection: Disinfection is the removal or destruction
of any micro-organism capable of causing infection.
Three levels of disinfections are differentiated
depending on the type and form of micro-organisms
destroyed:
i. High level disinfection: A process that can kill some,
but not necessarily all bacterial spores. It is
‘tuberculocidal’; and if the disinfectant is capable
of destroying bacterial spores, it is labelled as
‘sporicidal’.
ii. Intermediate level disinfection: A process that is
capable of killing mycobacterium tuberculosis,
HBV and HIV. It may not be capable of killing
bacterial spores.
iii. Low level disinfection: A process that kills most
bacteria, some fungi and some viruses. It does not
kill Mycobacterium tuberculosis or bacterial spores.
Antisepsis: Antisepsis is a measure used to prevent
infection by inhibiting growth of micro-organisms in
wounds or tissues. An antiseptic is an agent that inhibits
the growth of micro-organisms on living surfaces.
CATEGORIZATION OF INSTRUMENTS
The instruments have been categorized, depending
on their contact with body tissues, to determine the
preferred mode of sterilization/disinfection.

The categories are as follows:
1. Critical items: Instruments, which touch sterile
areas of the body or enter the vascular system or
penetrate the oral mucosa; e.g. curettes, burs, files
and scalpels. Dental handpieces are kept in this
category. Instruments in this category must be
sterilized and stored in appropriate packages.
Single use items must be properly discarded.
2. Semi-critical items: Instruments, which touch
mucous membranes but do not penetrate body
tissues; e.g. amalgam condensers, saliva ejectors,
etc. These items should be sterilized; however, if
this is not feasible, high-level disinfection is
preferred.
3. Non-critical items: The items that do not come in
contact with the oral mucosa but are touched by
saliva or blood contaminated hands; e.g. light
switches, countertops and drawer pulls of cabinets.
These items should be properly disinfected.

PRESTERILIZATION
The instruments and equipment prior to sterilization
includes pre-soaking, cleaning, rinsing, drying and
packaging.
a. Pre-soaking, Cleaning, Rinsing and Drying
Pre-soaking of contaminated instruments keeps them
wet until adequate cleaning is carried out. This
prevents drying of blood, saliva and debris on
instruments and facilitates cleaning.
The cleaning process can be either mechanical or
manual.
1. Manual Cleaning
• Use stiff nylon cleaning brushes
• Use only neutral pH detergents
• Brush delicate instruments carefully and if possible,
separate them from general instruments.

elevated gradually to achieve:
• Initial microbial activation phase (temperature
45°– 52°C); endospores germinate and enter

• Microbial inactivation phase (temperature 85°–

inactivated or killed. Spores already entered
into germinative phase are also killed here
(indirectly sporicidal).
Requirements of detergent used in washer
disinfector:
– Anti-lipid action at high temperature
– Anti-protein action at low temperature
– Enzymatic action
• Likewise temperature, contact time is also a
crucial factor. Lower disinfection temperature
requires longer contact times (disinfection
phase).
iii. Enzyme cleaners: Enzymes are powerful tools for
cleaning reusable instruments. A properly selected
enzyme cleaner can facilitate pre-cleaning work
and improve effectiveness. The key to
effectiveness is the selection of the proper enzyme.
Enzymes accelerate the chemical reactions of the
biological processes and are specific in their
catalytic behaviour, such that a protease will work
only on proteins. Some enzymes are specific and
work on specific proteins while others will work
on a broad spectrum of proteins, e.g. Klenzymer
cleaners.

B. Moist Heat
Moist heat (steam, boiling water) kills by denaturation
and coagulation of protein. The advantage of steam
is that the latent heat condenses on a cold surface,
raising the temperature of that surface. In a complete
moisture-free atmosphere, bacteria are resistant to
heat (because oxidation of cell contents requires
higher temperatures than that needed for coagulation
of proteins).
The procedures employed are:
a. Temperature below 100°C: It is used for
pasteurization of milk. Either 63°C for 30 minutes
(holder method) or 72°C for 15–20 seconds (flash
process) followed by cooling rapidly to 13°C or
lower. All non-sporing pathogens are destroyed.
Vaccines or non-sporing bacteria are inactivated at
60°C for one hour in vaccine baths. Staphylococcus
aureus and Streptococcus faecalis require 60 minutes
at 60°C; 80°C for 5–10 minutes destroy the vegetative
forms of all bacteria and yeasts. Poliomyelitis virus
requires 60°C for 30 minutes and hepatitis virus
requires 60°C for 10 hours.

D. Cold
The cold method is used to retard microbial growths
in foods and to preserve biological materials. It acts
by:
• Increasing viscosity of the proteins
• Impeding diffusion of toxic products from the cell
• Increasing enzymatic activity
• Formation of ice crystals within and outside the
bacterial cell, which mechanically ruptures the cell.
Temperatures around 0°C have more inhibitory
effect than sub-zero temperature.
a. Desiccation: This method is unreliable and is only
of theoretical interest.
b. Sunlight: The microbicidal activity of sunlight is
due to the presence of ultraviolet rays. Sunlight is
not sporicidal; hence, it does not sterilize.
II. Chemical Method
Various chemical agents are used to disinfect, mainly
the operating surfaces. A few agents can achieve
sterilization also.
The desirable properties of disinfectant are:
• Non-corrosive
• Readily available
• Pleasant flavour
• Non-irritating
• Effective on most micro-organisms
• Economical.
The chemical agents used for disinfection act in
various ways:
• Protein coagulation
• Disruption of cell membrane
• Removal of free sulfhydryl groups, which are
essential for functioning of the enzymes
• Substrate competition
The levels of activity of various chemicals for
disinfection are tabulated in Table 9.4.
Office disinfection involves using a liquid chemical
at room temperature on submerged instruments or
operatory surfaces. If the chemical is not sporicidal
(iodophor, phenol, alcohol/phenolics, etc.), it is called
disinfectant.
If the chemical is sporicidal, it is called a sterilant
(2.0% or 3.0% concentration of glutaraldehyde) and
may be expected to achieve sterilization; however,
only after 10 hours contact time.
Various factors, which affect germicidal activity of
chemical agents are:
• Effective concentration of agent
• Time of exposure, kind and number of organisms,
type of material to be disinfected, presence of
organic matter.
• Increased temperature results in increased lethal
activity.
• Some chemical agents have more activity in acidic
pH (anionic detergents) and some in basic pH
(cationic detergents).
• Some agents may combine with organic matter and
become ineffective.

F. Surface Active Agents
Surface active agents are used as wetting agents,
detergents and emulsifiers. They are classified into
anionic, cationic, non-ionic and amphoteric groups.
The cationic surface active agents are important
antibacterial agents. They act on phosphate groups
of cell membrane, causing the membrane to lose its
semipermeability; subsequently, cell proteins are
denatured.
Cationic agents in the form of quaternary
ammonium compounds are bactericidal, active
against gram-positive organisms and to a lesser

Longer period of submersion has been more effective

• Ability to alkylate the carboxyl, hydroxyl and

CONTROL OF STERILIZATION
The operator must check the appropriateness of
sterilization, i.e. whether the required temperature is
achieved or the object is free of micro-organisms.
Various tests and techniques have been designed
to ensure authenticity of the sterilization process. The
tests employed are:
i. Autoclave tape: The tape is impregnated with a
dye; the color white is changed to brown/black
depending upon the temperature achieved. This
test is only an indication of the temperature and
cannot be taken as surety of sterilization. Similar
tapes are also designed, which indicate the
penetration of steam under pressure.
ii. Thermocouple: The thermocouple probes are
designed specifically to monitor the temperature
and the time during sterilization. The probes can
be inserted into the article to be sterilized and the
meter indicates the temperature and the time.
iii. Microbial tests: In this test, a particular species of
bacteria (usually Bacillus stearothermophilus for
steam and chemical vapour, Bacillus subtilis for dry
heat and ethylene oxide) is used as a test organism.
Paper strips impregnated with around one million
bacterial spores are dried and put along the object
to be sterilized. After sterilization process is over,
the strips are incubated for 5–7 days. Though this
method is perfect to test sterility; however,
practically not suitable because of the time lapse
for knowing the exact status of the sterilization.
iv. Chemical indicators: Certain chemical agents, at
certain temperature and particular time, lead to
color change, which shows the sterilization
achieved. The chemical shows red for danger;
orange for caution and green for complete
sterilization. Different types can be used for steam
sterilizers and for autoclaving.
STERILIZATION OF HANDPIECES
The dental handpiece and turbine due to constant
contact with oral fluids are contaminated on both
external and internal surfaces. Micro-organisms are
capable of forming biofilms on the internal surface of
handpieces and waterlines. Contamination of the
internal parts of the handpiece may originate from
saliva aspirated during treatment or from the air and
water supply of dental units, where micro-organisms
may build up in biofilms.
A range of microbiological flora has been identified
in dental unit waterline samples.
Rinsing out waterlines of the unit with Tween 80

For handpieces with a metal-bearing turbine, the

• Leave the handpiece attached to the hose after

• Wipe away visible debris from the handpiece.
• Flush water and air lines with the air/water system.

Chemical vapors work well with ceramic-bearing

of other hand pieces.

facilitate ethylene oxide gas to penetrate the
handpieces. However, oil left in the handpieces can
impair sterilization.

ENVIRONMENT OF DENTAL OPERATORY
The dental operatory may get exposed to various
types of microbial contaminations. The features,
which contaminate the environment are:
a. Air-borne contamination: Air-borne contaminants,
viz. aerosols, spatter, etc. are commonly encountered
in dental operatory.
b. Digital contamination: The methods to minimize
direct contact with various surfaces are required
to avoid digital contamination (walls, partition,
cabinets, etc. are all contaminated).
c. Cross-infection: In the dental settings, evidence of
oral or systemic cross-infections is difficult to
obtain, because such patients may have contacted
infections elsewhere, before or after having a dental
treatment. Infected patients usually are not aware
of the source of their infection. A thorough history
and previous medical records are helpful.
d. Patient vulnerability: Infection risks for dental
patients have not been investigated as properly as
for hospital patients; appear to be less vulnerable.
If infection control practices are used, transmission
of infection from dental patients is rare.
e. Personnel vulnerability: When dental personnel
experience exposure to saliva, blood and possible
injury from sharp instruments, they are  vulnerable
to infections. Use of proper protective barriers can
minimize the contaminants.
The operating area/chamber is to be sterilized for
better clinical outcome. The complete eradication of
bacteria may not be possible in operating room. The
source of bacteria can be from air, patients attending
the area, articles in the room, working equipment, etc.
The methods used to keep the operating area
bacteriologically safe are:
i. Air flow is filtered; circulating purified air and
continuously removing the contaminated air. The
patients are allowed in the area ‘off shoes’ and
preferably in sterilized gowns.
ii. Standard cleaning and disinfection procedures
should be followed, which minimizes the bacterial
growth.
iii. Fumigation of the area with formalin fumes. Since
formalin is pungent, the procedure is carried out
overnight in a closed environment. The room is
kept closed for 12–14 hours and is ready for use
once all the fumes are out. Formalin is usually
neutralized with ammonia (2 hours contact) prior
to use of the room. Formalin use (fumigation) is
obsolete in many countries because of its
potentially carcinogenic nature. Frequent use of
inhalation is hazardous. Newer agents are being
used to sterilize the room.
The ethylene oxide exposure for at least 12 hours
is required for an effective sterilization of handpieces.
A device, which combines flushing, lubrication and
autoclaving has been marketed as ‘Sterimax’.
STERILIZATION OF BURS
Dental burs are heavily contaminated after use.
Presently, single-use burs are recommended to
overcome cross-contamination.
Several options exist in handling contaminated
dental burs prior to sterilization.
• Bur is covered with wet gauze using a disinfectant.
• Place in an ultrasonic cleaner or washer disinfector
using separate bur holder.
• Hand scrub with a mild disinfectant.
For autoclave sterilization, burs can be protected
from rusting by keeping them submerged 2.0%
sodium nitrite solution.
• Add 20 mg of sodium nitrite crystals in 1 L of
distilled water to prepare 2.0% sodium nitrite (not
nitrate) solution.
• After ultrasonic cleaning, rinse the burs and place
them in a small glass or metal beaker with a
perforated lid.
• Fill the beaker with fresh nitrite solution ensuring
complete submersion of burs.
• Autoclave with a normal sterilization cycle.
• Fluid from the container is discarded through the
perforated lid and burs are transferred to a
sterilized bur holder or tray using a sterile
forceps.
• Before use, if needed, rinse off burs with sterile
water to wipe away any residue left on burs.

The infection control involves protection of patients
and members of dental team from different kinds of
infectious diseases. The procedures for infection
control are designed to kill/remove microbes and to
protect against contamination. One must follow
‘infection control assurance’, i.e. taking all steps
necessary to ensure the desired results.
The goals for infection control program are:
• Decrease the number of pathogenic microbes to the
level where normal body immune mechanisms can
prevent infection.
• Eliminate cross-contamination.
• Treat all patients and instruments as though they
could transmit an infectious disease.
• Protect patients and personnel from infection.

The identification of patients with transmissible

The medical history serves the following purposes:
• Detect any recognized illness that requires medical
diagnosis and treatment.
• Identify any infection or risk that may be important

• Manage the infected patients.
• Follow adequate infection control procedures,

d. Protective Clothing
The protective clothing includes reusable/disposable
gowns, laboratory coats with long sleeves, high neck
and long knee length. Head covers are also
recommended during procedures that result in
splashing blood or other body fluids as hair can trap
heavy contamination and if not washed thoroughly,
can be rubbed back from a pillow onto the face at
night.
The gowns should be changed at least daily and
should be changed immediately, if soaked or
spattered with blood or other contaminants. The
reusable garments should be placed in bags marked
with a ‘biohazard’ symbol. Laundering can be
effectively accomplished with a high temperature
(60°C to 70°C) wash cycle with normal bleach
followed by machine drying (100°C or more). Cool
water containing 50 to 150 ppm of chlorine can also
be used. This method has been found to be effective
in killing the AIDS virus.
The slippers in operatory should also be cleaned/
disinfected, at least once a day.
e. Eyewear
Protective eyewear is highly beneficial for dental
personnel and for the patient. Herpes virus infection
of eye and infection from hepatitis B are possible
consequences of viral contact with eye.
Put an eyewear with clean hands before gloving
and remove it with clean hands after gloves are
removed. When eyewears are removed, they should
be cleaned and disinfected. After removal, eyewear
should be placed on a paper towel and sprayed with
a water-based disinfectant. Wash eyewear well,
reapply disinfectant for 10 minutes, or preferably
allow it to soak in a 1:50 to 1:100 solution of 5.0%
hypochlorite or other disinfectant solution that does
not affect eyewear. Rinse well and dry.
Plastic lenses, if used, should be immersed in 20%
alkaline glutaraldehyde solution and then thoroughly
rinsed.
C. Surgical Asepsis
a. Surgical Staff Preparation
The staff in a surgery (operatory) should be prepared
as follows:
• Street clothes are replaced with a scrub consisting
of clean linen trousers and short-sleeved blouse.
Conducive footwear should be used.
• The operators and paramedical staff moving in an
operating room should cover their hair.
• Face mask must be worn. Sneezing and coughing
are not permitted in the operation room.
• The nails should be checked for cleanliness. All
gross sub-nail contaminations should be removed
with a nail cleaner.
• The arms are then wetted to several inches above
the elbows. The hands are kept elevated above
elbow height so that the direction of water flow
will be towards the elbows.
• The surgeon is helped into the sterile gown by a
properly gowned and gloved assistant. A
circulating assistant secures the gown ties at
surgeon’s back. The surgeon’s back as well as area
below waist are considerably unsterile.
b. Preparation of the Surgical Site
Preparation of surgical site is not of importance in
conservative dentistry; only for procedures employed
on skin.
The operative site may not be totally sterilized, but
gross cleaning action reduces the incidence of post-
operative infections.
The iodophor compounds are the most effective
solutions for the skin preparation.
If there are hair in the surgical area, it is removed
just prior to scrubbing the skin. (Shaving the area the
night before may produce small abrasions on the skin
and resident bacteria can multiply as a result of the
injury.)
The scrub should begin in the center of the area
and then move outward concentrically. Once the
center has been scrubbed, it should not be touched
again with the same sponge.

c. Draping the Patient
A double layer drape is considered adequate. The
patient’s head is wrapped with double sheet, using a
drape as the lower sheet and a hand towel as the upper
sheet.
Sterile drapings are secured with towel clips. The
anesthetist and his or her equipment are isolated from
the operating team by drape-covered screen.
A gown, drape or covering is considered to be
contaminated when wet, unless they are waterproof
or otherwise backed by a waterproof lining.
D. Protection against Aerosols and Spatter
The rubber dam has been shown to be an effective
barrier to reduce the number of organisms in aerosols.
The rubber dam prevents aerosolization of saliva and
should be used whenever possible.
In addition to rubber dam, high volume evacuator
and low volume saliva ejector help in minimizing
contact with aerosols and spatter. While using low
suction saliva ejection, caution is needed to reduce
chances of back flow into patients’ mouth.
Patients rinsing their oral cavities with an
antimicrobial solutions just before the treatment has
been shown to reduce the number of bacteria that exit
the oral cavity in the form of aerosols and spatter
during operative procedures.
E. Surface Asepsis
Micro-organisms can survive for prolonged period
on instrument surfaces, particularly when associated
with saliva or blood.
The dental equipment must be disinfected/
sterilized in between patient visits.
Surface asepsis includes:
• Clean and disinfect contaminated surfaces
• Prevent the surface from being contaminated (use
of surface barriers).
The general principle is to use barriers, which are
manufactured from a material that is impermeable to
water, blood, saliva and microbes.
The barrier can be of laminated paper-plastics,
treated paper/fabric or aluminum foil.
a. Instrument Delivery
Instrument delivery implies shifting of sterilized
instrument to the site of operatory. The surfaces
should be cleaned and disinfected before the next
patient is attended. A disinfectant should be applied
to the contaminated surface either by spraying or by
using a saturated pad.
The effective method of instrument delivery is
through the use of barrier materials. Non-permeable
single layer covers and barriers should be used during
each patient.
• Plastic food wraps, plastic bags, aluminum foil or
custom made barriers provide an inexpensive
method of protection from local contaminants.
• Special attention must be paid to equipment knobs,
switches and handles that are touched during
treatment. These items should be covered
individually with wraps.
• Handpiece hangers are subjected to direct
contamination and must be carefully disinfected
between patients.
• The edge of unit heads is especially prone to
contamination when grasped with fingers during
movement. Equipment should be moved only with
handles, which are easily disinfected and/or
protected with barrier materials.
b. Dental Chair
The dental chair including upholstery, should
withstand repeated disinfection. The upholstery
should be removable for cleaning and replacements.
The frame should be of non-organic material, that
does not support the growth of pathogens. All chair
functions should be controlled from a foot switch to
avoid possible contamination by use of hand operated
switches.
The potential for cross-contamination in a dental
chair is from chair mounted controls. The finger-
operated switches normally have cracks and crevices,
which are difficult to clean and disinfect.
The headrests should be covered by disposable
covers. Arm slings tend to trap debris and must be
carefully cleaned prior to disinfection.
c. Operator Stool
Adjustment of the height of the stool is usually
accompanied by grasping a lever located under the
stand. Covering the lever with plastic sheath will
control cross-contamination.
The seat material should be of vinyl plastic,
particularly when maximum asepsis is required.
Cleaning and disinfecting the seat covering may be
accomplished with soap and water.
d. Cabinet
The materials and equipment should be stored out of
the treatment room and brought in only when needed
for a particular procedure.
The cabinets should be made of materials that will
withstand repeated cleaning and disinfection. Work
surfaces should be disinfected for each patient. Other
surfaces can be disinfected twice a week.

Sterilization and Infection Control
e. Utility Items
A potential source of physical and microbial
contamination is the main water supply. Preventive
measures for water systems include use of a water
sediment filter and softening and/or deionization of
the incoming water supply, which will help prevent
clogging of water passages in dental equipment.
The sediment trap on incoming line is a real source
of contamination. The trap should be located in an
accessible area. The trap should be disassembled and
flushed with copious amounts of water to remove all
sediments and debris collected in the cup.
The air compressor intake filter must be positioned
in an area of clean, cool and dry air. Care must be
taken so that the intake filter is cleaned and replaced
on a regular schedule.
Air compressor tank must be drained periodically
to ensure that water is not building up in the tank.
This water is a source of microbial contamination.
f. Dental Unit Water Lines
It has been established that the dental unit water lines
are usually contaminated.
Contaminated oral fluids may be drawn back into
turbine chamber of handpiece or may be retracted into
the water lines. While dental units have anti-retraction
valves, these valves do fail. The retraction should be
checked periodically by observing the tip of waterline
proper. If a drop of water ‘hangs’ on tip, retraction is
not adequate.
The dental unit waterlines become colonized with
micro-organisms including bacteria, fungi and
protozoa, which form the biofilm. The micro-
organisms in the biofilm produce a protective
polysaccharide matrix that serves as a reservoir,
which can amplify the number of micro-organisms.
These micro-organisms exit through dental handpiece
or air water syringe.
The ADA recommends that infection control
measures be followed, such that dental unit should
contain <200 CFU/mL (colony-forming unit/mL).
The dental waterlines should be flushed at the
beginning of the day to decrease the microbial load.
However, this does not affect biofilm in the waterline
or improve the quality of water used during dental
treatment. The effective methods include self-
contained water systems combined with chemical
treatment, and in-line microfilters.
The blood and saliva with micro-organisms can
enter the dental water system during patient treatment.
The majority of dental units are engineered to
prevent retraction of oral fluids. Even with anti-
retraction valves, flushing for a minimum of 20–30
seconds after each patient is recommended.
Sterile solutions are used as a coolant/irrigant
performing surgical procedures. Conventional dental
units cannot deliver sterile water even when equipped
with independent water reservoirs because the water
bearing pathway cannot be reliably sterilized.
Delivery devices, preferably disposable products,
should be used to deliver sterile water.
g. Saliva Ejectors
Back flow from low-saliva ejectors occurs when the
pressure in the patient’s oral cavity is less than that
in the evacuator. The micro-organisms present in the
line usually get retracted into the patients’ oral cavity.
This back flow can be a potential source of cross-
contamination.
The suction tubings should be smooth and even
on both internal and external surfaces. A few systems
are available with removable tubings, which may be
heat sterilized; however, most systems need
disinfection procedures.
Tubings for handpieces, scalers, air/water syringes
and other instruments are difficult to maintain in an
aseptic condition.
Cameras, light-curing units, lasers, air abrasion
units, etc. are miscellaneous items that must be
protected against contamination. They are used in
operatory and cannot be sterilized or even readily
disinfected. Clear plastic bags of suitable size should
be used to cover these items.
The researchers are working hard to improve the
sterilization and asepsis protocol of the dental
equipment. Molded, seamless components are
being designed and manufactured for all types of
treatment equipment. Non-contact controls are being
popularized for achieving better asepsis.
