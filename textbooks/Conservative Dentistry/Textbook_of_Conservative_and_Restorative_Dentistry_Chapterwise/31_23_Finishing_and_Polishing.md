The main objective of restorative dentistry is to restore
the individual tooth to its form and functions along
with imparting pleasing esthetics and maintaining
periodontal tissues in good esteem. Many a times, the
form and function is restored but the surface may not
be smooth. It has been established that rough/uneven
surfaces invite microbial flora; also the light reflected
by these surfaces is not uniform. A restored tooth
should be evenly smooth and reflect light uniformly.
The process of making the surface smooth, reflecting
the light evenly, is described as finishing and polishing.
DEFINITIONS
Finishing is the removal of surface irregularities;
whereas, polishing is the creation of surface layer,
which can reflect light as good as enamel surface.
‘Finishing’ is defined as “the transformation of an
object from a rough to a refined form. The procedure
involves removal of surface irregularities and shaping the
restoration according to functional occlusion”. ‘Polishing’
is defined as “the production of a shiny, mirror like surface,
which reflects light similar to enamel without creating
supplemental films by the addition of wax or lacquer”.
‘Buffing’ refers to the polishing process in which
the abrasives are applied via bristle brushes (for
leather and cloth materials).
‘Finishing’ can be described as a process whereby
substrate particles are removed by the action of
cutting and/or grinding. The surface of the substrate
is made to come into frictional contact with a
comparatively harder material. This contact generates
enough tensile and shear stresses to overcome the
forces of atomic bonds and thereby release particles
from the substrate. In a ‘cutting operation’, the
substrate particles are removed by the use of any
instrument, which acts in a blade-like fashion. A
‘grinding operation’ on the other hand removes small
particles of the substrate through the action of bonded
or coated abrasive instruments.
The process of finishing leads to:
• Gross reduction of excess restorative material
• Contouring: Reproduction of size, shape, pits/
grooves and other details of the tooth form.
• Establishes a well-adapted tooth–restoration
junction
• Removes gross scratches.
‘Polishing’ is the most refined process and acts on
an extremely thin region of the substrate surface. It
produces very fine scratches that can be visible only
under high magnification. An ideally polished surface
does not have any surface imperfections, but this
condition is very difficult to achieve because most of
the restorative materials are brittle and get damaged
even during fine grinding.
The polishing material does not cut or grind, but
fills fine scratches and produces a perfectly smooth
surface. During the polishing of metals, a highly
microcrystalline layer is formed on the surface, called
the ‘Beilby layer’. This layer is composed of non-
oriented crystals and is 20–40 Å thick. It is hypo-
thesized that because of rapid movement of the
polishing agent, top layer of the material gets heated
up, causing it to flow and fill the scratches.
The optimum speed for polishing varies with the
hardness of material. The harder the material, higher
should be the speed (Chrome cobalt alloys require
higher speed than gold alloys).
The final surface character achieved on a restorative
material is the combination of finishing and polishing.
The polished surface can also be obtained by a coating,
viz. electroplated deposits, sealants, unfilled resin and
overglazes on porcelain.
The following factors influence the effectiveness of
finishing/polishing procedures:
• Physical/mechanical properties of the restorative
material
• Hardness, size and shape of the abrasive used
• Difference of hardness between the restorative
material and the abrasive used. (Greater the
difference, more will be the abrasive effect; for
example, hardness of porcelain (Moh’s scale) is 7
and the hardness of tungsten carbide/aluminum

Objectives of Finishing and Polishing
The objectives of finishing and polishing are:
• Adequate adaptation and continuity of the
restoration margins with the tooth surface.
• Optimum restoration contour; proper occlusal
contacts.
• Surface free of any scratches or irregularities;
biologically acceptable.
• Adjustment of minor morphological or marginal
defects.
• Enhanced oral functions.
• Obtain smooth surface, which can reflect light
similar to that of enamel (A surface becomes reflective
only when surface irregularities are well below 10 m;
below the resolution of wavelength of light).
• Improve oral hygiene.
Finishing and Polishing Instruments
a. Finishing burs: 12–30 fluted carbide burs are
usually used for finishing depending upon the
restorative material. Plain burs are also used before
final polishing. Burs are available in various sizes
and shapes, viz., straight fissure, tapering, flame
shaped, rounded, wheel, etc. (Fig. 23.1).
b. Diamond instruments and pastes: Diamond is
considered as a super abrasive because it is capable
of abrading almost any other material. It is available
in the form of bonded abrasive rotary instruments,
metal backed abrasive strips and polishing pastes.
Finishing diamonds of medium-fine grit contain
diamond particles of 8.0–50.0 m in diameter
(Fig. 23.2). These instruments should always be
used with a light speed (less than 50,000 rpm) and
copious water spray to preserve the very fine
diamond coatings. Diamond polishing pastes are
also available with particle sizes  ranging  from  2.0–
5.0 m.
c. Brushes: Brushes can be used either alone or in
combination with the abrasive particles. Most of
the brushes have synthetic bristles; a few may have
wire bristles for polishing cast restorations. Various
types of brushes are available, which may be
inserted into a handpiece or attached to a mandrel
(Fig. 23.3).
d. Cloth: Cloth carried on a metal  wheel may be used
for final polishing, with or without a polishing
medium.
e. Felt: Felt is used to attain lustre for the metallic
restorations usually with a polishing agent. It is
available in different shapes of wheels, cones and
cylinders.
f. Rubber instruments: Rubber-end tools are commonly
used for polishing procedures. These are available
in various shapes of cups, wheels, cones, etc., and

ABRASIVE MATERIALS
a. Chalk: It is a white-colored abrasive composed of
calcium carbonate. It serves as a mild abrasive for
polishing tooth enamel, gold foil, amalgam and
plastic materials.
b. Pumice: It is the most common abrasive, used
mainly for softer materials. Pumice slurry is a mix
of pumice and water whereas a pumice paste is a
mix of pumice and glycerine. Pumice can be used
for polishing tooth enamel, gold foil, amalgam and
acrylic resins. Pumice powder is mixed with water
or glycerine and carried to the surface by a
revolving brush. Slow speed is recommended
during its use; otherwise the centrifugal force
throws out most of the pumice from the brush.
c. Corundum: This is a form of aluminum oxide
(usually  white) usually available in the form of
bonded abrasive. Corundum  is  used primarily for
grinding metal alloys.
d. Emery: Emery is greyish-black corundum; a
mixture of aluminum oxide and iron. It is
commonly used as an abrasive coating on a cloth
or paper disc. It may be used for finishing metal
alloys and plastic materials.
e. Garnet: Garnet includes a number of materials,
such as silica, aluminum, cobalt, iron, magnesium
and manganese. Its color is dark red and is
extremely hard (highly abrasive). It is used in
grinding metal alloys and plastic materials.
f. Sand: Sand is predominantly silica. Irregularly
shaped particles are bonded to the paper with glue
or resin. It is supplied in the  form of sand paper.
Sand particles under air pressure are also used to
remove the investment material from base metal
alloy castings.
g. Tripoli: The gray- and red-colored tripoli are
commonly used for polishing metal alloys and
some plastic materials.
h. Kieselguhr: In addition to its use as a fine abrasive
on paper discs, it is also used as filler in various
impression materials. The coarser form is called
‘diatomaceous earth’. Chronic exposure to this
material can cause respiratory silicosis.
i. Cuttle fish bone: It is available as coated discs and
strips for polishing metal margins and amalgam
restorations.
j. Quartz: Quartz is hard, colorless and transparent
material. Sharp particles of quartz are coated over
paper plastic discs. Quartz abrasives are mostly
used to finish metal alloys.
k. Zirconium silicate: It is an off-white mineral used
to make coated discs and strips. It forms an
important component of the prophylaxis pastes.
l. Silicon carbide (carborundum): Mounted points,
coated discs and grinding wheels are the most
common form of carborundum. It has a green and
blue-black color. Carborundum wears away
rapidly at high heat; should be kept wet when in
use. Boron carbide has also been used.

ADJUNCT FINISHING MODALITIES
Certain procedures, designed as adjust finishing
modalities, are used to achieve smooth surfaces before
the final process of finishing and polishing.
A. Microabrasion
The process of microabrasion is basically used to
remove stained surfaces. A paste (18% hydrochloric
acid, pumice and water) is applied to the desired
surface with a slow driven rubber cup. The rubber
cup should be rotated at very slow speed to avoid
removing any excess tooth structure and to prevent
spatter. If the defect is only confined to the superficial
0.2–0.3 mm of enamel, the acid pumice paste strips
away the affected surface, resulting in improved
appearance with clinically unrecognizable enamel
loss. Acid causes dissolution of enamel and the
abrasive causes removal of superficial stains and
defects. After application of a topical fluoride solution
and final finishing and polishing, normal enamel
surface characteristics are established.
One such system, ‘Air-flow handy system’, uses
sodium bicarbonate as an abrasive agent. The powder
is passed through a narrow nozzle of specially
designed handpiece with the help of compressed air
(Fig. 23.6).
B. Burnishing
‘Burnishing’ is defined as “the process of rubbing a metal
over the restorative material to make it shiny or lustrous”.
Further, it aids in adaptation and compaction of the
material at the margins.
‘Burnishing’ has also been described as a procedure
in which the surface of a metallic restoration is
smoothened by rubbing it with another small, highly
polished, hard metal surface.
Instrument tips used for burnishing are smooth
faced and are available in various shapes, such as,
bell shaped, egg shaped, apple shaped, etc. Special
burnisher, like ‘Spratley burnisher’ is used for
proximal gingival marginal burnishing of metallic
restorations.
Burnishing is commonly employed for amalgam
and gold foil restorations and is always carried out
from the restoration surface to the tooth surface.
a. Burnishing of Amalgam Restorations
The appropriate time for burnishing amalgam
restorations may be divided as the ‘pre-carve
burnishing’ and the ‘post-carve burnishing’. Pre-carve
burnishing, as the name suggests, is carried out before
the carving is initiated and is in fact a continuation of
the condensation process. Large egg-shaped/ball-
shaped burnisher is used with a pressure equal to that
used for condensing amalgam. The burnisher is
moved mesiodistally and faciolingually taking care
that its head contacts only the cuspal slopes and not
the margins. It is established that burnishing during
condensation improves marginal adaptation both for
the conventional and high copper amalgams.
‘Post-carve burnishing’ is carried out after the
carving is completed. Instruments used for amalgam
carving are shown in Fig. 23.7. A round burnisher of
appropriate size is moved in light strokes from the
amalgam to the tooth surface. Proximal portions can
be similarly burnished using a beaver tail burnisher
and/or Spratley burnisher. Caution should be
exercised against using excessive pressure leading to
heat generation. Temperature greater than 60°C can
damage the pulp beyond repair and release mercury
to the surface. The presence of mercury-rich layer at
the margins promotes corrosion and may lead to
fracture.The properties achieved by post-carve
burnishing are: Increased surface hardness, decrease
in the size and number of voids on the surface and
marginal areas, a slower rate of corrosion, denser
amalgam at the margins, and improvement in the
marginal seal. With the high copper amalgams, post-

b. Burnishing of Direct Gold Restorations
Spratley burnisher is used with considerable pressure

c. Burnishing of Cast Gold Restorations
The time of burnishing a cast gold restoration varies
with the type of cementing medium used. If zinc
phosphate cement is used for cementing, burnishing
is delayed and carried out after 24 hours. The
burnishing is delayed so as to let the cement dissolve
at the margins. Once the cement is dissolved,
burnishing is carried out to achieve the gold–tooth
interface rather than gold–cement–tooth interface. On
the other hand, if glass-ionomer cement/resin cement
is used, the burnishing can be carried out immediately
after setting, since these cements are less soluble in
oral fluids.
Hand burnisher and rotary burnisher may be

A. Amalgam Restorations
The cavity  is slightly overfilled during  amalgam
condensation. The mercury-rich layer of the last
increment is removed during carving. (Within 3–5
minutes, the amalgam is sufficiently hardened.)
Clinically, a ringing sound is considered the ideal
period for carving. It is opined that burnishing should
be carried out both prior to and after carving to
prepare the restoration for finishing and polishing.
Burnishing produces ten-fold decrease in surface
roughness.
The restoration is left undisturbed for a minimum
period of 24 hours before it is finally finished and
polished (time required for setting, hardening and
dimensional changes of amalgam).
After 24 hours, the surface of the restoration is
rough due to the heterogeneous structure of set
amalgam.
Finishing is initiated using finishing burs/stones
(Figs 23.8 and 23.9).  Initial finishing help trimming
excess amalgam at the margins; also correct occlusal
disharmony, if any. A common error in the trimming
of amalgam is that of leaving fins or flashes at the
margins. Since amalgam has a low tensile strength,
thin sections of amalgam  can  easily  get  fractured
resulting  in marginal ditching.
For the proximo-occlusal restorations, overhangs
are removed by using periodontal files or gold foil
knives. Finishing of cervical areas is carried out by
moving the fine water-resistant strips cervical to the
contact area through the interdental space. The facial
and lingual proximal margins are  finished with light
cuttle fish sandpaper discs.
It is emphasized that abrasives should be applied
in a descending grade, (i.e. coarse, medium, fine and
ultra-fine), to achieve the final finish. The final polish/
metallic lustre is achieved by the application of a
suitable polishing agent, like tin oxide, zinc oxide,
chalk, pumice, etc. carried with a soft rotating brush
or rubber cup. For polishing in the gingival
embrasures, the abrasive is introduced through
polishing strips.
During polishing, the restoration should be kept
moist; low rotational speeds with light intermittent
pressure should be used to avoid any overheating.
Excessive heat may damage the pulp and also tend
to bring mercury to the surface.

B. Composite Restorations
The features, which influence the process of finishing
and polishing are as follows:
i. Conventional composites pose difficulty in
achieving a smooth surface because of the
difference in hardness of organic and inorganic
phases. The resin matrix is soft and filler particles
are hard. If fine grit polishing methods are used,
the softer resin matrix abrades away easily, leaving
behind the harder filler particles, which gives it a
rough surface. If coarse abrasives are used,  the
organic  and inorganic phases are removed
equally, but the abrasive leaves behind rough
marks. The unequal surfaces of conventional
composites lead to unequal wear, which may
further disturb the occlusal harmony.
ii. Hybrid composites can be polished to a semigloss
state, but the surface is hydrophobic; unpleasant
for the patient.
iii. Microfilled composites can be polished to the
highest gloss state and are considered to be
esthetically best amongst all composites.
A surface finish attained with the use of a plastic
matrix band is the most desirable finish; however, this
is rarely achieved, because of the need for contouring
and removal of excess material. It is advisable to
contour the unpolymerized composite with hand
instruments, so that the need for removal of large
amounts of set resin leading to surface damage are
minimized.
The procedures for finishing and polishing of direct
and indirect composite restorations are described
separately:
a. Direct Composite Restorations
Excess composite at the cavosurface margins is
scraped away using a scalpel or a sharp gold knife.
The use of stainless steel instruments should be
avoided, as these tend to leave gray marks on the
restoration. The 30 fluted carbide burs and diamond
points (15–45 m size) are recommended for gross
finishing (Fig. 23.12).
Rotary instruments should be used with absolute
gentle pressure. High pressure tends to loosen and
dislodge the filler particles. For the occlusal surface,
cuttlefish or silicon dioxide-coated discs and strips
are used in a descending grade of their abrasiveness.
Finer discs are used for producing a smooth surface
texture and refined marginal adaptation (Fig. 23.13).
Vaseline or petroleum jelly should be used as
lubricant with these discs and strips (Fig. 23.14A, B).

The composite inlay/onlay is primarily trimmed and

Finishing and Polishing
The ideal surface finish for glass-ionomer cements
is produced by the matrix strip. The gross finishing
is carried out immediately after the strip matrix is
removed. The surface should be coated with a
protective layer while the excess is being trimmed. A
sharp gold finishing knife is used to remove the excess
cement. The trimmed restoration is again coated with
cavity varnish/petroleum jelly to protect the cement
from moisture. Rotary instruments can also be used
at slower speeds to remove excesses at the margins.
(Manual cutting instruments may tear the material at
the margins, causing marginal breakdown, eventually
staining and recurrent caries).
The contours and embrasures are finished with fine
diamonds points/12 fluted carbide finishing burs.
Margins are finished by using 12 – 38  inch fine cuttle-
fish discs. Aluminum oxide-coated discs and Soflex
discs with Vaseline have shown good results;
whereas, use of a tungsten carbide bur disrupted the
surface of even mature glass-ionomer cements. A
lubricant, such as cocoa butter or petroleum jelly,
should be used along with finishing and polishing
instruments to avoid excessive heat generation and
desiccation. Water spray cooling is not recommended,
as it may lead to rough surface. Air coolants may be
used. Polyacrylic gel has also been used for polishing
glass-ionomer restorations.
The scanning electron microscopic picture of
polished glass-ionomer cement is shown in Fig. 23.23.
D. Direct Gold Restorations
Burnishing is the first step in finishing a gold
restoration. For the occlusal surfaces, a Spratley
burnisher or ball burnisher is moved with
considerable pressure from the metal to the tooth
surface. A Spratley blade carver is used in the
embrasures for proximal restorations.
The optimum contour to the restoration is
provided by instruments, viz. Morse scaler, Jones
knife or Cleoid discoid carver. The abrasive strips and
discs are used to finish proximal  areas. Small discs
should be used to prevent any damage to the gingival
tissue. Abrasives (mostly cuttlefish) used on the discs
should be in a descending grade. The discs are rotated
from the filling to the tooth surface to achieve smooth
margins and surfaces.
Excess gold in the gingival area of the restoration
is removed using the contrabevel end of a Wedelstedt
chisel. A No. 12 Bard-Parker blade/Wilson’s knife
may also be used. Discs should not be used in the
cervical areas; cementum may get damaged.
After the surface has been smoothened, burnishing
is repeated on all the margins. Polishing with tin oxide
applied with a rubber cup provides high metallic
lustre and satin finish. Polishing instruments should
be confined to the restoration surface and used with
air coolants.
E. Cast Gold Restorations
Initial finishing of a gold inlay is carried out on the
die. The casting is cleaned using fine steam at pressure
(Fig. 23.24). The casting is examined for any nodules/
voids or adherent investment. Defective casting is to
be repeated. The casting is pickled in 50% hydrochloric
acid and neutralized in sodium bicarbonate. The clean
casting is tried in the die. Once satisfied with the

Any extension of the casting beyond the margins is

into the prepared cavity with mild pressure. If the
casting does not seat completely, it can be because of
heavy proximal contacts, uneven internal surface or
an actual distortion. A dental floss passed through
the contact area gives an idea of tightness of contact
and should be accordingly reduced.
After the casting is seated into the cavity, the

occlusal contacts are first verified in the intercuspal

surfaces, the excess is reduced with a carborundum
disc or fine cuttle discs.

bristle brush and Tripoli. The brush should always

achieving a very high lustre, a chamois wheel with
rouge may be used (Fig. 23.26A, B). Over polishing

Finishing and Polishing
Electrolytic polishing works on the principle of
reverse electroplating in which the restoration acts
as the anode. The electrolyte is usually a mixture of
sulphuric acid, phosphoric acid, glycerine and water.
A current density of 0.5–1.0 amps/mm2 at voltage of
4–10 V is passed for a few minutes at room
temperature. The anode, i.e. the restoration ionizes
and is depleted of a very small amount of alloy (a
few angstroms) more readily on the rough surfaces
than the smooth surfaces. These first products of
electrolysis are collected in the hollows of the rough
metal surface. The prominences are gradually
dissolved and a smooth surface results.
G. Porcelain Restorations
There are three methods of fabrication of ceramic
inlays and onlays:
a. Conventional method using firing porcelain,
b. Milling ceramic blocks using machinable ceramic
c. Casting by the lost wax technique using castable
and pressable ceramic.
The second and third methods do not use the
application of a glaze layer to attain the final surface
finish.
The technique for finishing and polishing of the
restoration by each method is as follows.
a. Restoration formed by Firing Porcelain
The desired finish on the fired ceramic restoration is
achieved by glazing and to a smaller extent by polishing.
A glazed surface of porcelain is comparatively much
stronger than the unglazed porcelain.
Positive effects of glazing:
• Glazed surface leads to reduced wear of opposing
surfaces.
• Oral functions are improved; food slides easily over
the restoration.
• Improved oral hygiene (Rough/grinded/unglazed
surface invites bacterial accumulation.)
• Restorations are esthetically pleasing (Light
reflection is appropriate.)
• Glazing provides proper strength (Strength is
reduced during grinding/adjusting ceramic 
restoration.)
• Glazing reduces flaws and eliminates crack
propagation.
Once the glaze layer is removed, transverse
strength of the restoration almost reduces to one-half.
This situation has a clinical significance, since the
restoration often requires occlusal adjustment after
placement. Any adjustment makes the surface rough
and may lead to loss of glaze layer, thereby reducing
the strength of the restoration. However,  with the
use of polishing systems, like Soflex discs, Porcelain
polishing kit, etc., it is possible to minimize the
damage caused by intraoral adjustment.
Types of Glazing
Glazing is of two types:
i. ‘Autoglazing’: It is a process in which a smooth
surface is obtained without the use of an additional
glaze. By careful control of the furnace
temperature (955°C–1065°C), a glaze is created.
The surface porcelain melts slightly, flows and
glazes; may result in little roundening of the
corners. The disadvantages with this technique are
that any excessive glazing would destroy the
shape of porcelain, increase porosity, reduce
strength and may alter the color. This technique
requires great precision, but provides adequate
esthetic appearance.

A
Concept of Finishing Polish
A ‘finishing polish’ is considered as the last step in
polishing (can be designated as final polishing).
Finishing polish removes residual oxidation layer and
left-over minor imperfections. The modality provides
smooth polish without any surface cutting. A few
agents contain oil in polishing pastes to enhance glaze.
A polishing pad is used with gentle finger pressure
to achieve the requisite finishing polish. A few authors
have labeled the technique as ‘Jeweling’.

restorative material or micro-organisms) in air.

more than 24 hours, thereby promoting cross-
contamination. A major pulmonary disease that

• Adequate ventilation in the operatory that filters

• Use of safety glasses with side shields,

• Use high vacuum suction.

during finishing of amalgam may form mercury
vapor. Thermal decomposition of polymeric
materials, like acrylic resins and composites,
produces monomer vapors. The production of
vapors can be minimized by using low speeds and
intermittent cutting. If using high speeds, copious
air-water spray should be used as a coolant.
Inhalation of these vapors can be prevented
following the same procedures as given for
aerosols.
c. Pulpal reactions: The finishing and polishing
instruments may raise the temperature by at least
20°C. If the remaining dentin and enamel thickness
is less, this heat is rapidly conducted to the
underlying pulp resulting in various pulpal
ailments. Proper use of coolants, such as water, air
and air-water spray protects against excessive rise
in temperature. Cup brushes are preferred for
polishing as compared to rubber appliances as the
former generate less heat. Also, use of intermittent
polishing at low speeds is preferred to prevent
excessive heat production.
d. Soft tissue injuries: Soft tissues, like lips, cheeks,
tongue and gingiva may be injured, if adequate care
is not taken to protect them.
e. Miscellaneous
• Impact of the instrument may damage the filler
particles of composites.
• Voids incorporated during the filling process
may be exposed, lodging unsightly strains.
• Composites can be lodged in the oral soft tissues
during finishing and shaping procedures, which
can lead to persistent chronic inflammation of
those tissues.
• Beryllium fumes could be formed when finishing
chrome–cobalt alloys containing beryllium.
Beryllium is also a known carcinogenic.
• Nickle, which is also a component of chrome–
cobalt alloys, has an ability to produce allergy.
The well-finished and polished restorations are not
only esthetically pleasing, but are also conducive to
the oral environment. The proper marginal
adaptability of the restoration saves  an individual
from periodontal problems, subsequently achieving
better clinical success of the treatment.
