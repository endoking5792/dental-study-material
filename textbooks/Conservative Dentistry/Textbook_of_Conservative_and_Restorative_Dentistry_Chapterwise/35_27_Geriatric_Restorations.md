The relationship of oral health/diseases with the
systemic health, particularly in the elderly, is well
established. Therefore, retention of teeth can have an
impressive impact on the overall physical and mental
health of an individual.
The term geriatrics stems from a Greek word ‘Geron’
meaning ‘old man’ and ‘Iatros’ meaning ‘healer’. The
chronological age of ‘old’ is dynamic and keep
changing with time. The groups recognized by
consensus are: Young old (65–74); older old (75–84)
and the old (more than 85). Geriatric dentistry is
defined as “the delivery of dental care to older adults
involving diagnosis, prevention and treatment of problems
associated with normal aging and age-related diseases as
part of an interdisciplinary team with other health care
professionals”.
Life expectancy has doubled in the past century,
more so in the last two decades. This improved life
expectancy is a result of improved health awareness,
accessibility, affordability and acceptability of
improved medical care.
Old age is associated with several risk factors;
general as well as specific to the oral cavity. Managing
compromised dentition is indeed a multi-faceted
challenge. The general risk factors include various
intercurrent and psychological problems. The risk
factors related specifically to oral cavity are gingival
recession and age-related odontometric changes.
The health care providers should be familiar with
the course and the complications associated with the
systemic conditions. For example, patients with some
form of cardiovascular disease are vulnerable to
physical/emotional stress, which may be encountered
during dental treatment.
Some patients may not be able to tolerate reclining
position on the chair. The elderly are more susceptible
to orthostatic hypertension, hence need special
attention, especially when they move from a reclining
posture to a standing position. Vasoconstrictors should
not be administered to patients  with  unstable  angina,
uncontrolled hypertension or people undergone
coronary bypass surgery. Prophylactic antibiotics may
be necessary for patients with history of high-risk
cardiac conditions, while undertaking surgical
procedures; however, restorations can be placed
without prior medication. The psychoemotional chord
of the elderly patients should always be respected and
taken care of; individuals be motivated as regard their
life expectancy and prognosis of the treatment.
The elderly individuals may suffer from decline in
their cognitive functions. Senile dementia, a common
phenomenon amongst the elderly, may lead to
confusion, difficulty in making decisions, compre-
hension and even alter the patients’ ability to learn
and concentrate on new tasks.
AGE CHANGES IN DENTAL TISSUES
Aging of an individual is manifested at all levels of
life. Age changes are considered to be the result of
day-to-day biological activities. Age changes are slow
or non-existent in tissues that do not exhibit biological
reactivity. It is often difficult to differentiate between
physiological and the pathological age changes. Aging
can be defined as the combination of process beyond
development and maturation, which results in a
diminution of capacity of the tissues. The physical
state of aging is referred to as 'senescence' and the study
of the aging process is known as 'gerontology.'
Effects of Aging
The general features encountered in aging are:
• Tissue desiccation
• Reduced elasticity
• Diminished reparative capacity
• Altered cell permeability
• Keratinization is diminished (thin dermis and
epidermis)
• Decreased blood supply
• Degeneration of nerve endings
• Capillaries are fragile; may result in development
of hematomas even after a minor trauma

MECHANISM OF AGING
The mechanism of aging is categorized into four types:
i. Biologic clock
ii. Immunological
iii. DNA damage related
iv. Damage of other cellular components
The mechanism of aging has been summarized in
following two theories:
a. Cross-Linkage Theory
According to this theory, aging starts with the
formation of cross-linkages in proteins and nucleic
acids. Cross-linkages can also occur between molecules
of collagen, which makes it tougher and more rigid.
Cross-linked collagen is more readily infiltrated with
calcium deposits. A previous aging theory, known as
‘calcium theory’, linked aging to the gradual
accumulation of calcium deposits throughout the body.
Once a substance is cross-linked, it eventually loses its
function. It is considered that cross-linkage prevents
certain protein and nucleic acid molecules from being
metabolized by the body’s enzymes, which then
accumulate in the cells.
Aging leads to accumulation of cross-linkage agents.
These agents can be derived from foods, drugs, and
pollutants.
The cross-linkage agents include acetaldehyde,
formaldehyde, pyruvic acid, citric acid, lipid peroxides,
silicon, lead, aluminum, copper, iron, calcium, zinc,
etc. Glucose can attach to proteins and nucleic acids to
form cross-linkage products. Tobacco smoke contains
acrolein and glycerin, which are considered to be cross-
linkage agents. Cross-linkages can induce somatic
mutations and protein degeneration; subsequently,
change the immunologic behavior.
b. Free-Radical Theory
Free radicals can propagate through hundreds of
thousands of molecules and wreak havoc before they
encounter another free radical and become neutralized.
Free radicals have been called the “great white sharks of
the biochemical sea of life”. If unchecked, free radicals
can continue to create other free radicals from normal
molecules, thereby disrupting vital functions. In this
way, free radicals can cause serious effects to cellular
metabolism as a consequence of damage to DNA,
RNA, enzymes, lipids, immune cells, and cell
membranes. Free radicals are produced as byproducts
of normal oxygen metabolism and oxidation of
unsaturated fatty acids.
This theory states that oxygen-derived free radicals
cause lipid oxidation, which in turn damages cell
membranes and other cellular structures.
Mitochondria are the main source of free radicals. The
body naturally contains antioxidant mechanisms that
quench free radicals. With aging, these mechanisms
become less effective and more free radicals are
generated, leading to tissue damage.
AGING AND DENTAL TISSUES
Aging affects all tissues of oral cavity; described as
follows:
I. Age Changes in Enamel
a. Wearing of the enamel (loss of vertical dimension
and flattening of proximal contours).
b. The surfaces of recently erupted teeth are covered
with pronounced rod ends and perikymata. As the
age advances, there is generalized loss of rod ends
and flattening of the perikymata. Rate at which the
structure is lost depends on location of the surface
of the tooth and on location of the tooth in the oral
cavity.
c. Pronounced wear facets.
d. Surface darkening (Localized increase of nitrogen
and fluoride and change in organic content lead to
darkening of enamel surface).
e. Enamel becomes less permeable.
II. Age Changes in Dentin
a. Secondary dentin: The major age change in dentin
is the formation of secondary dentin. Secondary
dentin is deposited only after completion of
primary dentin.
A few authors describe secondary dentin as regular
and irregular. The regular secondary dentin is
considered physiological; whereas, irregular
secondary dentin is considered as pathological. The
irregular secondary dentin formed as a result of
abnormal irritation/stimulation is also called
‘reactionary dentin’ or ‘tertiary dentin’. Reactionary
dentin differs from reparative dentin as reactionary
dentin is generalized deposition by the pre-existing
odontoblasts and reparative dentin is localized
deposition formed by newly differentiated
odontoblasts in response to an aggressive local
stimulus.
This process of secondary dentin formation results
in gradual reduction in size of the pulp chamber
and root canals. The number of dentinal tubules is

no longer filled with living protoplasm, resulting

and other pulp cells attempt to seal off the dead
tracts by elaboration of the reparative dentin.
Dead tracts are seen in ground sections of teeth and
are manifested as a black zone by transmitted light
and as a white zone in reflected light. This optical
phenomenon is due to differences in the refractive
indices of affected tubules and the normal tubules.
The nature of change in the affected tubules is not
known, although these tubules are not calcified and
are permeable to the penetration of dyes.
d. Tertiary dentin: Tertiary dentin develops when
pulp irritants are more intense. Tertiary dentin
differs from secondary dentin, being localized
adjacent to the irritated zone, tubules irregular,
tortuous, and reduced in number or absent.
III. Age Changes in Pulp
a. Reduction in Size and Volume of Pulp
• The reduction in coronal pulp is the result of
continual apposition of dentin occlusally as well
at furcation area (Deposition of dentin at furcation
area is greater than that of occlusal dentin).
• Secondary dentin formation continues throughout
life and may result in complete pulpal obliteration
(Fig. 27.1A, B). In maxillary anterior teeth, the
secondary dentin is formed on lingual of the pulp
chamber; in molars, the maximum deposition takes
place on floor of the chamber.
b. Decrease in Number of Cells
• Number of cells are reduced by 50% in aged pulps.
• Fibroblasts show degeneration characterized by
small size and a decrease in  number  of  organelles.
• Degeneration of odontoblasts.
• Increase in vacuole numbers and gradual
degenerative changes leading to localized or
generalized absence of cells.
c. Presence of Calcifications
The types of calcifications recognized in dental pulp
are:
i. Discrete calcification
ii. Diffuse (dystrophic) calcification
i. Discrete calcification: Pulp stones or denticles are
discrete calcifications found freely within the pulp
tissue or attached/embedded in dentin. Denticles
are further classified according to structure and
location.

IV. Age Changes in Cementum
The process of depositon of cementum with age,
termed as ‘hypercementosis’, is an abnormal thickening
of cementum. It may be diffuse or circumscribed. It
can affect all the teeth; may confine to single tooth, or
even affects only parts of one tooth. The thickness of
young apical cementum is 100 to 200 m,  which may
increase two or three times, as the age advances.
The causes of hypercementosis are:
• Accelerated elongation of tooth
• Inflammation around the tooth
• Tooth repair
• Paget’s disease of bone
Accelerated elongation of tooth owing to loss of an
antagonist, leads to hyperplasia of cementum (may be
to maintain the normal width of the periodontal
ligament). Inflammation at the apex of the tooth root
stimulates excessive deposition of cementum. In such
case, cementum is laid down on the root surface at
some distance above the apex. Tooth repair does not,
always, result in deposition of the cementum. Occlusal
trauma may result in mild root resorption. Such root
resorption is repaired by deposition of cementum
between the root fragments as well as on their
periphery.
V. Age Changes in Bone
The characteristic changes in bone with age are:
• Density of bone is decreased with age; more so in
women (being less dense, the bone is more brittle).
• Lamina dura is often lost and the cortical bone
becomes thinner, especially at the angle of the
mandible.
• Porosity is increased, mainly due to increase in
vascular spaces.

a. Changes in gingiva and other areas of oral mucosa
• Diminished keratinization
• Reduced amount of stippling

• Decreased connective tissue cellularity

• Reduced oxygen consumption

• Increased keratinization of lip and cheek mucosa
• Atrophy of connective tissue with loss of

• Decrease in number of protein bound elements

• Increase in number of mast cells.

• Decreased width because of  continuous

VII. Age Changes in Salivary Glands
The diminished function of salivary glands is common
in geriatric individuals. With advancing age, there is
an atrophy of acinar tissue, coupled with degenerative
changes in the salivary glands. Decreased salivary
flow may not be directly related to the aging; however,
intake of certain drugs during aging might affect
salivary secretion. The diminished flow of saliva/
absence of saliva has many deleterious consequences
for the elderly individual.
VIII. Miscellaneous
• It is hypothesized that the older adults may exhibit
adverse reaction to injury or they might be more
resistant because of decreased permeability of
dentin (no proven documentation).
• No change in periradicular tissues as regard
cellularity and vascularity with age.
• Similar pattern of healing in young and old (critical
to healing is vascularity).
• Systemic conditions may affect restorative
treatment prognosis at any age.
CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS
The clinical implications of the effects of aging are as
follows:
I. Restorative Aspects
a. Attrition: With age, the enamel surface wears off
due to tooth-to-tooth contacts during various
functional/parafunctional movements of the
mandible. Attrition may result in exposure of
dentin, loss of vertical dimension and flattening of
proximal contour. Abnormal attrition may relate
to habits, such as tooth grinding or bruxism. The
attrited teeth are preserved and covered with full
crowns.
b. Cavity design: With age, the dentin loses its
resiliency (formation of transparent dentin);
subsequently, chances of fracture due to occlusal
stresses are increased. The loss of resiliency
(reduced organic components) of dentin can
increase the susceptibility of tooth to cracks and
cuspal fractures. Appropriate cavity preparation
without any overhanging and unsupported enamel
should be preferred.
c. Bonding: Partial or complete obliteration of the
dentinal tubules is commonly observed with age.
Even after increasing the etching time, the sclerotic
obliterations in the dentinal tubules cannot  be
completely  removed, resulting in minimal or no
resin tag formation. Further, the zone of resin-
impregnated sclerotic dentin is thinner as
compared to normal dentin. The tensile bond
strength to sclerotic root dentin with some
contemporary adhesives was found to be 20–45%
lower than those bonded to normal dentin.

gentle in aged patients. The cracked teeth should
always be considered when pulpal or periapical disease

III. Radiographs
The techniques of taking radiographs do not differ
much among older adults; however, physiologic and
anatomic changes may significantly affect their
interpretation. The presence of tori, exostoses, and
denser bone may require increased exposure times for
proper diagnostic contrast. Pulp recession is
accelerated by reparative dentin deposition. Receding
pulp horns that are apparent on a radiograph may
remain microscopically much higher. Narrow canals
are the rule in old patients. The narrowest point in the
canal may be difficult to determine; it is positioned
farther from the radiographic apex because of
continued cementum deposition.
IV.Access Preparation and
Working Length Determination
Identification of canal orifices is the most difficult
aspect of providing root canal treatment to older
patients. The decreased pulp chamber size makes the
canal orifice location difficult. The presence of pulp
stone creates problems in the biomechanical cleaning
and shaping the root canals. Although aging may
reduce the volume and coronal extent of the chamber
or canal orifice, its buccolingual and mesiodistal
portions remain the same. In addition, the cemento-
dentinal junction (CDJ) moves farther from the
radiographic apex with continued cementum
deposition, leading to difficulty in accurate working
length determination.
V. Tooth–Periodontium  Relationship
Attrition is the common  feature of aging. Occlusal
wear reduces  cusp  height  and  resultant increase in
food table area.
The process of attrition is related to changes, such
as continuous tooth eruption and gingival recession.
As the tooth erupts, cementum is usually deposited in
the apical region of the root. The reduction in bone
height that occurs with aging is not necessarily related
to occlusal wear.
If bone support is reduced, the clinical crown tends
to become disproportionately long and exerts excessive
leverage on the bone. By reducing the clinical crown
length, attrition appears to preserve the balance
between tooth and its bony support.
Wear of teeth on the proximal surfaces is
accompanied by mesial migration of the teeth.
Proximal wear reduces the anteroposterior length of
the dental arch by approximately 0.5 cm by the age of
40. Anteroposterior narrowing from proximal wear is
greater in teeth that taper toward the cervical aspect,
such as incisors. Progressive attrition and proximal
wear result in a reduced maxillary–mandibular overjet
in the molar area and on edge-to-edge bite anteriorly.
VI. Masticatory Efficiency
Slight atrophy of the musculature is considered as a
physiologic feature of aging.
The reduced masticatory efficiency in aged
individuals is due to:
• Unreplaced missing teeth
• Loose teeth
• Poorly fitting dentures
• Unwillingness to wear dentures
Reduced masticatory efficiency may lead to poor
chewing habits and the associated digestive disturbances.
Avitaminosis is relatively common in aged
individuals (An adequate intake of vitamin, calcium,
iron and potassium is particularly important to
geriatric patients).
VII. Root Caries
Root caries is one of the common findings in older
adults. Both active (progressive) and inactive (non-
progressive) root caries can be observed in aged
individuals.
The associated features, which lead to development
of root caries are:
i. Saliva: Aging per se has no significant clinical
impact on salivary secretion. The  salivary hypo-
function in the elderly is mostly due to intake of
medications.
ii. Gingival recession: Root surface caries cannot
develop unless preceded by gingival recession
(Fig. 27.2). With advancing age, the loss of
periodontal attachment is accompanied by
gradual recession of gingiva (Fig. 27.3).
iii. Age and sex: Root caries increases with age. Men
are generally more affected than women.

Four types of needs for any dental treatment have

perceived and normative. One should integrate these
needs into a holistic approach to achieve patient’s

Geriatric patients can be grouped into three
categories while planning treatment strategies, viz.
i) functionally independent, ii) frail, iii) functionally
dependent.
i. Care for functionally independent individuals:
The treatment is planned in following stages:
Stage I—Emergency phase: Initially, emergency
treatment is carried out to relieve pain, if any.
Stage II—Maintenance phase: It includes
management of infection, root planing and
curettage, restoration of carious lesions, and
patient education to improve oral health (such as,
changes in dietary habits, plaque control and use
of fluorides).
Stage III—Rehabilitation phase: It includes
restoration of occlusal plane and rehabilitation of
vertical dimension with fixed prosthesis.
ii and iii. Care for frail and functionally dependent
individuals: These patients require assistance even
for their normal day-to-day activities. Such
patients should be given minimum treatment
required to maintain physical and psychological
comfort. Physician’s presence may be necessary
in some patients. ART techniques may prove
useful in these cases for restorative management.

plan oral care for the geriatric patients, termed as

Systemic Diseases Influencing Treatment
Planning in Elderly
A thorough medical history should be recorded,
analyzing the probability of any systemic disease.
(Patient’s physician should be consulted.)
The systemic conditions, which warrant extra-
attention before any restorative treatment in older
adults, are as follows:
a. Cardiovscular Diseases
In patient with cardiovascular disease, it is advisable
to minimize any kind of stress in the clinic.
Hypertensive and patient taking anticoagulants
should be treated with utmost care. In any case where
the patient is at risk, the treatment is preferred under
antibiotic premedication.
Vasoconstrictors should not be administered to
patients with unstable angina, recent myocardial
infarction, recent stroke, recent arrhythmias and
hypertensive patients. Local anesthetic agents without
vasoconstrictor or minimal vasoconstrictors are
preferred for non-surgical procedures.
Vasoconstrictors should also be minimized in patient’s
undergone coronary bypass surgery, especially in first
three months. These patients may not require antibiotic
prophylaxis unless there are other complications.
Patients with artificial heart valves are considered
highly susceptible to bacterial endocarditis. Such
patients should be given prophylactic antibiotics before
initiating any treatment.
Key points
• Check whether hypertension is under control or not
• Carefully administer local anesthesia
Even though adrenaline is contraindicated in hypertensives,
amount available in 1:100000 solutions is safe. As
anesthetics without vasoconstrictors have a less effect and
shorter duration of action, slow injection (one minute for
1.8 mL) using 26 or 27 gauge needle is preferred.
• Aged patients under hypertensive drugs may suffer
from orthostatic blood pressure; operator should
elevate the back of chair slowly to the upright position.
b. Pregnancy
Unless emergency treatment is warranted, it is
advisable to defer elective  treatment, especially during
the first trimester, because of potential vulnerability
of the fetus. The second trimester is the safest period
during which any procedure can be carried out.
Treatment planning should be directed at eliminating
pain symptoms during pregnancy.
The postpartum period, wherein the mother breast-
feed the child, should also be considered. The clinician
should avoid any drug harmful to the infant.
Patient’s gynecologist should be consulted as regard
treatment needs of the patient and also the drug
regime.
Key points
• Proper chair position for the comfort of the patient.
• Organogenesis is taking place in first trimester; any
medication given has to be cleared by the gynecologist.
Second trimester is relatively safe. Third trimester is
also safe but care has to taken with chair position as
the fetus is growing and postural hypotension may
develop.
• To prevent supine hypotensive syndrome in the
dental chair, the pregnant woman should have the
right hip elevated (10 to 12 cm) or placing the patient
in a 5.0 to 15% tilt on her left side can relieve pressure
on the inferior vena cava. If hypotension is still not
relieved, a full left lateral position may be helpful.
c. Oral Cancer
Many a times, the periapical radiolucency can be due
to malignant lesions of the jaws.  Whenever, there is a
discrepancy between initial diagnosis and the clinical
findings, the patient should be referred for second
opinion. Patient undergoing chemotherapy and/or
radiation to the head and neck may have impaired
healing responses.
To minimize risk of osteoradionecrosis, no
treatment is advised two weeks before radiation
therapy. Prophylactic use of antifungal drugs can
help prevent candidiasis. Glass-ionomer is effective
in restoring caries due to radiations.
d. Renal Diseases (Dialysis)
Patient with renal failure and other kidney dysfunction
may not be able to metabolize certain drugs.
Hemodialysis tends to aggravate bleeding tendencies
through physical destruction of platelets.  The patients,
who have had hemodialysis session, generally have
bleeding tendencies and are fatigued. The elective
treatment should be postponed till a day after
hemodialysis. The operator should also be aware of
the fact that hemodialysis removes certain drugs from
the circulating blood and can shorten the effect of
prescribed medications. The drugs used, if any, may
have to be avoided or their doses are to be adjusted.
Penicillin V and cephalexin requires increase in dosage;
however, erythromycin requires no adjustment. The
patient’s physician should be consulted as regard
specific drug requirements.

MANAGEMENT OF COMMON
ORAL DISEASES IN ELDERLY
The following features are important for management
of common oral diseases in elderly patients.
• Educating the need of oral health on periodic basis.
Principle of Tell-Show-Do (‘Tell’, to explain; ‘show’
to demonstrate and ‘do’ to guide the individual to
practice) be communicated to the elderly.
• For patients with impaired cognitive function, due
to stroke, Alzheimer disease, etc., a family member
or a professional caregiver should be educated as
regard daily oral health needs.
• Frequent recall with fluoride application, especially
for individuals at high risk of caries; also for those
who are unable to maintain oral hygiene.
• For individuals with physical disability or limited
dexterity, improved techniques of brushing/flossing
(electric brush, water flossing) can be suggested.
• For individuals with dry mouth, agents which
relieve dry mouth can be advised (sugar-free
lozenges or xylol-containing lozenges, alcohol-free
mouthrinses, gums, etc.). Encourage them to
hydrate with water frequently.
• Periodic monitoring by dental professional is
mandatory, especially for homebound/bedridden
patients.
I. Dental Caries
It is established that incidence of coronal caries in the
old patients is more or less similar to the young;
whereas, the incidence of root caries is much higher
(around 40–70%). It  has been  recognized  that in  the
elderly, proportion of secondary caries predominates
over primary caries.
It is imperative to evaluate the risk factors involved
in planning treatment of caries for the elderly. A
thorough history of medical conditions and medication
has to be analyzed. Treatment can be instituted in two
phases:
a. Restorative phase
b. Maintenance phase
a. Restorative Phase
The following features are important during
restoration of teeth in elderly:
i. Restorative considerations for coronal caries: In
older adults, indirect restorations are generally not
preferred. Direct restorations can be readily
modified, repaired or replaced as incidence of
secondary caries is high in such patients. Owing
to high incidence of secondary caries, frequent
maintenance of these restorations might be
required, which is difficult in indirect restorations.
ii. Restorative considerations for root caries: Root
carious lesions are predominantly seen on the
proximal surfaces, followed by the facial surface.
The rapid progression coupled with the thinness
of tooth structure on the root may lead to pulp
exposure. Therefore, even a shallow lesion should
be considered potentially deep. Billings, et al
(1984) had categorized root caries into four grades
and outlined the treatment plan accordingly
(Table 27.3).
Glass-ionomer cement is the preferred restorative
material, as compared to composites (Fig. 27.4A,B).
Alternative caries management strategies have
also been suggested. Ozone has been used to
inhibit root caries. Exposure of the lesion to ozone
for 10–40 seconds is effective. Lasers have also
been tried, especially for those who cannot tolerate
local anesthetics; however, location of root caries
may cause difficulties for the accomplishment of
the procedure.
b. Maintenance Phase
All possible preventive measures need to be under-
taken to maintain restored and the unrestored teeth.
The preventive strategy remains the same with
minor modifications to suit the elderly. Daily use of
fluoride dentifrices and fluoride rinses along with
periodic topical fluoride application regime is
advisable. Fluoride varnishes may be preferred over

II. Non-Carious Tooth Tissue Loss
Tooth wear is one of the most common problems in
the older adults (Attrition, abrasion and erosion may
lead to tooth wear). One important contributing factor
is medication-induced xerostomia, which results in
loss of buffering action of saliva, making the teeth
more susceptible to acid erosion. With increasing age,
gingival recession exposes the cementum, making it
more susceptible to abrasion and erosion. Improper
use of tooth brush and tooth pick/floss, etc. can also
abrade the cemental surface.
Consequences of tooth wear include unsightly
appearance, possible development of caries in the
exposed cemental/dentinal surfaces, sensitivity and
reduction in clinical crown height. Reduction in the
height of the crowns is usually accompanied by
progressive eruption, so that teeth continue to migrate
incisally or occlusally.
The treatment for tooth wear is directed towards
eliminating etiological factors and implementing the
restorative measures. The treatment may be ‘passive’
or ‘active’.
Passive treatment: It consists of monitoring and
prevention. Periodic monitoring using study casts,
photographs, etc. helps in assessing the progress.
Preventive treatment includes dietary modifications
along with fluoride regimen. Most patients can be
successfully managed only by passive treatment.
Active treatment: It may be required in case of
sensitivity, esthetics, functional difficulty, etc.
Localized defects due to attrition, abrasion or erosion
can be restored by using composite resin or glass-
ionomers, depending on the location, occlusal load
and esthetic needs. Very few patients need advanced
rehabilitative therapy (Fig. 27.5A, B).
III. Treatment of Sensitivity
In case of sensitivity, potassium-containing desensiti-
zing toothpastes are preferred for home use; while
sodium and stannous fluorides are preferred in office
treatment ( Fig. 27.6). The dentin bonding agents may
be effective in reducing sensitivity and preventing
further damage. Root canal treatment may be carried
out as the last resort for extreme sensitivity that cannot
be treated by conservative methods.

IV. Esthetic Rehabilitation
Smile is one of the most important attributes of human
beings and is carried throughout life. Older individuals
should be encouraged to improve upon their
appearance, which minimizes psychological stress.
The teeth darken  with  age can  be because of
structural changes in the enamel, the addition of
organic material to the enamel from the environment
and due to deepening of dentinal color seen through
the progressive thinning of translucent enamel.
Pigmented foods consumed over a period of years
may even darken teeth. The esthetic treatment for
elderly includes bleaching, laminates and crowns.
Any esthetic rehabilitation should be undertaken only
after proper occlusal analysis.
V. Chipping and Cracking of Tooth Structure
Chipping and cracking of tooth structure is a common
phenomenon in the elderly. These areas take up stains
easily and appear unsightly. Occlusal and incisal
surfaces (enamel into dentin) may get worn off. The
dentinal cracks are generally painful because of their
access to the pulp. In such cases, posterior teeth may
require onlays and crowns to prevent further crack
propagation. In anterior teeth, direct composites can
be preferred, being less invasive.
VI. Candidiasis/Stomatitis
Candidiasis and denture stomatitis are common
findings in elderly. Fissures at the corners of mouth
(angular cheilitis) and petechial hemorrhage around
denture-bearing areas are prevalent in older adults.
Improving oral hygiene measures, low concentration
of mouthwashes and antifungal therapy are the
measures for management.
VII. Exposed Roots
Root exposure is universally prevalent in older
individuals. The root exposure may lead to root caries
and/or loss of cementum, leading to exposure of dentin.
It may involve pulp. Gingival recession is defined as
the apical shift of marginal gingiva from its normal
position on the crown of the tooth to the level on the
root beyond the cementum–enamel junction. The
esthetics is also compromised in case of recession
and/or root caries (Fig. 27.7A,B).
The exposed root(s) should be covered to overcome
the problem of sensitivity and to improve upon the
esthetics. The following conditions are mandatory for
successful root coverage:
• Sufficient interdental papilla adjacent to gingival
recession area
• Sufficient blood supply
• Decay/abrasion of root surface be managed prior
to covering.
(Minimum of 1.0–2.0 mm attached gingiva is
adequate for covering; inflammation be kept under
control.)
The root coverage can be managed either by surgical
or non-surgical methods.
a. Surgical methods
• Pedicle soft tissue graft
• Free soft tissue graft
• Double papillary flaps
• Coronally advanced flaps
• Connective tissue grafts
• Guided tissue regeneration procedures
b. Non-surgical methods
• Pink glass-ionomer cement (Fig. 27.8A,B)
• Gum veneers (Fig. 27.9A,B)

MEDICINES TO THE ELDERLY
The phenomena of adverse drug reactions are more
common amongst elderly. The operator should know
the status of prescribed drugs to the elderly; minimum
medication is advised. The elderly are more sensitive
