Matrices, Retainers and Wedges
Human teeth are designed in such a way that the
individual tooth contributes to their own support and
collectively support the stomatognathic system. Each
tooth is attached in the alveolar bone socket with fine
periodontal fibers. These fibers act as cushion and
help uniformly dissipating the masticatory forces
towards bony tissues. The dissipation of forces is
uniform when the teeth are in proper contact and
work in unison.
In case the contact is lost, the underlying perio-
dontal fibers and the alveolar bone may not withstand
the force, leading to periodontal problems. Lack of
stimulation and food impaction, etc. further
propagate such problem.
A clear understanding of the interproximal
relationship will help the clinician to preserve these
structures in a much better manner. To achieve an
ideal contact, a clinician should have adequate
knowledge of the ideal tooth forms. For example:
• A perfect triangular interproximal space between
two adjoining teeth. This space gradually widens
out to the labial/buccal and lingual surfaces to form
the embrasures.
• Interproximal embrasures are extending on all the
four sides, i.e. occlusal, gingival, buccal/labial and
lingual having definite shape around each contact
area.
• The base of the triangular-shaped interproximal
space is located at the alveolar border, while the
apex is at the point of contact. Reverse is true for
other embrasures, the apex is always at the contact
and the base towards the outer surface.
• Anterior teeth usually exhibit marble contacts with
less pronounced embrasures.
These ideal conditions are frequently marred by
the stresses incident to time, wear, local irritants,
configuration disturbances, and imperfectly
performed dental operations. The most important
function of proximal contact is the protection of the
interdental papillae. On anterior teeth, where the
papillae form cone-like projections, properly placed
point contacts are necessary. A broader buccolingual
contact area is required on bicuspids because the
crests of the papillae broaden out in this region.
Similarly, moving distally, the wider contact area is
required on molars because they have the wider
interproximal papillae. Improper configuration of the
proximal area may lead to:
• Displacement of teeth buccally, lingually, mesially
or distally.
• Exert a lifting force on the tooth when placed too
high occlusally.
• Disturb the axial relationship of the teeth, resulting
in trauma.
• Rotation of the teeth.
• Injury to the investing structures by excessively
opening or closing the contact and interproximal
embrasures.
• Disturbing the coordination of the inclined planes
and cusps, causing defective occlusal contacts.
• Vertical or horizontal food impaction.
TOOTH SEPARATION
Tooth separation implies separating contact of two
adjacent teeth, which can be helpful in following
situations:
• Examination of interproximal spaces
• Fine preparation of proximal cavities
• Polishing of proximal restorations
• Removal of foreign bodies, such as fruit seeds,
fragments of toothpicks, or bone sequestrums , etc.
Two methods, generally employed for accompli-
shing separation, are:
A.Slow separation
B. Rapid or immediate separation
A. Slow Separation
In this method, the teeth are slowly and gradually
forced apart inserting certain materials between them.
The advantage of slow separation is that the
repositioning occurs physiologically without injuring

B. Rapid or Immediate Separation
The rapid separator should be applied carefully to
produce desired results. Such a method is more
advantageous over slow separation method. Though,
the method is quick and useful in clinical conditions,
yet it may rupture the periodontal ligament fibers and
rapid separation may induce pain at the site. The rapid
or immediate separation is achieved following two
principles, viz. wedge principle and traction principle.
a. Separation by Wedge Principle
The separation by ‘wedge principle’ is accomplished
by insertion of a pointed wedge-shaped device
between teeth in order to create space at the contact
area. The more the wedge moves facially or lingually,
the greater will be the separation. This separation is
brought about by mechanical device (Elliot separator)
along with wedges.
i. Elliot separator: Many a times, the separation is
required for short duration. The Elliot separator,
which work on wedge principle, is effective in
such conditions (Fig. 10.1). During its application,
care must be exercised to prevent slipping. This
type of separation is useful in examining a
proximal surface or for final polishing of the
restoration.
ii. Wedges leading to separation by wedging principle
include:
• Wooden wedges
• Metal wedges
• Silver wedges
• Celluloid or plastic wedges
• Medicated wood wedges
b. Separation by Traction Principle
The separation by ‘traction principle’ is accomplished
with mechanical devices, which engage the proximal
surfaces of the teeth to be separated by means of
holding arms. These are mechanically moved apart,
creating separation between the teeth.
The separators, which work on traction principle,
are:
i. Non-interfering true separator
ii. Ferrier double bow separator
iii. Ivory adjustable separator
iv. Perry separator
v. Woodward separator
vi. Parr’s universal separator
vii.Dentatus-Nystrom separator
i. Non-interfering true separator: This device is
indicated when continuous stabilized separation
is required during the dental operation. Its
advantage is that the separation can be increased
or decreased after stabilization, and the device is
non-interfering (Fig. 10.2).
ii. Ferrier double-bow separator: With this device, the
separation is stabilized throughout the operation.
Its advantage is that the separation is shared by
the contacting teeth, and not at the expense of one
tooth, as with the non-interfering separator (Fig. 10.3).
The separators from (iii) to (vii) are not used in routine.

Requirements
The matrix should create the exact three-dimensional
contour (including the contact area) of the future
restoration. It should be immobile while the material
sets; also should not react with the material. It should
be easily removable after hardening of restorative
material without compromising the created contact,
contour and characteristics of the restoration.
Requirements of matrices
• Should be inserted easily and be sufficiently rigid.
• Should retain its contour, which can easily be
transferred to the restoration.
• Should not adhere to or react with the restorative
material.
• Should resist the condensation pressure.
Matrix Materials
The materials used as matrices include stainless steel,
cellulose acetate (cellophane), cellulose nitrate
(celluloid) and polymer materials. Matrix system is
mostly formed of two parts: A band, which is made
of metal, polymeric material or celluloid (Fig. 10.4A,
B) and a retainer. Matrices are commonly supplied as
strips of different dimensions. They may be 0.001–
0.0015 inch (0.025 mm or 0.002 mm) thick. The widths
of the matrix band, mostly marketed, are 1/4 , 3/8,
5/16 and 1/8 inch.
Objectives
The objectives of matricing are:
• Should act as a temporary wall of resistance during
introduction of the restorative material.
• Should provide shape to the restoration.
• Should confine the restoration within acceptable
physiological limits.
• Help in maintaining the dry operative field thereby
preventing contamination of the restoration.
• Assist in isolating the gingiva and rubber dam
during introduction of the restorative material.
Classification
Matrices are classified in two ways; one is based on
mode of retention and second is based on
transparency.
a. Matrices based on mode of retention
i. Mechanically retained matrices
ii. Self-retained matrices
b. Matrices based on transparency
i. Non-transparent matrices
ii. Transparent matrices

the advent of stainless steel matrices (0.002 inch
thickness) to be screwed with the help of retainers.
The disadvantages of early matrices were eliminated,

MATRICES FOR CLASS III RESTORATIONS
The matrices used for Class III restorations are usually
cellophane or mylar strips.
The strip is burnished over the end of a steel
instrument; for example, handle of a tweezer, to
produce a ‘belly’ in the strip. This will allow for a
curvature, which, if properly contoured and designed,
will reproduce the natural proximal contour of the
tooth (Fig. 10.9A and B).
In distal surface of canine, since the fixation of
retainer is difficult, a metal band is molded into ‘S’
shape and stabilized using wedges and/or impression
compound (Fig. 10.10).

WEDGES
Wedges are the third component of the matrix system.
Wedging serves the following purposes:
• Prevents surplus amalgam being forced into the
gingival crevice.
• Assists in contouring the cervical part of the
proximal surface.
• Separates the teeth to compensate for the thickness
of the matrix band, such that proximal contact is
re-established when the band is removed.
• Stabilizes the matrix.
Classification
a. Wooden or plastic wedges: Wedges are made of
wood or plastic. Wooden wedges are preferred
because:
• They are easy to trim. They adapt well to the
tooth surface.
• They remain stable during condensation.
b. Preformed or custom made wedges: Preformed
wedges are commercially available in different sizes
and are preferred because of their convenience of
use.
The requisite size of custom-made wedges may be
fabricated from tooth picks.
c. Triangular (anatomic) or round wedges: In general,
a wedge must be triangular or round in cross-
section (Fig. 10.23). The width of the base should
be slightly larger than the cervical embrasure. If
the wedge is not high enough, only point contact
between the wedge and the band is achieved. This
may lead to poor contour or displacement of the
wedge during condensation. A uniform tapering
of the wedge is needed in order to render sufficient
and even contact throughout the proximal
embrasure.
d. Opaque or transparent: Transparent (resin) wedges
can be used during placement of composite resin
restorations as they allow visible curing light to
pass through.
