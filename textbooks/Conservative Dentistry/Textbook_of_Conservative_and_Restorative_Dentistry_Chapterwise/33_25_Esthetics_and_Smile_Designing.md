The clinical practice of dentistry involves
morphological, functional and esthetical components.
Esthetics is an intellectual phenomenon, which deals
with scientific knowledge, principles and perception
of essential and natural beauty in nature and art.
Esthetic dentistry is the “art and science of dentistry
applied to create or enhance the beauty of an individual
within functional and physiological limits”. It involves
the skills and techniques used to improve the symmetry
of the teeth and face to enhance the appearance as well
as functions of teeth, oral cavity and the face.
The history of esthetic concepts dates back to more
than 4000 years. Over the years, newer materials and
techniques have revolutionized the dental restorations,
creating esthetically pleasing facial expressions.
PRINCIPLES OF ESTHETICS
The basic principles of esthetics are:
I. Composition
II. Proportion
III. Illusion
IV. Color
V. Translucency
I. Composition
Composition, the act of combining elements, can be
defined as “the relation that exists between the teeth
(when illuminated), due to attributes of contrasts in color,
lines and texture”. Any composition can be esthetic  or
non-esthetic  depending  on  how  these elements are
combined.
Various attributes of elements of composition that
impart the esthetic value are:
a. Unity or oneness: A dentition appears pleasing
when teeth are arranged in repetitions of their
shape (outline form), line (axial inclination and
position) and color. This repetitive fashion in right
and left sides in the oral cavity gives unity to the
composition.
b. Subtle variation: The unity of the composition is
not static but dynamic; nature never provides
absolute bilateral symmetry. There exist subtle
variations in right and left halves of the dentitions,
such that dynamic unity can be perceived.
c. Dominance: Dominance implies when a strong
structure in the center is surrounded by well-
demarcated structures. The centrally placed
structure dominates the visual perception.
For example, when numerous anterior natural
dentitions are viewed, then one feature that will
be dominant in all is maxillary central incisor (Color,
shape and size are the factors that control the dominance).
d. Contrast: Contrast visualizes various elements
of a composition. The eye can differentiate the
parts of an object due to contrast of colors, lines,
patterns, etc.
e. Symmetry: It is the regularity of arrangement of
objects either in horizontal/vertical direction, or
from a central point to either side, like a mirror
image (radiating symmetry). In a dentofacial
composition, radiating symmetry of the teeth is
esthetically more appealing.
II. Proportion
The  term  ‘proportion’  is  used  to  ascertain mathe-
matical representation to the relationship of the various
units in a composition.
In natural dentition, though each individual tooth
has a different shape and size, they are related to one
another and the entire dental composition relates to
face in a certain proportion, that must be pleasing and
harmonious. The proportion in esthetics is the key
tool in shaping the dental and facial components.
The routinely used ratios applied in dentistry are:
a. Golden proportion
b. Golden percentage or golden mean
c. Red proportion
d. Esthetic gauges
a. Golden Proportion
The golden proportion, also named as ‘divine
proportion’, represents the most agreeable proportion

IV. Color
Teeth are composed of multitude of colors. A gradation
of color can be perceived from gingival to incisal, with
the gingival area being darker because of thinner enamel.
The use of different shades of restorative material may
be required to esthetically restore a tooth.
V. Translucency
Translucency implies as to how deeply light penetrates
into the tooth before it gets reflected outward.
Normally light penetrates through the enamel into
dentin before it gets reflected outward. This provides
vital characteristic of normal, unrestored tooth.
Shallow penetration often results in loss of esthetics.
Illusion of translucency can also be created to enhance
the realism of restoration (addition of tints or
opacifiers).

III. Illusion
The art of creating illusion consists of changing
perception, to cause an object to appear different from
what it actually is. Teeth can be made to appear
smaller, larger, wider, narrower, shorter, longer,
younger, older, masculine or feminine. For example:
a. Illusion achieved from the principle of gradation of size:
The central incisor closest to the viewer appears
the largest; whereas, the premolar appears smaller
because they are farther away from the viewer,
even both teeth may have same occlusogingival
length.
b. Illusion achieved from light/illusion of transillumi-
nation: An object exhibits two dimensions, that is,
length and width when subjected to light. True
natural light is multi-directional and on striking
the surface of the object, also reveals texture and
shadows (This is third dimension of depth).
• The horizontal lines illuminated under light
make the object wider and vertical lines make
the object appear longer (principle of line).

below the maxillary incisal edge).

D. Gingival Tissue Analysis
a. Gingival contour: The gingival contours should be

Ideally the papillary contour should be pointed and
should fill the interdental spaces to the contact
point. An unfilled interdental space creates an
unwanted black interdental triangle in the gingival
embrasure and makes a smile less attractive.
b. Zenith point: The gingival zenith point is the most
apical point of the gingival tissues along the long
axis of the tooth (Fig. 25.8). Zenith point can
enhance:
• Preparation of tooth axis
• Length of the tooth
• Gingival shapes
Importance of zenith point
• When closing diastemas or changing the mesial
or distal tilt position of the tooth (These are
examples of moving zenith points horizontally.)
• In case where tooth needs to be shown longer or
more taper at the gingival 1/3rd, zenith point
can move apically.

F. Midline
The midline refers to a vertical line formed by the
contact of the maxillary central incisors. The midline
should be perpendicular to the incisal plane and
parallel or coincident to the midline of the face
(Fig. 25.11).
Several anatomical landmarks can be useful guides
to assess the midline of the face as it relates to dental
midline. These include:
• Midline of the nose
• Interpupillary plane
• Philtrum
• Midline of chin
(The philtrum of the lip is considered to be one of the most
accurate anatomical guides as it is always in the center of
the face.)
Minor discrepancies between facial and dental
midline are acceptable (maximum discrepancy
allowed is 2.0 mm). Location and alignment are the
two important features of midline.
• Midline should be parallel to the long axis of the
face.
• Midline should be perpendicular to the incisal
plane.
• Midline should be just straight down the papilla.
Maxillary and mandibular midlines may not
coincide (Mandibular midline is not used as reference point
for establishing maxillary midline). Maxillary midline is
more effective in smile as compared to mandibular
midline (not visible during smile).
G. Axial Inclination
Axial inclination refers to the degree of tipping any
plane of reference (Maxillary central incisors
positioned vertically/slight labially; lateral incisors’
incisal edge inclined, labially and cervical tucked in;
canines’ tips are angled lingually and cervical labially).
The interproximal area follows 50:40:30 rule in
reference to maxillary central incisor (Increasing the
contact area helps to create the illusion of longer teeth
and minimize the black triangles) (Fig. 25.12A, B).
The axial inclination of the anterior teeth tends to
incline mesially toward the midline and becomes
more pronounced from the central incisors to the
canines:
i. The axial inclination of the posterior teeth from
the frontal view exhibits the same mesial
inclination toward the midline as the cuspid.
ii. This creates a natural visual gradation, making the
teeth appear to diminish in size as they progress
posteriorly.
H. Buccal Corridor
While smiling, the areas between the corners of the
mouth and the buccal surfaces of the posterior maxillary
teeth form a space known as the ‘buccal corridor’. The
more pronounced this space becomes, the more these
posterior teeth are concealed, restricting the full
breadth of the smile. A symmetric buccal corridor is
an important element of an esthetic smile. In these
posterior segments, the artistic perception of esthetics
can be used to alter the inclinations to produce an
enhanced esthetic effect (Fig. 25.13).

2. Diastema Closure
The presence of a space, or diastema, between anterior
teeth is a common feature of adult dentitions. The
space usually distorts a pleasing smile; the observer
concentrate on the diastema, rather than overall dental
composition.
The etiological factors leading to diastema are:
i. Developmental: Microdontia, missing laterals,
mesiodens, macroglossia, fibrous frenum
ii. Pathological: Midline cysts, tumors and local
periodontal problem
iii. Neuromuscular: Oral habits, such as tongue
thrusting, swallowing or abnormal pressure
during rest.
Many forms of management can be used for diastema
closure, such as porcelain laminate/veneers, composite
veneers, and full veneer crowns (Fig. 25.16A, B).

c. Lumineers
Lumineers are made from cerinate porcelain, that is
sufficiently strong, but much thinner than traditional
veneers. Their thickness is comparable to contact
lenses; often called contact lenses for teeth. The main
advantage of the ultra-thin veneers is the minimal

grinding/cutting of tooth structure for placement.

(Fig. 25.19).
The types of laser currently used for the esthetic
dental practice include argon, carbon dioxide, diode,
Er:YAG, and Nd:YAG lasers.
1. Argon lasers: Used for curing of composite
restorations and bonding cements; transillumina-
tion of teeth for the purpose of detecting tooth
fractures and carious lesions.
2. Carbon dioxide lasers: Used for cutting and
coagulation of soft tissue, which includes gross
tissue debulking, frenectomy, gingivoplasty,
gingivectomy, etc.
3. Diode laser: Used for rapid cutting, vaporizing and
bacterial reduction adjacent to tooth structure.
4. Er:YAG laser: Used to cut soft tissue, tooth
structure/bone, bleaching of teeth, etching and for
treatment of dentin hypersensitivity.
5. Nd:YAG laser: Used as a hard and soft tissue laser
and for selective removal of initial caries with little
interaction to the surrounding healthy enamel.

Prior to undertaking any elective, ‘cosmetic’ dental
procedures, it is vital for the operator to discuss the
merits and drawbacks of all viable options. It is
important that the patient understands what the
consequences and limitations of treatment are likely
to be, and what the potential failures could entail later
in his/her life. Informed consent should be obtained
(preferably in writing) and the clinical notes and
records should be clearly documented. The operator
should be aware of the expectations of patients and be
cautious about accepting unrealistic ‘cosmetic’
expectations. The cosmetic/esthetic dental treatment
should be provided, which is minimally destructive
and, in the long term, be in the ‘best interests’ of the
patient.
The operator should keep in mind the following
ethical issues prior to performing an ‘esthetic’
treatment.
• The use of false credentials promoted by non-
accredited institutions
• Reliance on unproved science to promote treatments
• Exaggeration of clinical skills
• Unnecessary treatment procedures
• Lack of informed consent
• Harmful practices, such as, unnecessary removal
of tooth structure and the replacement with heavy
prosthesis
• Exposing  patients  to  the  unknown  risks
All dental professionals own an ethical duty to
weigh the benefits and the risks of any procedure, and
if the potential harm or risks outweigh the benefits,
even patients’ requests for treatment should be
declined.
