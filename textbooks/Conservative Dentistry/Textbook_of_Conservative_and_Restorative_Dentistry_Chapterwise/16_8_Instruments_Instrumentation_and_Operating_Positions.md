A. HAND-CUTTING INSTRUMENTS
Hand-cutting instruments are mandatory for refining

The standardization of hand-cutting instruments,
is identified by a nomenclature and number;

The proficiency and quality of the clinical treatment

Metallurgy
The hand-cutting instruments are generally
manufactured from carbon steel/stainless steel.
Carbon steel: It is an alloy made by the addition of a
small percentage of carbon to iron. There are two
varieties of steel, viz. soft and hard.
Soft steel contains 0.5% or less carbon, while hard
steel contains 0.5–1.5%, carbon. Other ingredients are
manganese 0.2%, silicone 0.2% and the rest is iron
(91.4–91.6%).
Instruments, Instrumentation
and Operating Position
In order to improve upon physical characteristics,
two heat treatments (hardening and tempering) are
given to steel.
In hardening heat treatment, steel is heated
approximately to 1500°F (815°C) in oxygen-free
environment, then immediately plunged into cold
water or oil. The hardening heat treatment hardens
the alloy but makes it brittle.
In tempering heat treatment, steel is reheated for
1 hour at 350°F (176°C) and then plunged in cold
water/slightly acidulated water/oil/mercury. Heat
tempering relieves strain, decreases brittleness and
increases toughness.

Various alloys of steel are used in manufacturing;
the main are:
a. Stainless steel: It is composed of carbon 0.6–1.0%;
chromium 18% and iron 81–81.4%. It is extremely
hard; requires careful and precise heat treatment
during manufacture. It loses a sharp edge easily as
compared to carbon steel (used for handle, shank
and part of blade).
b. Monel metal: A natural alloy of nickel, copper and
iron, Monel metal contains nickel 67%, copper 28%,
and iron 5%.
c. Nichrome: Nichrome contains nickel 60–80%,
chromium 12–20% and iron 0–2.6%.
d. Stellite: Stellite consists of cobalt 65–90%,
chromium 10–35%, with small quantities of
tungsten, molybdenum, iron or nickel. Its chief
characteristics are hardness, strength, density, high
melting point, maintenance of cutting edge and
resistance to action of acids.
e. Tarno: Tarno is made up of chromium alloy, used
mainly for manipulation of the cements.
Design Characteristics
A hand-cutting instrument consists of the following
parts:
a. Handle or shaft: It is mostly straight and octagonal
in cross-section, and may be serrated to increase
friction for hand gripping (Fig. 8.1).
Handles are available in various sizes and shapes.
Mostly handles are in a continuation of the shank.
If the shank and blade are separate from the handle
and intended to be screwed into it, the instrument
is known as a cone socket instrument. The
advantage with the cone socket instrument is that,
if the working end is broken, it may be easily
replaced and the disadvantage is its tendency to
loosen at the joint.
b. Shank: It connects the handle with the blade
(Fig. 8.1). It usually tapers from its connection with
the handle. Any angulation in the instrument can
be placed at the junction of shaft and shank.
c. Blade/nib: The working end of cutting instrument
is called the blade; whereas, working end of the
non-cutting instrument, such as condenser, is called
a nib (Fig. 8.2A to F). The working surface of nib is
called a face.
d. Cutting edge: The working part of the instrument,
usually in the form of a bevel with different shapes
(Figs 8.3 and 8.4).
e. Blade angle: The angle between the long axis of
the blade and the long axis of the shaft (Fig. 8.3).
f. Cutting edge angle: The angle between the margins
of the cutting edge and the long axis of the shaft
(Fig. 8.3).
The angle of the shank is so designed that the cutting
edge falls in the diameter of handle; feature allows
concentration of force onto the blade without any
rotation of the instrument: A few authors name it as
‘Balancing’.

Instruments, Instrumentation and Operating Position
Instrument Nomenclature
Dr GV Black established a nomenclature for hand
instruments, similar to the biological classification.
1. Order: Purpose of instrument, e.g. excavator or
scaler.
2. Suborder: Position or manner of use, e.g. push, pull.
3. Class: Form of working end, e.g. hatchet, chisel.
4. Subclass: Shape of the shank, e.g. monoangle,
binangle.
Naming the instruments moves from 4 to 1, e.g. a
binangle (subclass), hatchet (class), push (suborder),
excavator (order). In most cases, the suborder
describing the position or manner of use is variable
and non-specific; may be omitted practically.
Instrument Formula
Dr GV Black proposed an instrument formula that
describes dimension and angulation of the hand
instruments (Fig. 8.4).
The instrument formula consists of three units,
whose measurements are based upon the metric
system:
i. The 1st figure represents the width of the blade in
tenths of a millimeter.
ii. The 2nd figure represents the length of the blade
in millimeters.
iii. The 3rd figure represents the angle, which the blade
forms with the axis of the handle. This angle is
expressed in 100th of a circle or centigrades.
These three measurements are sufficient for
describing a great percentage of instruments.
However, for instruments with their cutting edges at
an angle other than a right angle to the long axis of
the blade, a fourth unit, cutting edge angle, is added
to the basic three-unit formula. This additional
number represents the angle formed between the
cutting edge and the long axis of the handle. It is
placed in the second position of the formula, i.e. before
the length of the blade. For example, a gingival margin
trimmer has a 4 unit formula (Fig. 8.4).
Many a times, manufacturers also quote their number
on the handle; this does not relate to the instrument formula.

mainly used for ‘direct cutting’.
Direct cutting instruments are made ‘left’ or

Single-plane instruments with no angle in the
shank have the potential for five cutting
movements, viz. vertical, right, left, push and
pull.
A right-left single-plane instrument with one or
more angles in the shank will have four
potential cutting movements; viz. vertical, push,
pull, right or left. For the mesially and distally
beveled single-plane instruments, the four
movements are vertical, right, left and push or
pull, depending upon the location of the bevel.
ii. Double plane instruments: The instruments
where the force is applied at a right angle to the
plane of the blade and handle (usually have a
curved blade) are called ‘double plane
instruments’ (Fig. 8.7). They have an angle or
curve in a plane perpendicular to that of the
handle. They are used mainly for ‘lateral cutting’.
Lateral cutting instruments are made ‘left’ and
‘right’, by having the curve/angle, either on the
right or left. Holding the instrument with its
blade down and away, the instrument having
curve of the blade directed to the right is a ‘right’
instrument and similarly for the ‘left’.
Single plane instruments are considered safer to use;
however, double-ended instruments are more efficient,
and also less instrument exchange is required. Blades
of different  widths can also be placed on double-ended
instruments.
iii. Contra-angling: In many instruments, shank
has bends at one or more points to angle the
blade relative to the long axis of the handle. The
extent of this arrangement depends on the
length of the blade and the degree of angulation
in the shank. Accordingly, the working point is
moved out of line with the axis of the handle. If
the working point is moved out of line with the
axis of handle, the instrument can be out of
balance in lateral cutting motion.
To avoid this, instruments are designed to have
one or more angles in the shank, placing the
working point within 3.0 mm from the axis of
the handle. This principle of design is called
contra-angling.
The length of the blade is determined by the
depth of the cavity and the blade angle is
determined by the accessibility requirements.
Greater angles are required posteriorly and in
proximal cavities of anterior teeth. Contra-
angling also provides better access of the
operating field.
iv. Single beveled instruments: Instruments which
have a bevel only on one side of the instrument
blade are known as single beveled instruments.
If the instrument is beveled on the side away
from the shaft, it is called ‘distally beveled’. If
beveled on the side of the blade towards the
shaft, it is called ‘mesially beveled’.
If the bevel is on the right side of the instrument
blade, it is a right-sided instrument and if the
bevel is on the left side, it is a left-sided
instrument.
When these types of instruments have no angle
in the shank or an angle of 12.5 centigrade or
less, they are used in push (direct cutting) and
scraping motions (chisel). If this angle in the
shank exceeds 12.5 centigrade, the instruments
could be used in pull (distally beveled) and push
(mesially beveled) motions (hoe) (Fig. 8.8A to C).
v. Bibeveled instruments: Instruments beveled on
both the sides of the blade, which meet together
to form the cutting edge are called bibeveled
instruments, e.g. ordinary hatchet (Fig. 8.13C).
vi. Triple-beveled instruments: Beveling the blade

Spoon is used for removal of decayed dentin.

excavator, except that the blade resembles a claw;
hence, the name ‘cleoid’. It is used in carving
amalgam and excavating decay from areas of
difficult access (Fig. 8.12A, B).

Advantages of hand-cutting instruments
• Self-limited in cutting enamel; do not cut sound
enamel, only enamel undermined by loss of dentin.
• Remove large pieces of undermined enamel quickly;
saving time and effort.
• No vibration or heat during cutting; painless and no
adverse effects on the tooth tissues.
• Most efficient means of precise cutting, especially
when cutting is needed adjacent to important
anatomy.
• Create smooth surface.
• Long lifespan; can be re-sharpened.
d. Miscellaneous
i. Knives: Nibs of these instruments carry knife-
edged faces on one side only. The knives are made
in various sizes and shapes (Fig. 8.19).
They are used for trimming off excess filling
material on the gingival, facial or lingual margins
of a proximal restoration or trimming and
contouring the cavosurface margins of a Class V
restoration.
The knives used for specific purposes are:
 Wilson’s knife: The nib is at perpendicular angle
to that of the shaft, so that it can be introduced
interproximally for use.
 Stein’s knife: It has a trapezoidal nib and is used
in direct gold restorations for contouring and
margination.
ii. Dental files: Nib in files can be foot-shaped,
hatchet- shaped or parallelogram-shaped with

away from the handle, it is a ‘push’ file and if the
serrations are directed towards the handle, it is a

procedures and these act as guards when thrust force
is applied.
The rests should be on the arch being operated on
and preferably on the same quadrant. The closer the
rest to the operating area, the more reliable the effects
are. In some instances, it is impossible to secure a rest
on tooth structure. In such cases, soft tissues can be
used; however, soft tissues do not provide the reliable
rest (Fig. 8.33A to F).
Guards
Guards are finger positions opposite the one using
the instrument, to steady the area being operated on.
The guards provide protection in case the instrument
accidentally slips off the working surface (Fig. 8.33A
to F).
These should be used particularly when adjacent
teeth are not available as rests or when rests are
obtained on soft tissues.
SHARPENING OF HAND INSTRUMENTS
With frequent use and sterilization, the cutting edges
lose sharpness and become dull.
Use of instruments with dull cutting edges causes
pain, prolongs operating time and reduces preciseness
in cavity preparation. Therefore, it is essential that all
cutting instruments be sharp.
Sharpening is carried out by reducing the bulk of
the metal at the cutting edge, following the original
configuration of the bevel.
There are many types of sharpening instruments,
viz. Arkansas stones, mechanical sharpener and
diamond stones (used in the handpiece).
The principles followed for sharpening are:
• Do not sharpen dirty instruments
• Establish the proper bevel angle (usually 45–60°)
and the desired angle of the cutting edge to the
blade before placing the instrument against the
stone and maintain these angles while sharpening.
• Use a light stroke or pressure to minimize frictional
heat.
• Use a rest or guide, whenever possible.
• Remove as little metal from the blade as possible.
• The unbeveled side of the blade should be finished
to remove the fine edges that may be created.
• Keep the sharpening stones clean and free of metal
cuttings.

Cutting Efficiency of a Bur
The cutting efficiency of a bur depends upon
following factors, viz:
1. Rake angle, clearance angle and blade angle: The
positive rake angle is directly proportional to the
cutting efficiency of the bur (more positive angle,
better cutting). However, a positive rake angle has
two major drawbacks: (i) It reduces the bulk of the
bur blade, as a result, the edge of blade can easily
curve, flatten or even fracture during use; (ii) A
positive rake angle produces a larger chip and
tends to clog the flute space.
The clearance angle eliminates friction between the
cutting edge and the work and prevents the bur
from digging excessively into the tooth structure.
An increase in the clearance angle, however,
reduces the blade angle, thereby decreasing the
bulk of the bur blade.
Increasing the blade angle reinforces the cutting
edge and reduces the chances of the blade edge to
fracture.
Carbide burs have blades with slightly negative
rake angle and a blade angle of approximately 90°.
Their clearance faces are usually curved to provide
a small clearance angle near the cutting edge and a
greater clearance space.

diameter will be unable to resist lateral forces. A
large neck may interfere with the visibility and
restrict access. As the head of the bur increases in
length and diameter, the movement exerted by the
lateral forces also increases and the neck needs to
be larger.

angles are preferred at high speeds as small angles

Crosscuts are notches in the blade edges to increase
the cutting effectiveness at low and medium

to reduce the total length of the bur blade that is
cutting at any one time. This increases the force

required to initiate cutting.
As each crosscut blade cuts, it leaves ridges of tooth
structure in place of the notches. Because the
notches in the two succeeding blades are not in line,
ridges left by one blade are removed by the other.
However, at high speeds, only one blade cuts
effectively, leaving behind the ridges and hence, a
rough surface.
4. Concentricity and run-out: Concentricity is a direct
measurement of the symmetry of the bur head. It
measures how closely a single circle can be passed
through the tips of all the blades. It is an indication
as to whether one blade is longer or shorter than
the others.
Run-out is a dynamic measurement of the
maximum displacement of the bur head from its
axis of rotation while the bur runs. The average
clinically acceptable run-out is 0.023 mm. Run-out
occurs, if the head is not in line with the axis of the
bur, if the bur neck is bent and if the bur is not
held straight in the handpiece. Run-out tends to
increase the vibrations of the bur and also removes
excessive amount of tooth structure. Tooth removal
with run-out is inefficient, inaccurate and heat
generating. Run-out is more important clinically
as compared to concentricity.
The primary cause of vibrations during cutting is runout; an
important feature clinically. Runout determines the
dimensions of hole vis-a-vis the diameter of the bur head. (Burs
usually cut holes larger than the head diameter.)
5. Heat treatment: Heat treatment is used to harden a
bur made of soft steel. This process preserves the
cutting edge, subsequently increasing the life of the
bur. Carbide burs do not require heat treatment.
6. Influence of load: The load is the force exerted by
the operator on the tool head and not the pressure
induced in the tooth during cutting. Load should
be in the range of 1000–1500 gm (2–3 pounds) for
low speeds and from 60–120 gm (2–4 ounces) for
high speeds. The cutting effectiveness of the bur is
reduced when the load applied is below the
minimum required and also is reduced when the
load applied is above the maximum required.
7. Influence of speed: At a given load, the rate of
cutting increases with the rotational speed but not
in direct proportion. The rate of cutting is more at
speeds above 30,000 rpm; however, it has been
found that at speeds greater than 1,50,000 rpm, the
rate of cutting is nearly the same. There is also a
minimum rotational speed for a given load below
which the tool will not cut.
8. Number of teeth or blades: The number of teeth in
a dental bur are usually limited to 6–8. Since the
load is distributed among the blades, decreasing
the number of blades increases the force on one
blade and also increases the size of the chip
removed. This tends to reduce the clogging
tendency since the flute space becomes larger.
However, a major drawback with burs having less
teeth is that the tendency for bur tooth wear is more
and the cutting life is reduced. Also, a fewer
number of bur teeth increases the tendency for
vibration.
A fissure bur with straight flutes also produces less
temperature rise than one with spiral flutes. This
may be due to the formation of large chips with
the use of a straight fluted bur. Large chips carry
away more heat as compared to smaller chips.

C. ABRASIVE INSTRUMENTS
The head of abrasive instruments consists of small
particles of a hard substance held in a matrix of softer
material. This matrix that holds the particles together
is called binder. Different materials used as binder
are ceramic, metal, rubber, shellac, etc. Rubber and
shellac wear away easily and are used for delicate
abrasion, like finishing and polishing.
Cutting occurs at a large number of points, where
the hard particles protrude from the binder. As the
instrument is used, the particles got worn off and new
particles replace the older ones, thereby providing a
continuous supply of particles. The abrasive should
be so distributed that the surface of the tool wears
away evenly. The wide spacing between the particles
prevents the packing or clogging of the resultant
debris.
Abrasive instruments can be grouped into diamond
abrasives and other abrasives. Diamond instruments
have great clinical impact in operative dentistry
because of their long life and great effectiveness in
cutting enamel and dentin. Other abrasive
instruments include use of silicon carbide, boron
carbide, aluminum oxide, garnet and sand for

a. Diamond Abrasive Instruments
Diamond abrasive instruments are preferred over
tungsten carbide burs because of their greater
resistance to abrasion, lower heat generation and
longer life. The instrument consists of three parts: A
metal blank, powdered diamond abrasive and a
binding material (Fig. 8.48).
Like other rotary instruments, it has three parts—
head, neck and shank. The shape and size of the metal
blank control the shape and size of the instrument.
The abrasive diamonds may be natural or synthetic
that are applied in multiple layers by electro-
deposition, sintering or microbrazing.
The binding agent serves the purpose of holding
the abrasive particles together as well as attaches the
particles to the metal blank. Ceramics are most
commonly used binding agents.
Classification
The diamond abrasive instruments are classified on
the basis of average particle sizes of the abrasive, viz:
• Coarse grit diamond burs (125–150  particle size)
• Medium grit diamond burs (88–125  particle size)
• Fine grit diamond burs (60–74  particle size)
• Very fine grit diamond burs (38–44  particle size)
Head Shapes and Sizes
Diamond instruments are available in a variety of
shapes and sizes that mostly correspond to the burs
(except very small numbers, as ¼, ½ round burs).
Because of abrasive coating over the shank, diamond
instruments cannot be of very small sizes as compared
to burs. However, additional number of diamond
sizes are available (Fig. 8.49).
Due to the lack of any standard nomenclature for
diamond instruments; these are selected visually by
their shape.

4. Hardness of the abrasive particles: The hardness
of the abrasive particle should be greater than the
hardness of the work; only then the abrasive indent
the surface and remove the material.
5. Clogging of the abrasive surface: Clogging of the
spaces between the abrasive particles affects
grinding, because this partially blocks the
penetration of the abrasive particles into the
surface. Clogging is enhanced when the particles
are close together. Use of coolants during grinding
may wash away the debris and prevent clogging.
The diamond points should be cleaned after every
use.
6. Speed and pressure: Proper speed and pressure are
the major factors determining the life of a diamond
point. The usual cause of failure of abrasive
instruments is when excessive pressure is applied
to increase their cutting rate at inadequate speeds.
This results in the loss of diamonds, decreasing
their cutting efficiency.
7. Miscellaneous: These include uncontrolled
operating parameters, like differences in dental
hard tissues, rotation speed, turbine air pressure,
differences in handpieces, etc.
Disposable Diamond Abrasives
The centers for disease control and prevention
recommend that all dental instruments that penetrate
soft or hard tissues or come into contact with the oral
tissues must be sterilized after each use to prevent
cross-contamination (Fig. 8.50). The “single patient
use” or disposable diamond bur is introduced that
minimizes the risk of cross-contamination of blood-
borne pathogens. Some of the disposable diamond
burs are marketed as Cobra, Monosteryl, Neo, Patriot,
SS White and Spring.
b. Other Abrasive Instruments
Many types of abrasive instruments other than the
diamonds are used in dentistry. They are used for
shaping, finishing and polishing restorations both in
the clinic and in the laboratory. The cutting surfaces
of the head are composed of abrasive particles held
in a continuous matrix of softer material. They can be
of two type—molded abrasives and the coated
abrasives.
i. Molded abrasive instruments are manufactured by
molding or pressing a uniform mixture of abrasive
and matrix around the roughened end of the shank
(Fig. 8.51A). The abrasive is distributed
throughout the matrix such that new particles are
exposed by continual wear. Rigid molded
instruments have rigid polymer (grinding and
sharpening procedures); whereas, soft molded
instruments use flexible matrix materials (used for
finishing and polishing procedures).

MECHANISM OF CUTTING
Cutting can be measured in terms of effectiveness and
efficiency. Cutting effectiveness is the rate of removal
of tooth structure in mm/min or mg/sec. Effective-
ness does not include side effects, such as heat or
noise. Cutting efficiency is the percentage of energy,
which produces cutting. Cutting efficiency is
decreased when some amount of energy is wasted as
heat or noise. Increase in rotational speed increases
both effectiveness and efficiency; but also has
disadvantages, like heat, noise and vibration.
Rotary instruments can cut by two mechanisms—
blade cutting and abrasive cutting.
Blade cutting refers to the use of a bladed instrument
or cutting by any instrument in a blade-like fashion.
For the blade to cut, it must be sharp, have higher
hardness and modulus of elasticity than the material
being cut. The chips created by cutting accumulate in
the clearance space between the flutes, until they are
washed away or removed by centrifugal force.
A lot of heat is produced with the use of these
bladed burs. Distortion of the tooth structure
produces heat. Frictional heat is also produced by the
chips rubbing against blade tip along the cut surface
of the tooth.
Abrasive cutting/grinding utilizes bonded or coated
abrasive instruments for removing small particles of
the substrate. Grinding instruments contain randomly
arranged abrasive particles that are sharp and have
negative rake angles.
When these instruments are used on the substrates,
some part may be removed as chips and some part
may flow laterally around the cutting tip forming
ridges. This type of cutting is less efficient; hence, burs
are preferred for cutting ductile materials, such as
