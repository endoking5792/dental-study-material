Laser is being widely used in dentistry. The
investigators have long been studying their effects on
oral hard and soft tissues. Food and Drug
Administration has approved the use of laser on
dental tissues.
Laser is a device that transforms light of various
frequencies into a chromatic radiation in the visible,
infrared and ultraviolet regions with all the waves in
a phase capable of mobilizing immense heat and
power when focused at a close range.
Laser is acronym for ‘light amplification by stimulated
emission of radiation’. The first laser was fabricated by
Theodore Harold Maiman in 1960 using synthetic
Ruby (aluminum oxide doped with chromium oxide).
The light emitted was 107 times stronger than the solar
light. The first lasers marketed for intraoral use were
carbon dioxide lasers.
PRINCIPLE OF LASER
If a microwave beam containing photons passes
through a cloud of ammonia gas, the gas molecules
get raised to a higher energy state as the energy is
absorbed. Earlier, Einstein observed that if a photon
of correct size struck a molecule already in an excited
state, that molecule would fall back to lower energy
level and would emit a photon of exactly the same
size and moving in the same direction as the entering
photon. Thus, in case of ammonia gas, the molecules
can undergo two possible changes, i.e. either moving
to a higher state or being pushed back to a lower one.
Normally, the first process would predominate, but
if the majority of molecules are already in a higher
energy state (termed as population inversion), then
the released photon will speed on with the original
photon. These two photons will strike two further
molecules causing production of another two photons
and a cascade reaction starts. This effect is known as
‘Maser’ (microwave amplification by stimulated
emission of radiation). This principle was applied to
the electromagnetic wavelength of light and the
process known as ‘LASER’.
LASER DEVICE
The laser device (Fig. 33.1) consists of the following:
a. Laser medium: Laser medium determines the
wavelength of the light emitted from laser. The names
of lasers are based on the active medium that is
stimulated. The active medium can be a gas [(e.g.
argon, carbon dioxide), a liquid (dyes)] or a solid
crystal rod [e.g. neodymium yttrium aluminum
garnet (Nd:YAG), erbium yttrium aluminum garnet
(Er:YAG) or a semiconductor (diode lasers)].

A population inversion happens when there are
more atoms in the excited state rather than non-
excited state. Atoms in excited state spontaneously
emit photons of light, which bounce back and forth
between the two mirrors in laser tube. As they bounce,
they strike other atoms, stimulating more photons
emissions. Photons as energy, wavelength and

laser beam. The beam is directed via a flexible fiber-

where it is focused by a lens to the focal area. Energy
impact that produces population inversion is called
‘pumping’. Gas lasers are pumped by electrode
discharge between electrodes. Laser is a beam of very
high intensity light. It is monochromatic, coherent and
collimated (directional).
• Monochromatic: Radiation in which the waves have
same wavelength.
• Coherent: The waves are in a certain phase
relationship to each other, both in space and time.
• Collimated (directional): The emitted waves have low
divergence (nearly parallel).

LASER DELIVERY SYSTEM
There are three delivery systems:
a. Articulated arms (with mirror at joints): For UV,
visible and infrared lasers.
b. Flexible hollow waveguide: A flexible hollow
waveguide or tube has an interior mirror finish.
The laser energy is reflected along this tube and it
exits through the handpiece at the surgical end with
the beam striking the tissue in a non-contact
fashion.
c. Glass fiberoptic cable: The third delivery system
is a glass fiberoptic cable. This cable can be more
pliant than the waveguide as it has a corresponding
decrease in weight and resistance to movement,
and is usually smaller in diameter (the delivery
system of choice).
LASER EMISSION MODES
The dental laser device can emit the light energy in
three modalities as a function of time:
a. Continuous (‘constant on’) emission: It is the
continuous wave in which the beam is emitted at
only one power level for as long as the operator
depresses the foot switch.
b. Pulsed (‘pushed on and off’) emission: This is a
pulse mode having periodic alterations of the laser
energy, much like a blinking light. This mode is
achieved by the opening and closing of a
mechanical shutter in front of the beam pathway
of a continuous wave emission.
c. Running pulse mode: In this mode, a large laser
energy is emitted for an extremely short span; in
microseconds followed by a relatively long span
of laser (energy being the same).

MECHANISM OF ACTION
The possible mechanisms of action of laser can be:
• Photothermal ablation: When high power lasers are
used to vaporize/coagulate tissue through
absorption (ablation).
• Photo-mechanical ablation: It causes the disruption
of tissues due to shock wave formation, cavitations,
etc.
• Photo-chemical effects: Use of light sensitive
substances to treat cancer, etc.
LASER–TISSUE INTERACTION
This is the interaction of laser photons with the
atoms/molecules of the target tissue. Lasers exhibit
four types of interactions with the target tissue,
depending on optical properties of the tissue.
The types of interactions are:
a. Photobiological interactions: The photobiological
interactions are of four types:
i. Reflection: During reflection, the incident laser
beam redirects itself without affecting the target
tissues.
ii. Absorption (absorption of laser energy by the
intended target tissue): The amount of energy that
is absorbed by the tissue depends on the tissue
characteristics, such as pigmentation and water
content; and on the laser wavelength and
emission mode. Argon has a high affinity for
melanin and hemoglobin in soft tissue.
iii. Transmission: The transmission of laser energy
through the tissues without affecting the same.
iv. Scattering: Scattering of the reflected light
weakens the intended energy; may not produce
any useful biologic effect.
b. Photochemical interactions: The basic principle of
photochemical process is that specific wavelength
of laser light is absorbed by naturally occurring
chromophores, which are able to induce certain
biochemical reactions. Photosensitive compounds,
when exposed to laser energy, can produce a single
oxygen radical for disinfection of the root canals.
c. Photothermal interactions: The radiant energy
absorbed by tissue substances are transformed into
heat energy; subsequently, produces the tissue
effects (Table 33.2).
d. Photomechanical and photoelectrical interactions:
These include photodisruption, photoplasmolysis
and photoacoustic interactions. In photoacoustic
effects, the pulse of laser energy on the dental
tissues can produce a shock wave. When this shock
wave explodes or pulverizes the tissue, it creates
an abraded crater. Photoplasmolysis implies
removal of tissues through formation of the
electrically charged ions.

ROUTINELY USED LASERS
a. Carbon Dioxide (CO2) Laser
Carbon dioxide (CO2) laser (Fig. 33.5) is considered
suitable for soft tissue surgeries, sealing of pit and
fissures, prevention of caries, erosive lesions and
bleaching etc. CO2 is mainly used for intraoral soft
tissue surgery, such as, frenectomy or removal of
hyperplasic tissue (high degree of absorption of CO2
wavelength by oral mucosal tissues; 90% water).
Absorption of this energy results in cellular rupture
and vaporization (photothermal effect). During this
process, heat is generated and conducted into
surrounding tissues; may damage the surrounding
tissues. One advantage of this thermal damage is
providing excellent hemostasis.
In case, control of the extent of lateral thermal
damage is required,  the laser application is to ‘pulse’
the laser wavelength (faster the pulse, the less time
would be available for conduction into adjacent
tissues).
Although CO2 laser is primarily absorbed by water,
it is also well absorbed by hydroxyapatite (component
of enamel). The wavelength has the higher absorption
in hydroxyapatite out of all other lasers (use as pit
and fissure sealant/caries prevention). However,
generation of high temperature is not conducive with
hard tissue preparations (tissues adjacent to surgical
site must be shielded from laser beam).
b. Argon Laser
Argon laser uses argon gas as the active medium.
Energy passing through the gas generates coherent
visible light with primary wavelengths of 488 and
514.55 nm. These visible blue-green laser light
wavelengths have peak absorption in red pigment,
such as, hemoglobin; thus, laser light is well absorbed
in pigmented tissues with abundance of hemoglobin,

mechanical bur. Gold crowns and cast fillings cannot

removal and apicoectomy because of bone healing
properties.
The thermal side effects are greatly reduced with

transmitted through optical fibers and, therefore, is
usually applied via mirror and arm delivery systems.

b. Caries Prevention (Pit and Fissure Sealants)
Dental caries is characterized by subsurface enamel
demineralization. Laser energy has the potential to
alter the enamel to make it less susceptible to this
subsurface demineralization.
CO2, Nd:YAG and excimers are commonly used.
CO2 laser is suitable since its long wavelength can
easily be absorbed by enamel.
Only the shallow depth is affected, thereby
minimizing possible harmful effects on the pulp. The
most effective wavelength for inhibiting artificial
caries was found to be 9.3 to 10.6 m.
Nd:YAG laser, when used as pit and fissure sealant,
removes inorganic and organic debris in pit and
fissure without injuring the surrounding healthy
enamel. The laser effect would block the pits/fissures.
The important features of lasers helping caries
prevention are:
i. Minimum energy is required to avoid damaging
the dental pulp.
ii. Ability to direct the laser beam within the
restricted area by means of a flexible beam guide.
Various hypotheses were put forward explaining
how lasers prevent caries.
Earlier authors opined that Nd:YAG laser may
increase the acid resistance of tooth enamel; melting
thin surface layer of enamel.
The use of laser for the prevention of dental caries
is based on reduction of micro-organisms, due to
action on plaque and also morphological alteration
of the enamel surface.
Nelson et al (1978) observed reduction of enamel
permeability and also reduction of enamel solubility.
Formation of pyrophosphate on lased enamel
reducing enamel solubility was also suggested.
Oho and Morio (1990) observed that the lased
enamel was found to have a high positive birefringence,
which might be due to presence of micropores. The
loss of water, carbonate and organic substances from
enamel might have caused formation of micropores.
The micropores were thought to act as sites for
deposition of ions released by an acid attack. During
the treatment with lactate buffer, acid solution
penetrates the enamel and calcium ions are released
by acid decalcification. In unlased enamel, released
ions diffuse to the surrounding surface. On the other
hand in lased enamel, some of released ions would
be trapped in microspaces, which lead to remineraliza-
tion; responsible for changes of birefringence.
It is established that laser acts in the following ways:
• The enamel surface gets sealed and becomes less
permeable to diffusion by ions into and out of
enamel during demineralization.
• The composition of enamel is altered (reduced
carbonate content of apatite crystals), which
reduces its solubility and permeability.
The application of laser is most effective when the
emitted laser light is matched to the absorption of
target tissue. The degree of difference between these
two wavelengths determines the amount of laser
energy, i.e. reflected or absorbed into the target tissue;
for example, when applied to enamel and dentin,
treatment of tooth is different. Enamel and dentin
have different peak absorption values of 9.6 m and
2.9 m, respectively. As a result, no single laser has
proven fully versatile when applied to oral tissues.
The routinely available lasers operate at one
predetermined wavelength that cannot be changed

teeth. The transmission wavelength of Er:YAG laser
is equal to absorption of water (2.94 m). The new

Er:YAG laser has shown promising results. It is
absorbed by water and hydroxyapatite, which
partially accounts for its efficiency in cutting enamel
and dentin.
When tooth tissue is vaporized by laser, its surface
becomes darkened due to carbonization. This can be
removed by applying a steady stream of water while
simultaneously lasing the area.
Due to alteration of surface structure, the lased
tooth becomes resistant to decay. In case of root caries,
the placement of restorative material may not be
necessary after laser application.
It is suggested that laser should be pulsed at
intervals that are shorter than the stimulation period
required to trigger nerve pulse transmission. When
cutting the vital tooth, it can be lased for about 10
seconds (Fig. 33.8A to C).
The currently used lasers use pulse duration in
seconds or now in microseconds. Even with these pulse
durations, residual temperature may increase and cause
undesirable thermal changes in underlying tissues.

• Laser-induced analgesia (no need for local anesthesia).
• Less post-restorative sensitivity.
• Access to subgingival caries is improved; lasers

Lasers occlude the dentinal tubules by producing

laser therapy induces alterations within the nerve

h. Gingival Care
The health of gingiva is mandatory during and after
restorative procedures. Low intensity lasers are
effective in maintaining and recovering healthy
gingiva. The anti-inflammatory effect of low intensity
lasers is due to increase in microcirculation, resulting
in alteration in hydrostatic pressure reducing
resorption. It has also shown effective tissue
regeneration in gingiva. A multiple application may
be required to achieve the desired results.
The anti-inflammatory and biomodulatory effects
of lasers in gingival recovery are:
• Increase in fibroblasts
• Increase in endothelial cell proliferation
• Increase in collagen synthesis; better collagen fiber
organization
• Increase in vascular flow
• Increase in growth factors
• Increase in leukocyte phagocytosis
• Decrease in crevicular fluid
• Decrease in MMP8 (metalloproteins)
i. Prevention of Erosion
Dental erosion refers to pathological loss of tissue
without the involvement of bacteria. Acidic drinks
are considered as the main causative factors, which
may be difficult to avoid, being associated with habits.
Carbon dioxide lasers have been mostly tried to
prevent erosion, due to its interaction with
hydroxyapatite. The erosive preventive effects of
carbon dioxide laser (10.6 m, 5 second pulse, 0.3 J/
cm2 density energy) have been confirmed.
j. Miscellaneous
Laser can be used in variety of other fields such as:
• Nd:YAG laser is used for welding.
• CO2, Nd:YAG, and Argon lasers are used to
sterilize instruments and to kill bacteria on culture
media, glass slides, etc.
• Exposure of dentin to laser leads to activation of
dentinogenesis.
• Laser can produce 3-D images of objects. These
images are called holograms. The study casts, etc.,
can be saved as holograms.
• Cavity treated with laser offers better adaptation
of glass-ionomer cements.
• Laser produces effective analgesia; replace the need

• Laser is effective in soft tissue management.
• Incision and drainage of abscess
• Removal/softening of gutta-percha
• Effective in biopsy excisions
• Effective in vital pulp therapy (pulp capping)
• Effective in detecting vertical root fractures.

• Laser optical tomography allows to see inside the
tooth.

cementum-like osteoconductive surface.

a. Effect on Eyes (Ocular Damage)
Ocular damage (injury to the eye) can either be by
direct emission from laser or by reflection from a

result of thermal interaction of radiant energy with
tissue proteins. Temperature elevation of 21°C above
normal body temperature can produce cell

structural proteins, which interrupts basic metabolic
processes.

c. Effect on Enamel and Dentin
The deleterious effects of lasers on enamel and dentin
are:
• Enamel exhibits gross craters (0.1–1.0 mm deep),
depending on amount of energy delivered to the
target area. In deeper penetration, dark speckling
of exposed dentin can be seen. Examination under
polarized light may show crystallographic changes.
• Dentin shows shallow and irregular craters (0.1 mm
deep). Three distinct zones of dentinal destruction
are: Central zone of complete dentinal destruction;
an immediate surrounding area of partial dentinal
destruction; and a scattered zone of dark speckling
beyond first two zones.
d. Environmental/Respiratory Hazards
Inhaled air-borne contaminants can be emitted in the
form of smoke or fume generated through thermal
effect of laser on tissue or through accidental escape
of toxic chemical and gases from laser itself.
Lasers damage functioning of the respiratory
system. It is established that detectable level of
ethanol, isopropanol, etc. found in operating laser
room are potentially damaging to the respiratory
system. Toxic gases/chemicals (fluorine, etc.) are a
common hazard in dental operatory.
Lasers and electrocautery are used to vaporize,
coagulate and cut tissues. The vapors, smoke and
particulate debris produced during these surgical
procedures are called laser plumes. The plume contains
particles with mean diameters of 0.1–0.3 μm. Within
this plume of carbonized tissue, viable tumor cells and
viral particles have been cultured. Laser plume may
contain dust, viruses, bio-aerosols, etc., which can be
hazardous to the staff working in laser clinic. These
may lead to eyes, nose and throat irritation.
e. Electrical Hazards (Shocks)
Because lasers use high currents and high voltage
power supplies, these are potentially hazardous.
Electrical hazards of lasers can be grouped as electrical
shock hazards, electrical fire hazards or explosion
hazards.
An utmost care should be exercised in installing
and using laser equipment; should be performed by
a qualified person.
f. Fire and Combustion
Laser can be hazardous, if flammable materials are
lying alongside the use. Flammable solids (papers,
cloths, etc.), liquids (acetone, ethanol, etc.) and gases
(nitrous oxide, aromatic fragrance, etc.) can be ignited,
if exposed to laser beam.

The safety requirements include:

• Use of barriers within the operatory.
• Use of eyewear to protect against reflected laser

when permissible limit exceeds.)
• Provision of warning system (audible tone or flash
light), which gets activated as the floor of laser
exceeds maximal permissible limit.
Safety measures: Points to remember
• Install laser warning signal.
• Masks, gowns, glove, etc. should be worn during laser
procedures
• Wear protective glasses: Dark green for argon and YAG
lasers; and clear for CO2 laser.
• Use wet gauze pads around the target areas
(surrounding the treated area).
• Mirrors and other reflecting instruments should be
covered; may help stray beams.
• Anesthetic gases should not be placed in the operatory;
could be ignited by a laser beam.
• The vaporized soft tissues must be suctioned off; laser
plume emitted during laser procedure  can carry
viruses. Allergy to laser plume has also been reported.
The American Society for Laser Medicine and
Surgery have suggested guidelines concerning
hazards of laser plume:
• All laser personnel should consider the laser plume
potentially hazardous both in terms of the
infectivity and allergenicity.
• Evacuator suction system with high-flow volume
should be used to collect the plume.
• Eye protection, masks, gloves, and gowns should
be worn during laser use; the eyewear protects
from splatter, the mask has effective filtration, and
the gloves should protect from microbes.
Wearing a surgical mask is essential for infection
control; however, the standard surgical mask does not
filter out particles <0.5 μm.

filter to 0.1 μm particles. There are also evacuation
