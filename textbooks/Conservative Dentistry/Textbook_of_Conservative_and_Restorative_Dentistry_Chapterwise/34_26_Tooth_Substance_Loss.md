With the dramatic evolution of dental materials
coupled with patient’s awareness regarding
preventive regimes, the individuals retaining their
original dentition is increasing day by day. With the
retention of natural dentition, the management of loss
of tooth substance is posing a challenge for the
clinicians.
Apart from caries, non-carious processes, such as
attrition, abrasion and erosion may cause loss of tooth
structure. The etiology varies among different
individuals; may be associated with physiological or
pathological processes.
Eccles suggested the term ‘tooth substance loss’, which
can be used to encompass terms, such as attrition,
abrasion and erosion. Later, Smith and Knight (1984)
proposed the term ‘tooth wear’ so as to include all
these conditions and their combination. They
contended that the term ‘tooth surface loss’ did not
sufficiently reflect the severity of the condition and
advocated use of the term ‘tooth wear’. They also
proposed a tooth wear index as depicted in Table 26.1.
Later, Bardsley et al (2004) simplified the tooth wear
index for epidemiological studies (Table 26.2).
The types of wear can be as follows:
ATTRITION
This term is derived from Latin word ‘attitum’, which
describes the action of rubbing against any object.
Dental attrition is defined as “the physiologic wearing
of teeth resulting from tooth-to-tooth contact as in
mastication. This is an age-related process that can occur at
the incisal or occlusal surfaces and sometimes on proximal
surfaces”.
Clinically, the first manifestation is the appearance
of a small polished facet on a cusp tip or ridge or an
incisal edge. Severe attrition, however, can result in
dentinal exposure, which may increase the rate of
wear (Fig. 26.1A, B).
Attrition may be hastened by a hard and abrasive
diet or inorganic (silica compound) particles in allied
food items (tobacco, etc.). However, a few authors
disagree with the food as a major factor. They prefer
to use the term ‘demastication’ to describe the wearing
away of tooth substance during mastication influenced
by the abrasiveness of the individual food particles.
Some parafunctional habits, like bruxism and
clenching, may also contribute to attrition. A good
number of individuals may be affected by such habits.
A certain amount of attrition is considered physiologic;
however, excessive destruction of tooth structure is
pathological. If occlusal wear occurs at a rate faster
than the compensatory physiologic mechanisms, it is
not ‘physiological’ (Fig. 26.2).
At times, severe occlusal wear causes amalgam/
any restoration to appear protruded, while the
surrounded occlusal surface is flattened (Fig. 26.3).

BIOCORROSION
The term ‘biocorrosion’ embraces the chemical,
biochemical, and electrochemical degradation of tooth
substance caused by endogenous/exogenous acids,
proteolytic agents as well as the piezoelectric effects.
The definition of erosion does not account for proteolysis
and piezoelectric effects, which may also be involved
in the degradation of tooth substance; better defined
by the term ‘biocorrosion’.
Biocorrosion is regarded as one of the mechanisms
for development of non-carious cervical lesions.
The term ‘erosion’ accounts for degradation of
tooth tissues only because of action of acids. Erosion
does not recognize the proteolysis and piezoelectric
effects, which are also involved in the degradation of
tooth tissues. The term ‘biocorrosion’ was conceived,
which implies chemical/biochemical/electro-
mechanical degradation of tooth tissues [chemical
exogenous agents; biochemical proteolytic enzymes
and electrochemical piezoelectric effect action on
organic (collagen) matrix of dentin].
Erosion is considered as a physical mechanism
causing wear because of friction from movement of
liquids.
Since enamel is mostly inorganic, can easily be
disintegrated by acids; whereas, dentin being mostly
organic is degraded by proteolytic enzymes (protease).
The enzyme is secreted by plaque micro-organisms
through gingival cervicular fluid. The proteolytic
enzymes may enter the oral cavity during diseases,
like, gastroesophageal reflux disease, regurgitation
and bulimia necrosa.
The collective mechanism of chemical action and
the biochemical action may be termed as ‘biocorrosion’.
(Caries is considered as microbial biocorrosion; whereas,
biocorrosion is affected by microbial invasion.)
The combined effect of biocorrosion and stress
fatigue has also been reported. It is established that
chemical and biochemical activity is accelerated during
cyclic stress.
BRUXISM
Bruxism is a parafunctional activity of the masticatory
system, which includes clenching and grinding at a
subconscious level. Bruxism may occur during sleep
or during wakefulness, commonly referred to as ‘sleep
bruxism’ and ‘awake bruxism’, respectively.
Bruxism is classified as ‘acute’, ‘subacute’ and
‘chronic’, depending upon the patient’s signs and
symptoms. Bruxism is also classified as ‘diurnal’ and
‘nocturnal’, depending upon the activity timings. It is
also referred as ‘primary’, where no systemic cause is
evident; and ‘secondary’, where neurological/
psychological factors are associated with bruxism.
Bruxism is a multifactorial condition. The most
important etiological factor is the psycho-emotional
status of the patient. However, bruxism is also related
to the local, systemic and neurological features. Local
factors include, trauma from occlusion, premature
contacts, atypical eruptions, etc. Systemic factors
include nutritional deficiency, gastrointestinal
disorders, allergic reaction, mental retardation, etc.
Neurological factors include patients with
neurological orders (neuroleptic), etc. Use of anti-
depressants can also be the cause of sleep bruxism.
The management of bruxism includes occlusal
adjustments and physiotherapy, such as, splints reduce
symptoms, and distribute the masticatory forces.

I. Carious Cervical Lesions
Caries is an important factor in the loss of tooth
structure. Cervical carious lesions are smooth surface
lesions, with their morphology similar to carious
smooth surface lesions in any other region of the tooth
(Fig. 26.7). These begin over a broad surface area and
converge towards the dentinoenamel junction. On
reaching the junction, the lesion again spreads laterally.
Depending upon the stage of progression, lesions
may present with variable morphology. Incipient
defects are seen as chalky white opacities, which
become visible only when the enamel is desiccated,
and partially or totally disappear when the surface is
hydrated (wet). These may lose their translucency
because of the extensive subsurface porosity caused
by demineralization. Their surface texture is unaltered
and routinely cannot be detected by tactile examination
with an explorer. At this stage, the lesion can be
remineralized, if adequate preventive measures are
taken. An advanced lesion is seen as a rough surface
that is softer than the unaffected normal enamel.
Further destruction of the tooth by caries results in
frank cavitated lesions. The incidence and severity of
attack increases several fold in conditions of rampant
caries or radiation-induced xerostomia.
II. Non-Carious Cervical Lesions
Many non-carious destructive processes include
attrition, abrasion, erosion, abfraction, demastication
and resorption. Amongst these, abrasion, erosion and
abfraction are the common cervical lesions. It is
emphasized to attain a detailed knowledge of the
etiology of these defects for preventing further lesions
and halting the progress of lesions already present.
Also, treatment of these lesions will be ineffective
unless the etiologic factor(s) are eliminated.
Non-carious cervical lesions, depending upon their
etiologic factors, present a variable morphology,
ranging from shallow grooves to broad dished out
lesions to large notched/wedge-shaped defects,
mostly on facial surfaces. Floor of the lesion may be
flat, rounded or sharp angled. Rounded lesions are
less frequently encountered than angular ones. The
prevalence of non-carious cervical lesions, regardless
of form and etiology, varies from 5 to 85%. Both
prevalence and severity are known to increase with
age. The older the patient population, larger is the
percentage of individuals showing lesions, greater
are the number of lesions in any one individual and
deeper is the extent of lesions. The impact of these
lesions on individual patients may also vary. Some
patients present with no symptoms, while others may
complain of highly sensitive teeth. Severe lesions may
affect the vitality of pulp and threaten the structural
integrity of the tooth.
Why Non-carious Cervical Lesions are
usually on Facial Surface?
The relative thickness of bone on the lingual or palatal
surfaces of teeth is consistently thicker than the facial
surface. The bone may deflect the load differently on
the facial surfaces than on the lingual surfaces of teeth.
This can be termed ‘osteodeflection’ (from ‘osteo’
meaning bone and ‘deflection’ being the displacement
of a structural element under load). When a lateral
force vector from a facial to a lingual direction begins
to tip the tooth lingually, the tooth cannot move
bodily due to the thickness of the bone, but rather
must bend at the fulcrum (Fig. 26.8A). In doing so, this
places the lingual cementoenamel junction (CEJ) area
under compression and the facial CEJ area under
tension. This tensile load is far more damaging than
compressive load. Conversely, if the force vector comes
from a lingual to a facial direction, the tooth can tip
bodily because of lesser bone thickness on the facial,
thus not concentrating tensile stress at the lingual CEJ
(Fig. 26.8B). Recession is more common on the facial
and is generally accompanied by loss of vertical bone
as well. This loss of bone on the facial allows the tooth
to tip bodily in a facial direction, further reducing the

A. Abrasion
Abrasion is defined as “wearing  away of the tooth
substance because of grinding, rubbing or scraping caused
by external mechanical means, like in repeated contact of
the teeth with foreign objects or substances”. Abrasion
may affect one or more teeth, or the entire dentition.
Any surface of the tooth may be affected but the most
frequently affected site is the cervical region.
The cervical abrasion lesions can be due to:
• Faulty oral hygiene practice, like horizontal
brushing, excessive forces applied during brushing,
coupled with excessive time and frequency.
• Quality of tooth brush; hardness and shape of tooth
brush bristles, flexibility and length of the tooth
brush handle affecting the grip and also pH and
amount of dentifrice used (Tooth powder is
generally five times more abrasive).
• Ill-fitting clasps of partial dentures are also known
to induce localized abrasion lesions.
• Cervical abrasive wear on the proximal surfaces
is often caused by friction from objects, such as,
toothpicks, interproximal brushes, etc.
Generally, enamel  is hard  and not  easily abraded;
therefore, it serves as a protection for the underlying
dentin, which is abraded 25 times faster.
Cementum is the softest of all tissues; shows
abrasion 35 times higher than enamel. This explains
for the higher prevalence and severity of cervical
lesions in the older age. With increasing age, gingival
recession exposes the cementum and as a result the
tooth becomes highly susceptible to abrasion even
under the previously non-damaging oral hygiene
measures.
Cervical lesions, because of abrasive forces, generally
have sharp defined margins and hard smooth surfaces
with burnished appearance. Hypersensitivity is
intermittent in character, appearing or disappearing
at intervals. In slow progressive defects, reparative
dentin formation occurs over a period of time, making
them asymptomatic. Lesions may show varying grades
of depths like: (i) Shallow (S)—0.1–0.5 mm in depth,
(ii) Deep (D)—more than 0.5 mm but no pulp exposure,
and (iii) Exposure (E)—pulp is exposed.
Abrasion lesions usually affects facial surfaces of
maxillary teeth; whereas, lingual surfaces are rarely
affected. The canines and the premolars exhibit the
highest frequency.
A survey was conducted on 460 patients who visited
Government Dental College and Hospital, Amritsar,
(India). 11,040 teeth were examined for cervical
abrasion lesions. 2760 teeth were found to be cervically
abraded and the percentage of lesions increased with
age: 9.34% (31–40 years), 30.43% (41–50 years), 42.02%
(51–60 years), and 18.18% (61–70 years). Males were
comparatively more affected than females. The
frequency with which the lesions occurred was in the
following decreasing order of the affected teeth—
upper and lower first premolars, upper canines, upper
and lower second premolars, lower canines and
incisors, and molars. Premolars were more susceptible
to abrasion, being placed slightly protruded in the
dental arch.

a. Effect of tooth brushing techniques: Brushing

opposite side of the dental arch. The sequence in
which teeth are brushed influences the location of

Classification
Abrasion lesions, on the basis of morphology and
location, may be classified as:
i. Notch (N)/V-shaped defects: Where oblique occlusal
and cervical walls intersect at a certain depth with
no definite axial wall in between them.
ii. C-shaped defects (C): Where cross-section of the
defect is C-shaped with rounded floors.
iii. Undercut concave (UC): Where occlusal and cervical
walls intersect with a definite axial wall in between
them.
iv. Divergent box (DB): Where a definite axial wall is
present with the occlusal and cervical walls
diverging toward the surface.
B. Erosion
Erosion is defined as “the defects arising because of
dissolution of tooth structure subsequent to chemical attack
of either endogenous or exogenous origin, or combined
chemico-mechanical attack.”
Eccles (1979) classified erosion lesions (Table 26.3)
into three classes.
Bartlett, et al (2008) introduced Basic Erosive Wear
Examination (BEWE) to facilitate quantifying the risk
of erosion. It is used as criteria of current indices and
allows re-analysis of results from existing studies. All
teeth, except third molars, are examined from the
buccal, occlusal, and palatal aspects for acid damage.
The most severely affected surface in a sextant is
recorded with a 4-level score (Table 26.4). The sum of
the scores defines the severity of erosive wear and
guides management of the condition (Table 26.5). The
maximum score per subject is 18. Management
includes identification and elimination of the main
causative factor(s), prevention and monitoring, as
well as symptomatic and operative intervention.

Erosion rarely operates alone in causing tooth loss.
Erosion lesion is multifactorial, i.e. once the surface of
the tooth is hypomineralized by erosion, wear
resistance of dental hard tissues is lessened and they
become more prone to damage by mechanical abrasion,
demastication (abrasiveness of individual foods),
friction from labial and buccal muscles and possibly
abfraction. It is critical to differentially diagnose the
lesion before planning treatment.

A. Determinant Factors
a. Biological factors
i. Saliva: Saliva plays an important role in
modifying the erosive effects of dietary foods and
beverages; the possible mechanisms are:
• Dilution and clearance of an erosive agent from
the oral cavity
• Neutralization and buffering of dietary acids

fluoride to eroded enamel and dentin.

erosion is expected to be higher. It has been

Clinical Implications
Erosion lesions are mostly broad, shallow, saucer-
shaped depressions present in enamel and/or dentin;
with no sharp line angles and less well-defined
margins. At times, lesions may even be grooved,
wedge shaped or irregular. When the dentin is
exposed, tubular calcification occurs along with
discoloration. Hypersensitivity may be marked in
rapid forms or in lesions where dentin is exposed with
no underlying tubular calcification. In some instances,
caries may supervene and the characteristic features
of erosion are lost.
The features of importance are:
i. Decrease in enamel microhardness: The episodic
exposure to acidic challenges decreases enamel
microhardness, which upon exposure to
mechanical forces, makes the surface more
susceptible to disruption.
ii. Formation of reactionary dentin: The dentin–pulp
complex responds by producing reactionary
dentin and occluding the dentinal tubules in order
to compensate for the tissue loss. If the progression
of dental erosion surpasses the dentin–pulp
complex reparative capacity, it may lead to
toothache, dental sensitivity and pulpal
inflammation; subsequently pulp necrosis.
How Enamel and Dentin React to
Attack by Erosive Agents
Dental erosion is believed to be a result of
demineralization of the inorganic matrix of the tooth.
Eroded enamel appears smooth and polished;
perikymata are usually absent and it may or may not
be discolored. The hydrogen ions or chelating agents
start to dissolve the enamel crystal. First, the prism
sheath area and then the prism core are dissolved,
leaving a honeycomb appearance. Then, fresh and
unionized acid will diffuse into the interprismatic
areas of enamel and further dissolve the mineral
content underneath the surface.
Usually, two erosive phases are seen on enamel.
One is ‘manifest erosion’, which appears as a hollowed
out pitted surface resembling honeycomb. The pitted
surface characterizes ends of enamel prisms that have
been dissolved below the level of interprismatic matrix.
Second is ‘latent erosion’, which is inactive stage and
the prisms are less obvious. ‘Manifest erosion’ is more
common than ‘latent erosion’ and is seen more
frequently in females and young individuals.
The initial events in dentin demineralization are
similar to those in enamel. However, because of the
high organic content of dentin, diffusion of the
demineralizing agents are partially stopped by the
organic matrix, which acts as a barrier to acid diffusion.
The erosive process in dentin is not affected by this
phenomenon; the dentin substrate is more susceptible
to acid dissolution because its hydroxyapatite crystals
are smaller than that of enamel. Peritubular and
intertubular dentin may also be demineralized laterally
along the open tubules.

How Abfraction Lesions Develop
The masticatory system during function exposes the
teeth to three types of stresses: compressive, tensile
and shear. Both dentin and enamel have high
compressive strengths; however, weak in tension
(Enamel is 35 times stronger, and dentin is 7 times
stronger in compression than in tension). Dentin is
substantially stronger than enamel in counteracting
tensile stresses. The high resiliency and elasticity
enables it to withstand greater deformation without
fracture. Enamel is made up of three components: A
mineral component hydroxyapatite in enamel rods,
an organic matrix and water (free or bound). The
ability of enamel to withstand stresses depends
significantly on the direction of forces in respect to
orientation of enamel rods. Forces are best withstood
when they are in line with the rods; whereas, under
tensile forces that tend to pull the rods away from each
other, enamel surrenders. There occurs disruption of
bonds between hydroxyapatite crystals. As bond is
broken, small intercrystalline spaces are created, which
allow penetration of water and other molecules. The
disrupted crystalline structure is susceptible to
chemical dissolution and breakage from physical
forces, like brushing, compression, tension,
mastication and bruxism (Fig. 26.15).

• A wedge-shaped lesion with sharp line angles.

contact plane, and the distance of any one point of
force from the fulcrum is different from any other
point in the plane. This exposes a large area of the
tooth to tensile stresses and the lesion so formed is
dictated by the area of contact.

MEASURING TOOTH SUBSTANCE LOSS
The methods and the instruments measuring tooth
wear are as follows:
a. Measurement in vitro
Since simulating oral environment is difficult, most of
the experiments have been carried out in vitro. The
in vitro examination has the advantage, such as variables,
like exposure time and nature of the agent can be
controlled.
The in vitro methods for measuring the tooth wear
are:
i. Polarized light microscopy: Direct viewing under
polarized light is helpful; trends of tooth wear can
be ascertained.
ii. Microhardness: The technique assesses the
hardness of the tooth before and after the erosion.
This hardness data is qualitative and may not
measure the exact extent of the tooth tissues loss.
iii. Surface profilometry: The loss of the tooth
substance is measured by the surface profilometry
by driving the spherical diamond tip onto the
specimen. This method can measure actual depth
of the lesion and also the dissolution of enamel.
iv. Microradiography: The subsurface demineraliza-
tion of the area along with total mineral loss is
being measured by microradiography, which is
superior to surface profilometry.
v. Digital image analysis: The digital image analysis
is useful in calculating the damage to the enamel.
This technique is precise and sensitive and can be
used both qualitatively and quantitatively. This
technique measures the three dimensional loss of
the tooth structure.
vi. Scanning electron microscope: The casts of the
abraded or attrited  teeth  are examined under the
scanning electron microscope. The impressions
can also be examined by these methods. This
method is precise, but only comparisons can be
carried out at various intervals of time.
b. Measurement in vivo
The in vivo measurement of tooth substance loss
is difficult, since the wear is different at different
locations.
Certain indices may provide an idea of tooth wear;
tooth wear indices are available to quantify tooth
wear.
In vivo measurement can be indirectly carried out
using study casts after silicone impressions. Study
casts monitor the progress of tooth wear. Practically,
tooth wear is measured using in vitro methods.

TREATMENT OF TOOTH SUBSTANCE LOSS
A. Treatment of Carious Cervical Lesions
Smooth surface lesions in the cervical portion of the
teeth can be overlooked in a routine clinical
examination, unless the teeth are isolated and dried
and are free of debris. Radiographic analysis also
provides little benefit. The depth of the lesion cannot
be ascertained on the radiograph; also the radiolucency
superimposes on the pulp, mimicking the appearance
of a pulpal exposure. Management of cervical caries
involves prevention and restoration. If caries is
superficial, restrict to preventive measures; otherwise
restoration.
a. Preventive Protocol
The preventive protocol includes:
i. Diet modification: The sucrose consumption should
be limited and in-between snacks be avoided.
ii. Shift oral microbial flora from pathogenic to non-
pathogenic state by using bactericidal mouth rinses
like, chlorhexidine, topical fluoride applications,
etc.
iii. Periodontal treatment is carried out to improve upon
the gingival health. Oral hygiene should be
maintained.
iv. Modification of the tooth surface: The resistance to
demineralization is achieved by application of
topical fluorides. Their continued use prevents
development of new lesions and allows
remineralization of initial lesions.
v. Stimulating salivary flow facilitates, clearing away
of substrate/acids and promote buffering, etc. It
is carried out by consuming food stuffs that
require a lot of chewing, or chewing sugarless
gum, etc.
vi. Application of self-assembling peptide P11-4 (curodent
repair) has shown significant improvement in the
prevention of erosion from acid challenges. P11-4
can promote remineralization, effectively
preventing erosive lesions.
b. Restorative Aspects
In case of rampant caries and radiation-induced
xerostomia, etc. where multiple acute caries lesions
are rapidly progressing, treatment needs to be initiated
on an emergency basis. This includes removing
infected tooth structure, and restoring the defects
with temporary restorations. Most of the infecting
organisms are removed with this technique and further
lesion formation is prevented. The emergency regime
must be accompanied by preventive measures, which
further reduce and prevent micro-organism build up.
Strict dietary counseling and intensive oral hygiene
measures should be followed. The conventional caries
lesions are restored with appropriate materials.
B. Treatment of Non-Carious Cervical Lesions
A detailed history is mandatory comprising of the
following aspects: Dietary habits, gastric disturbances,
acidic mouth taste, drugs, radiotherapy, salivary gland
dysfunction, work-related exposure to acidic
environments, parafunctional habits, oral hygiene
measures, etc. Occlusion should be checked and a
record of the study models made to monitor
progression of lesions. After a thorough evaluation,
management is carried out in two phases, i.e.
preventive and restorative.
a. Preventive Protocol
The preventive protocol includes:
i. General preventive measures: These lesions may
not be entirely prevented, but definitely reduced
by regulation of diet, and parafunctional habits.
Consumption of plain water reduces  chances of
erosion lesions.
ii. Local preventive measures
o Use soft tooth brushes and low abrasive
toothpastes, utilizing less force.
o Avoid ill-fitting metal clasps.
o Control parafunctional habits.
o Regulate frequency of consumption of acid foods
and beverages.
o ‘Drink only’; do not sip or swish acid beverages.
o Do not brush immediately following intake of
acidic drinks.
o Use neutral pH fluoride mouth rinses daily.
o Relieve traumatic occlusion
o Enhance defense mechanism of the body by
increasing salivary flow.
o Enhance acid resistance and remineralization
potential by appropriate agents.
b. Restorative Aspect
The following factors should be considered prior to
restoration:
i. Structural integrity of the tooth: If the notched/
affected area is large/deep, strength of the root at
the cervix is lessened. Bonded restoration is
indicated in such cases.
ii. Pulp protection: When the lesion is deep,
endangering the vitality of pulp, pulp capping
procedures should be carried out.
iii. Sensitivity: When sensitivity continues to exist
despite use of desensitizing agents, lesions should
be filled.
iv. Esthetics: Esthetics should be considered before
planning restoration of these lesions.

placing slight undercut in dentin can be considered.

Glass-ionomers adhere to the tooth surface

mechanical retention is not required.
Since water is an important component in ionic
exchange, the tooth should not be unduly
dehydrated.
Placement of cement under moderate pressure is
desirable to ensure optimum adaptation of the

showing  rapid initial set, and less sensitive to
moisture contamination. The restoration is
contoured and polished after one day and if
possible, after one week (Figs 26.21A, B and
26.22A, B).

Dahl Concept
It is a method of gaining space in case of localized
tooth wear, where there is insufficient space available
in either centric occlusion or centric relation. It refers
to the relative axial movement that occurs when a
localized restoration is placed in supraocclusion and
the occlusion re-establishes its full arch contacts. Dahl
and Krungstad reported that interocclusal space would
be created through a process of combined intrusion
(40%) and extrusion (60%). Most of the cases re-establish
occlusal contacts within 4–6 months; however, it may
take 18–24 months.
