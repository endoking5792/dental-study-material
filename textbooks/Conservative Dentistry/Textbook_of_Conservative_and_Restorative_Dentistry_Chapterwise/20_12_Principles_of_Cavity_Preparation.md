The most prevalent and the most common ailment of
oral cavity, the caries, is frequently confronted by all
dental professionals. Removal of caries and preparing
proper foundation (cavity preparation) for the
restorative material is the key to success for any
clinician. It is mandatory for the operator to be
thoroughly apprised with all the fundamentals of
cavity preparation.
A cavity in dentistry is defined as “a defect in enamel,
dentin or cementum resulting from the pathological
processes, mostly the dental caries”. Other processes such
as abrasion, erosion, etc. can also cause such defects.
Preparation of the cavity includes “the performance
of all mechanical procedures required to remove the carious
and affected tissues and to shape the remaining tooth tissues
so as to receive a biologically and mechanically sound
restoration”.
The preparation of a cavity should be carried out
in an orderly sequence, following certain principles.
Dr GV Black suggested six principles for cavity
preparation, which by and large are followed; though
each principle has been modified keeping in view the
advancement in restorative materials. The principles
of cavity preparation are as follows:
1. Outline form
2. Resistance and retention forms
3. Convenience form
4. Removal of remaining carious dentin
5. Finishing the enamel walls and margins
6. Toilet of the cavity
The cavity preparation is accomplished following
systematic procedures. Thorough knowledge of tooth
anatomy is mandatory to understand these principles.
The direction of enamel rods, thickness of enamel-
dentin at various places, position of the pulp and the
relation of crown with the gingival tissue must be kept
in mind to facilitate proper cavity preparation. The
physical and mechanical properties of the restorative
materials to be used should also be taken care of
during cavity preparation.
Principles of Cavity Preparation
1. OUTLINE FORM
Dr GV Black described outline form as “the area of tooth
surface or the enamel margin to be included in the finished
cavity”.
This is basically the external outline form (implies
placing the margins of the cavity to the position it
will be at the final preparation). The internal outline
form, however, includes the inner dimensions (initial
depth) of the prepared cavity.
One should visualize the outline form in one’s mind
before any tooth (cavity) preparation is started. It
prevents over-cutting and over-extension, which often
leaves weak remaining tooth structure.
General features influencing outline form are:
• Location of the carious lesion
• Extent of the carious lesion
• Position of pits and fissures
• Proximity of the lesion to other defects in the enamel
• Relationship of adjacent and opposing teeth
• Relationship of soft tissue
• Type of restorative material to be used
• Functional requirements of the restoration
• Esthetic considerations
Establishing Outline Form
a. Occlusal Cavities (Class I)
• Extend the cavity margins to sound tooth structure
• Remove all unsupported enamel (weak enamel
margins).
• Include all susceptible fissures in the outline form
(extension for prevention).
(The phenomenon of ‘extension for prevention’ was first
suggested by Marshall Ebb and was later adopted by Black).
• When two cavities have less than 1.0 mm of sound
tooth structure between them, they should be joined.
• Cavity margins should not terminate in high stress
areas, such as cusp heights or ridge crests.
• Extend the margins to allow sufficient access for
proper cavity preparation, restoration placement
and finishing procedures.

Modifications of Outline Form
The basic principles can be modified; however, these
modifications should be within limits so as the remaining
tooth structure be preserved as far as possible.
Conditions which warrant reduced extension of cavity
margins are:
• Proximal cavities (for newer materials; conservative
cavity preparation)
• Root proximities
• Esthetic requirements

Various authors have questioned the principle of
‘extension for prevention’. [(i) All pits and fissures are
involved (may and may not be carious); the cavity margins
be placed in non-fissured areas, (ii) Proximal line angle
extend buccally and lingually in ‘self-cleansing areas’,
(iii) The cervical margins extend below the gingival margin.]
It has been established that the gingival sulcus is
not bacteria free and the self-cleansing areas are
virtually non-existent. Therefore, there is judicious
need to extend the cavity margins in self cleansing
areas. In Class II amalgam preparation, the objective
of preparation of the occlusal portion is to provide
access to the proximal lesion and to remove defective
pits and fissures. Various studies have concluded that
the incidence of isthmus fracture is reduced in
conservative cavity preparations.
The concept of ‘extension for prevention’ is visualized
as ‘prevention of extension’ in the present day restorative
dentistry. The advancement of restorative materials
in the form of chemical bonding (bonding agents) and
also remineralizing agents (calcium phosphate,
fluorides, etc.) have questioned the validity of
extension for prevention.
The external and internal outline forms are to be
modified as per requirements of newer restorative
materials.
The outline form for composite restorations should
be shallow and narrow as compared to amalgam. The
low tensile strength of amalgam dictates bulk
placement, especially where heavy forces are applied.
Amalgam requires a minimal cavity depth of 2.0 mm
with an adequately wide isthmus. Composites,
because of their high tensile strength, permit a narrow
and shallow isthmus. As composites undergo
volumetric shrinkage of 1.5 to 2.0% during polymeri-
zation, a minimal cavity outline reduces the tendency
for open margins. The proximal outline is modified;
the cavosurface margins are not placed in embrasure
areas (placed within the contact area).
In composites, the enamel margins are beveled to
promote better sealing. Enamel in the proximal wall
should be beveled because prism direction is at right
angle to the cavosurface. Occlusal bevels are
unnecessary because the prism directions in the zone
of central fossa are inclined towards the fossa. It has
been shown that non-beveled occlusal surfaces
provided better sealing against microleakage in
comparison with a beveled margin.
Should bevel be placed or not has always been
controversial in composites.
Features favouring beveling are:
• The surface area is increased and the mechanical
retention is improved.
• Marginal adaptability is improved.
• The prismless layer of surface enamel is removed.
• The ends of enamel rods are exposed for better
etching.
• The color matching is improved since the transition
from tooth to composite is gradual.
Features not in favour of beveling are:
• The orientation of prisms varies in different
anatomical sites and beveling each site is difficult.
• Composite is liable to fracture, especially at stress-
bearing areas.
• Optimum strength of restoration is achieved by
providing butt joints.
The enamel margin in composite restoration should be
beveled except at the occlusal surface. By beveling labial/
lingual surfaces of anterior teeth, color matching is definitely
improved. In case of cervical lesions, bevel should be placed
only on occlusal/incisal wall and not on cervical wall.
Similarly, bevel should be avoided in cervical/gingival
cavosurface margins in proximal restorations.
Enameloplasty
It is defined as the procedure of reshaping or
recontouring the enamel surface with suitable rotary
cutting instruments to remove the shallow fissures
by rounding or saucering the enamel so that the area
becomes cleansable and finishable (Fig. 12.6A, B).
Enameloplasty does not extend to the outline form.
Indications
• If one-third or less of enamel depth is involved,
fissure may be removed by enameloplasty.
• Where the supplemental grooves extend till the
cuspal inclines.
• Shallow fissure that approaches/crosses a lingual
or facial ridge.

2. RESISTANCE AND RETENTION FORMS
These two features are interrelated and interdependent;
that is why considered simultaneously.
Resistance Form
Resistance form is defined as “the shape and configuration
of the cavity that best enables both the restoration and the tooth
to withstand occlusal forces without fracture”. The amount
of remaining tooth structure and the choice of material
influence the resistance form.
Features contributing to resistance form are:
• The cavity should be prepared as a box with a flat
floor. Flat floor resists the occlusal stresses, being
at right angle to such forces (Fig. 12.7A). Flat floor
will help prevent the movement of the restoration;
whereas, the rounded pulpal floor may allow
rocking movement producing wedging force,
which may result in shearing of tooth structure
(Fig. 12.7B). Many a times, the flat floor is
compromised at one or more than one place
because of excavation of caries (Fig. 12.8A, B). Two/
three flat seats may provide better resistance form
to the prepared cavity (Fig. 12.8C).
• The extension of the external walls of the cavity
preparation should be kept as small as possible.
When a fissure is cut, the cavity margin should
extend one-third distance from central groove to
the cusp tip (Fig. 12.9).

‘Retention form’ is defined as “the shape and
configuration of the cavity that enables the restoration to
be retained in that cavity under all types of tipping and
tilting stresses”.
Features contributing to retention form are:
• Magnitude of the occlusal forces and the area of the
restoration, which will be under the occlusal load.
• Configuration of the cavity; total surface area, (i.e.
width and depth of the cavity)
• Available height of the cavity walls
• Amount of remaining dentin

3. CONVENIENCE FORM
Convenience form implies that the operator must
create sufficient access to the lesion to facilitate
visibility and instrumentation. It is defined as ‘the
modification in shape of the cavity to have better access
to the lesion, proper manipulation during restoration
and finally for better finishing and polishing’. Mostly,
this form is required in proximal cavities, in both
anterior and posterior teeth, i.e. Class II and Class
III cavities.
Features influencing convenience form are:
• Location of the cavity margins (cavosurface
margins be related to the restorative material)
• Accessibility for caries removal
• Ease of pulp protection procedures
• Accessibility for placing restorative material
– Finishing of the cavity margins
– Starting points, if any
– Creation of other retentive means
• Type of the restorative material
• In Class II cavities, access is gained through the
marginal ridge of the respective side.
• In Class III cavities, convenience form is achieved
through lingual side, since labial enamel is to be
preserved as far as possible.
• In Class I and Class V cavities, the convenience
form is not difficult to achieve.
• Diverging of the walls of the cavity in cast
restoration is a part of convenience form so as to
have proper withdrawal of the pattern.

4. REMOVAL OF REMAINING CARIOUS DENTIN
(FAULTY RESTORATIONS AND INFECTED DENTIN)
It is defined as “the elimination of any infected carious
tooth structure or faulty restoration left in the cavity
preparation”. After preparing the cavity and achieving
adequate resistance and retention forms, the
remaining portion is checked for any left-over caries.
The site and location of such caries is important since
it will dictate the future modification of the
preparation. Caries left on the pulpal/axial wall is
excavated slowly, maintaining 0.75–1.0 mm dentin
covering the pulp. Caries will have to be excavated,
even if it leads to exposure. Pulp capping is not
required, if the remaining caries is hard, arrested or
unaffected.
In no case, soft and infected caries should be left
over the pulp. Such residual caries leads to pulpal
inflammation and subsequently slow death of pulp.
Chemical dyes, such as 0.5% basic fuchsin, may be
helpful in differentiating the infected and the affected
dentin. A few authors are also of the view that if some
micro-organisms are left untreated and the cavity is
filled, these become inactivated and get destroyed.
However, it is advisable to remove all the infected
dentin.
In case of children and young pulps, the remaining
caries along the walls of the cavity should be
excavated and the extent of such lesions is thoroughly
checked. The undermining of enamel so produced can
be treated in two ways:
i. The undermined area is filled with glass-ionomer
cement making the configuration of the walls as
required.
ii. Cuspal coverage can be considered in case of
excess undermining.
The removal of remaining restorative material
needs extra care, especially the tooth-colored
restorative materials pose problem for their complete
removal. Use of round burs at slow speed is
recommended.
Pulp Protection
The pulpal injuries are usually caused by following
factors:
• Heat generated by injudicious cutting
• Restorative materials with good thermal
conductivity
• Chemical ingredients of the restorative materials
• Galvanic currents
• Ingress of micro-organisms through microleakage
The underlying pulp is to be protected, if the
remaining dentin is less. It has been established that
2.0–2.5 mm remaining dentin is capable of countering
external stimuli, therefore, procedure for pulp
protection may not be required. In case the remaining
dentin is less, pulp is to be protected using liners and
bases depending upon the amount of dentin left and
the restorative material to be used (Fig. 12.23A, B).
‘Base’ is the material, which is applied over the
pulpal/axial wall and acts as a substitute for lost
dentin. The thickness of the base depends upon the
amount of dentin lost. The total bulk (dentin + base)
should be at least 2.0 mm. The bases provide
mechanical, chemical and thermal protection to the
pulp. The materials like zinc phosphate cements,
polycarboxylate cements, glass-ionomers, calcium
hydroxide and zinc oxide eugenol are used as bases.
In case of pulp exposure or very near to pulp

the usual cements (Fig. 12.23C, D). Recently
introduced light cure calcium hydroxide can also be
applied as a liner base.
The term ‘liner’ is used for those materials, which
can be applied in a relatively thin film. The thickness
of liners usually does not exceed 0.1 mm. Apart from
providing thermal and chemical insulation, the liners
fill the minor intricacies between the tooth and the
restorative material. All the material used for bases
can be used as liner; however, calcium hydroxide and
zinc oxide eugenol are preferred.

Stress Distribution in Individual Restorations
Class I Restoration
Class I lesion can be restored with different materials.
For restoration with amalgam, it is recommended to
converge the side walls occlusally and to keep the
floor flat. In case the floor is not flat, the forces will
rotate the restoration on both the sides. And also, if
the remaining dentin is less at the center, the forces
might fracture the tooth.
The movement/rotation of the cast restoration is
easy, if the pulpal floor is not flat. Since, the depth of
the cavity is less and the walls are diverging
occlusally, the chances of rotation are much more.
The rotational forces can be counteracted by
adhesive materials, such as composite and glass-
ionomer cement.
Class II Restoration
In Class II restorations, the stress, which tends to
rotate the restoration (bending stress), mostly acts on
marginal ridges.

line angle increases rapidly as its radius of curvature

restoration, while the other is on tooth structure, there
is tendency to wedge the two apart. To prevent this
wedging, the occlusal lock is used even though
occlusal surface is not involved by caries.
Class III and Class IV Restorations
Since these lesions are not in direct contact with
opposing teeth, only transverse stresses play part in
dislodging/rotating the restoration.
In such restorations, there is tendency to rotate
about an axis approximately parallel to the long axis
of the tooth. As incisal retention cannot be made due
to thin labiolingual size, so lingual lock is placed on
lingual surface. It should be as close to the incisal edge
as possible and still be in dentin to reduce the stress
in this lingual lock.
In maxillary teeth, force of mastication has labial
component, which provides the seating effect on the
restoration. In case the labial enamel is not intact, the
chances of dislodgement of the restoration will
increase. In mandibular teeth, the component of the
masticatory force is from the labial to the lingual, so
chances of dislodgement of restoration are more.
Class V Restoration
The Class V restoration is not subjected to direct
occlusal stresses; however, physical forces putting on
occlusal surfaces could result in displacement of the
restoration.
It is established that the buccal cusps of mandibular
teeth and the palatal cusps of maxillary teeth contact
each other during occlusion. The vertical stresses on
the teeth lead to transverse stresses and this
component of stresses tends to rotate the cervical
restoration. The stresses increase with the cusp slopes
of the maxillary teeth and the lingual inclines of the
mandibular teeth. The mandibular teeth bend more
than maxillary teeth. A gap is evident on the cervical/
occlusal wall of the cavity and if the depth of these
walls is less, the restoration may dislodge.
