With increase in life expectancy and increased
retention of natural dentition, the incidence of dentin
hypersensitivity is likely to be a cause of concern for
the dental professionals. Dentin hypersensitivity is
an exaggerated response of exposed dentin to external
stimuli, regardless of its location.
Dentin hypersensitivity is characterized by sharp
and short pain, which arises from exposed dentin in
response to a variety of stimuli, viz. thermal,
evaporative, tactile, osmotic or chemical; the pain
cannot be ascribed to any other form of dental
defect/pathology. Another term, root sensitivity, has
also been suggested for dentin hypersensitivity
arising from gingival recession in periodontal
disease and following periodontal treatment. This
group of patients may have micro-organisms
invading the root dentinal tubules, coinciding with
recession in these areas. This condition may be of
different etiology, but results in similar pain
symptoms.
INCIDENCE AND PREVALENCE
Dentin hypersensitivity is mostly prevalent between
20 and 50 years of age, with peak incidence between
30 and 40 years. Later in life, age-related changes in
pulpal processes may result in a reduction of
sensitivity, owing to reparative processes (secondary
and tertiary dentin), fibrosis in the pulp and sclerosis
of the tubules, which will decrease permeability and
reduce the hydraulic conductance of dentin. Women
are more frequently affected, may be because of better
oral hygiene and dietary practices. The reported
prevalence of dentin hypersensitivity varies from 3
to 67%; however, the most consistent figure
documented is 15%.
Dental hypersensitivity after periodontal treatment
has reported higher prevalence values, in the range
of 60–90%.
Regarding the type of teeth involved, canines and
premolars of both the arches are the most affected
teeth, especially their buccal-cervical areas.
ETIOPATHOGENESIS
Dentin is a vital tissue, consisting of dentinal tubules
along with extensions of odontoblasts, as part of
dentin–pulp complex. The odontoblastic processes are
surrounded by dentinal fluid inside the dentinal
tubules. The fluid around 22% of the total volume is
an ultrafiltrate of blood from the pulp, containing
organic proteins and inorganic salts.
The dentin hypersensitivity occurs when the
external stimulus contacts exposed dentin, triggers a
rapid outflow of dentinal fluid, and the resultant
pressure change activates interdental nerve fibers,
causing immediate pain. Tactile, cold and osmotic
stimuli, all trigger rapid fluid outflow. Heat, on the
other hand, triggers a slow retreat of dentin fluid and
the resultant pressure change activates the nerve
fibers slowly; cold is generally more problematic than
heat.
In case, the protective covering of tooth (enamel/
cementum) is lost, may be because of attrition,
abrasion, erosion or periodontal therapies; the smear
layer, if present over the eroded surface, may prevent
initiation/localization of dentin hypersensitivity,
especially in cervical areas.
A number of theories have been proposed over the
years to explain the pain mechanism of dentinal
hypersensitivity.
1. Direct Neural Stimulation
An early hypothesis suggests that dentin hyper-
sensitivity is caused by the direct stimulation of
sensory nerve endings in dentin. On the basis of
microscopic and experimental data, it seems unlikely
that neural cells exist in the sensory portion of the
outer dentin. This theory is not accepted.
2. Odontoblast Receptor Theory
The odontoblast receptor theory suggests that
odontoblasts act as receptor cells, mediating changes
in the membrane potential of the odontoblasts via

3. Hydrodynamic Theory
The hydrodynamic theory, proposed by Brannstrom,

movement of the dentinal tubule contents. An
increased inward/outward fluid flow causes a
pressure change, leading to distortion of the nerve
fibers by a mechanoreceptor action, inciting sharp,
shooting pain (Fig. 29.1A, B).
The width of the tubule is very important, as the
rate of fluid flow is dependent on the fourth power
of the radius. If the tubule diameter doubles, a 16-
fold increase in fluid flow results. Sensitive teeth have
wider (2 times) tubules at the buccal-cervical area as
compared to non-sensitive teeth. A higher velocity of
fluid flow in tubules of smaller diameter may provoke
pain sensations. Dentin will only be sensitive, if the
tubules are patent from the pulp to the oral
environment.
The trigeminal nerve supplies the pulp, with
innervations from myelinated fibers (A and A) and
non-myelinated C fibers. It is proposed that the larger
myelinated fibers (A and A) can respond to
stimuli that displace the fluid in the dentinal tubule
through a hydrodynamic mechanism, such as tactile,
evaporative, osmotic or thermal challenges, to elicit
short, sharp, stabbing pain that lasts for only a few
seconds.
Subsequently, the pain experienced with dentin
hypersensitivity can persist as a dull, throbbing ache
for variable periods of time (Both types of pain, sharp/
short and dull/long, prevalent in exposed dentin).
The status of the pulp in dentin hypersensitivity is
not known; may be minor inflammation as a result of
length of time the symptoms persist. It is hypothesized
that when the pain continues as a throbbing ache,
pulpitis can be present.
Dentin hypersensitivity results as the dentin is
exposed from either coronal or radicular regions of
the tooth.
a. Exposure of Dentin due to Loss of Cementum
Gingival recession is a common finding both in
populations with high standards of oral hygiene as
well as in populations with poor oral hygiene. The
former type of recession can be attributed to
overzealous tooth brushing and is predominantly
found on the buccal surfaces of canines and
premolars. Teeth and tooth surfaces that receive most
brushing during the brushing cycle overall show the
highest predilection for recession.
The latter type of recession is seen in periodontal
patients, during and following the treatment. These
lesions can be found anywhere around the root.
Dentin hypersensitivity can be attributed to both types
of recession. The two etiologies result in the same pain
condition; however, they should be treated
differently, especially in terms of prevention.

Enamel is highly susceptible to acid erosion, which is
probably the most aggressive type of wear compared
with abrasion and attrition. The acid may be derived

FACTORS AFFECTING HYPERSENSITIVITY
• Enamel provides greater protection of the
underlying dentinal tubules; also provides
electrical resistance.
• Cementum provides less protection. Loss of the
cementum easily occurs with use of a hard
toothbrush and/or an abrasive toothpaste, or by
root scaling and planing during periodontal
therapy.
• Salivary calcium and phosphate ions facilitate
obturation of the dentinal tubules, affecting their
permeability.
• State of the pulp may affect the sensitivity
(Inflamed pulpal tissue may result in greater
sensitivity than normal; whereas, necrotic pulp
tissue results in lower sensitivity).
• Longevity of the stimulus is an important feature
(Time required for the pulp tissues to return to
baseline values affects the tooth sensitivity).
MEASURING TOOTH HYPERSENSITIVITY
The methods used to measure tooth hypersensitivity
are as follows:
a. Tactile
The tactile method implies lightly passing a sharp
dental explorer over the sensitive area of a tooth
(usually along the cementoenamel junction). The
patient’s response is graded on a severity scale,
generally 0 to 3 (a score of ‘0’ is assigned if no pain is
felt, ‘1’ if there is slight pain or discomfort, ‘2’ if there
is severe pain, and ‘3’ if there is severe pain that lasts
for more than 10 seconds.
The force-sensitive electronic probe, used to
measure the depth of periodontal pockets at fixed
pressures, is also used to quantify the sensitivity. The
operator can vary the force applied to the tip by
regulating the amount of current and the tip position.
The probe force is controlled within ±1 gram. The
probe force can be increased in steps (say 5 gm) until
the subject experiences discomfort. That point is taken
as the pain threshold. If a maximum force of 70 grams
is reached with no discomfort, the tooth is scored as
non-sensitive.
b. Thermal
Room air is used to test the sensitivity since it is cooler
than the oral temperature. The patient will feel pain
when cool air contacts the sensitive tooth. Blowing
air on a tooth leads to drying the area, which can also
be stimulatory.
Tactile stimuli are applied before thermal stimuli,
if the two are being used in the same subject.
Testing with heat is carried out keeping the basic
temperature at 37.5°C and increasing the temperature
in increments of 1°C to the point at which pain is felt.
c. Osmotic
After isolation of the test tooth, a cotton applicator
saturated with the sucrose solution is applied to the
root surface of the tooth and allowed to remain in place
for 10 seconds. The sensation as no pain (designated as
‘0’) or pain (designated as ‘1’) is recorded. The tooth
surface is later rinsed with warm water.
d. Electrical
Electrical pulp testers are widely used to determine
the vitality of tooth. The quantification of electric
current can be used for measuring tooth sensitivity.
Certain pulp testers may determine a condition
known as pre-pain. The pre-pain sensation has been
attributed to the larger, more rapidly conducting
nerve fibers than the smaller diameter nerve fibers.
For safety reasons, any electrical current that is
applied should be limited to less than 1.0 milliampere,
preferably 0.5 milliampere (the approximate range of
the human body’s threshold of current perception).
The voltage pulses with a width of 0.05 to 0.20
millisecond each and spaced at 5 to 10 millisecond
intervals will provide a stimulus of pre-pain instead
of pain.
e. Air Indexing
A restricted air stream is directed toward the cemento-
enamel junction at approximately 45° angle for 0.5 to
1.0 second. The tip is kept at a distance of 0.5 cm from
the tooth cervix. The duration of the air exposure is
for shorter duration to avoid neurogenic inflammation
or evaporative phenomenon. The air is applied
through block hole (Different holes have different
pressures, viz. No. 1: 2–3 psi, No. 2: 4–6 psi, No. 3:
11–17 psi, No. 4: 25–30 psi and No. 5: 35–40 psi). Usually
number one hole is used to initiate the response. If no
response is elicited, then the next number is used. The
least air pressure necessary for detection of sensitive
tooth is termed as ‘threshold patient response’. These
threshold response values are recorded as none (0),
slight (1), moderate (2) or severe (3).

DIAGNOSIS
The following features are important for diagnosing
dentin hypersensitivity:
i. Diagnosis of dentin hypersensitivity should be
based upon history and clinical examination.
ii. Differential diagnosis, to exclude other conditions
giving rise to similar pain symptoms.
iii. Identification of etiologic and predisposing
factors, particularly dietary and oral hygiene
habits, pertinent to erosion and abrasion.
iv. Removal or minimizing etiologic and predisposing
factors through dietary advice and oral hygiene
instructions.
v. Recommendation of treatment should be based
upon individual needs.
vi. Treatment of all secondary condition with
symptoms similar to dentin hypersensitivity.
Differential Diagnosis
Prior to advocating treatment regimen, it is important
to exclude the conditions simulating symptoms of
dentin hypersensitivity (differential diagnosis). A
number of dental conditions can give rise to pain
symptoms similar to those of dentin hypersensitivity.
Indeed, a definitive diagnosis of dentin hyper-
sensitivity is reached through exclusion of the
following conditions, which may need different
treatment options.
• Cracked tooth syndrome, often in heavily restored
teeth.
• Incorrect placement of dentin adhesives leading to
nanoleakage.
• Fractured restorations and incorrectly placed
dentin pins.
• Pulpal response to caries and to restorative
treatment.
• Inappropriate application of medicaments during
cavity preparation.
• Restorations in traumatic occlusion.
• Palatogingival groove and other enamel
invaginations.
• Chipped teeth, exposing dentin.
• Vital bleaching.

MANAGEMENT OF HYPERSENSITIVITY
The following features are important in management
of hypersensitive dentin before initiating specific
treatment modalities.
1. Dietary Counseling
Dietary acids are capable of causing erosive loss of
tooth structure, subsequently opening the dentinal
tubules. Dietary counseling should focus on the
quantity and frequency of acid intake. It is established
that wine, citrus fruit juices, apple juice, and yogurt
were capable of dissolving the smear layer; however,
tannic acids, low-pH carbonated drinks and Coca-
Cola had no effect on smear layer. Loss of enamel is
increased when brushing is performed immediately
after exposure of the tooth to dietary acids.
2. Tooth Brushing Techniques
Incorrect tooth brushing, especially using hard
bristles, may lead to abrasion. Instruction of proper
brushing techniques can prevent loss of enamel and
the resulting hypersensitivity.
3. Plaque Control
Saliva contains calcium and phosphate ions and is able
to contribute to the formation of mineral deposits
within exposed dentinal tubules. The presence of
plaque may interfere with this process, as plaque
bacteria produce acid and dissolve any mineral
precipitates, thus opening dentinal tubules.
Effective oral hygiene measures should be
followed.
TREATMENT MODALITIES
Various desensitizing agents (Fig. 29.2) have been
used and are currently being tried to treat dentin
sensitivity. Dentin fluid flow rate is proportional to
the fourth power of the radius of dentinal tubules;
certainly of clinical relevance to the treatment of
dentin hypersensitivity.
The requisites of desensitizing agents are:
• Should provide immediate and lasting relief of pain
• Easy to apply
• Well tolerated by patients
• Should not be injurious to pulp
• Should not discolor the tooth
• Relatively inexpensive
The treatment procedures are categorized as ‘at
home’ desensitizing therapy and ‘In-office’ treatment
modalities.
A. ‘At-home’ Desensitizing Therapy
A dentifrice is defined as an agent, usually
incorporated in toothpastes, helps in cleaning the
accessible surfaces of the teeth. The agent containing
desensitizing formulation in a dentifrice can be used
for alleviating symptoms of tooth sensitivity.
Dentifrices usually contain abrasive, surfactant and
thickener, etc. Therapeutic dentifrices contain
medicament in addition to the above mentioned
ingredients.
Abrasives are solid particles that clean or polish
the tooth surface; for example, calcium phosphate,
pyrophosphate, sodium metaphosphate and alumina.
Silica is preferred because of its compatibility with
fluoride. Dentifrice abrasives are essential to prevent
tooth staining.
The taste of a dentifrice is one of the most important
factors related to the continuous use of a particular
dentifrice.
Patients using desensitizing pastes should be
advised to use minimal amount of water to prevent
the dilution of the active agent. Desensitizing mouth-
washes and chewing gums can reinforce the action
of toothpastes. The ‘At-home’ therapy must be
reviewed periodically to assess the outcome;
otherwise, patient is referred for ‘In-office’ treatment.
Toothpastes are effective, but take longer time to
achieve pain relief (average 4–8 weeks). Another
challenge with use of toothpaste is compliance, and
difficulty of delivery to specific sites, which may
encourage non-compliance.
The hypothesized mechanisms of action of
desensitizing agents are:
i. Blocking dentinal fluid movement by occluding
dentinal tubules
ii. Blocking pulpal nerves activity by altering the
excitability of sensory nerves.
Various agents used as desensitizing agents are as follows:
a. Strontium Chloride

b. Potassium Salts
Potassium salts are the most commonly used agents
in toothpastes. The mechanism of action of potassium
salts is that they can reduce the excitability of intra-
dental nerves. Raising the concentration of potassium
ion above the physiological level in the extracellular
fluid induces depolarization of the nerve cells,
following which the nerves become unresponsive to
excitatory stimuli (Calcium and strontium are also
able to suppress nerve activity, but to a much lesser
extent than potassium). The potassium ion has to
diffuse into the dentin tubules against the flow of
dentin fluid to the site of action, i.e. the nerve endings.
Further, to induce depolarization of the nerves and
achieve significant pain relief, the concentration of
potassium must be sufficient surrounding the nerve
ending, which may take 4 to 8 weeks. As the
potassium level at the site of action is decreased, the
relief of sensitivity is compromised.
c. Oxalates
The oxalate ions reacting with calcium ions in the
dentinal fluid forming insoluble calcium oxalate
crystals help in occluding dentinal tubules. However,
these are acid labile and can be easily washed from
the surface of dentin.
d. Bioactive Glasses
Bioactive glasses mainly contain silica along with
calcium, sodium and phosphorus. Silica acts as
nucleation site for precipitation of calcium and
phosphate (crystalline hydroxycarbonate apatite), a
product similar to dental apatite. This apatite has the
potential to occlude dentinal tubules. Bioglass-based
dentifrices are effective, being active for longer
duration and are also acid resistant.
e. Fluoride Salts
It has been suggested that fluoride may mechanically
block the tubules, or that labile fluoride in the organic
matrix of the dentin could block the transmission of
stimuli. Sodium fluoride is superior to sodium
monofluorophosphate as regard fluoride deposition
on the teeth.
Various other fluoride salts have been compared
and evaluated; shown their efficacy as desensitizing
agents.
f. Milk
A couple of studies have confirmed the efficacy of
commercial milk as a desensitizing agent, especially
after scaling and root planing. Milk is easily available,
cheap and acceptable agent to be used as ‘at-home’
desensitizing agent.
g. Hydroxyapatite Nanocrystals
Nanotechnology implies processing of separation and
consolidation of material by one atom or molecule (size
of particles is roughly 2–3 atoms). The toothpaste
containing carbonated nanohydroxyapatite crystals
(nanotechnology) has been successfully used to reduce
dentinal hypersensitivity. Hydroxyapatite crystals bind
to enamel and dentin, producing a biomimetic coating
on enamel/dentin. This coating prevents tooth from
decay, helps revitalize the tooth and seals open dentinal
tubules (treating dentinal hypersensitivity).
B. ‘In-office’ Treatment Modalities
The treatment modalities should primarily reduce the
functional diameter of the tubules so as to limit fluid
movement. In order to accomplish this objective, the
possible approaches are:
i. Formation of a smear layer by brushing the
exposed root surface.
ii. Topical application of agents that form insoluble
precipitates within the tubules.
iii. Impregnation of tubules with plastic resins.
iv. Application of dental bonding agents to seal off
the tubules.
Most of the desensitizing agents have the potential
to occlude dentinal tubules; however, potassium
nitrate acts by lowering the nerve sensitivity (Table 29.2).
Prior to treating sensitive root surfaces, hard or soft
deposits should be removed from the teeth. Root
planing may cause considerable discomfort;
concerned teeth should be anesthetized prior to
treatment. The teeth should be isolated and dried with
warm air. When using desensitizing agents that have
a caustic effect on soft tissue, care must be exercised
to prevent them from contacting the alveolar mucosa.

Calcium phosphate has also been successfully

iv. Sodium fluoride/sodium silicofluoride/stannous
fluoride: 2.0% sodium fluoride has been routinely
used as a desensitizing agent.
The dentinal fluid is saturated with calcium and
phosphate ions and the application of sodium
fluoride to dentin leads to precipitation of calcium
fluoride crystals, thus reducing the functional
radius of the dentinal tubules. The crystal size of
calcium fluoride is very small (approximately
0.5 m) and, therefore, less effective as compared
to potassium oxalate that gives rise to larger
crystals. Furthermore, it has been shown that
fluoride is lost rapidly following application of
sodium fluoride to dentin. Sodium silicofluoride
is more effective than sodium fluoride. 10%
solution of stannous fluoride has also been
successfully used.
d. Primers/Bonding Agents
Various primers/bonding agents are used to seal the
dentinal tubules.
The area of sensitive dentin is cleaned and etched
with an acid conditioner for 5 seconds. The dentin is
dehydrated with a continuous blast of air for at least
15 to 20 seconds in order to dry the outer part of the
dentinal tubules. A drop of a bonding agent is applied
to the dentin and cured.
A few bonding agents (Gluma desensitizer) contain
glutaraldehyde and fluorides. Glutaraldehyde causes
coagulation of the proteins inside the dentinal tubules,
causing its precipitation and occluding the tubules.
e. Iontophoresis
Iontophoresis implies use of an electrical potential to
transfer ions into the target object.
When a direct current passes through an
electrolyte, positive ions travel toward the negative
electrode (cathode) and negative ions towards the
positive electrode (anode).
Iontophoresis involves the placement of a negative
electrode to dentin and a positive electrode to the
patient’s face or arm. It is recommended that teeth
should be isolated with plastic strips or cotton rolls,
rather than with rubber dam. The moisture that
accumulates between the tooth and the rubber dam
may provide a low resistance pathway for the current.
The chemical is applied to the tooth surface and the
current (0.5 mA) is passed through negative electrode.
Potassium oxalate (30%) and ferric oxalate (6%)
solutions make available oxalate ions that react with
calcium ions in the dentinal fluid to form insoluble
calcium oxalate crystals. These crystals block the
dentinal tubules.
It is established that application of potassium
oxalate to the etched dentin also reduces sensory
nerve excitability.
f. Lasers
Lasers have provided effective treatment option for
dentin hypersensitivity.
Various types of lasers used are:
• CO2 laser (10600 nm)
• Nd:YAG laser (1064 nm)
• Er:YAG laser (2940 nm)
• He:Ne laser (632.5 nm)
• Ga AlAs laser (810 nm)
It is demonstrated that when laser light acts on cell
membrane, it allows greater passage and increase in
calcium, sodium and potassium ions. Consequently,
the endorphin system of neural cells increase and
depolarization of C-fibers is blocked (do not allow
pain information to reach central nervous system).
Most lasers occlude the dentinal tubules by
producing local changes around the exposed dentin.
Ga AlAs laser act by affecting the neural transmission
in the dentinal tubules. It is hypothesized that lasers
coagulate the proteins inside the dentinal tubules and
block the movement of fluid. It is established that
lasers are effective in reducing dentinal hyper-
sensitivity without damage to pulp or any other

recession. A routine conservative cavity is prepared
and restored with any of these materials.

2. NovaMin
NovaMin is bioactive glass ceramic material that
provides calcium and phosphate. The active
ingredient is calcium sodium phosphosilicate that
reacts when exposed to aqueous medium and
provides calcium and phosphate ions that form
hydroxy-carbonate apatite with time.
NovaMin is successfully used to treat dentinal
hypersensitivity. The calcium and phosphate ions
block the dentinal tubules, thereby reducing the flow
of dentinal fluid. NovaMin has been used with and
without fluoride (Oravive). NovaMin in combination
with 5% sodium fluoride (Renew: 5 wt% NovaMin
and 5000 ppm fluoride) is also marketed. The
manufacturers also claim its ability to whiten and
remineralize the affected dentin.
3. Casein Derivatives
Casein is the predominant phosphoprotein in bovine
milk and accounts for almost 80% of its total protein.
Under alkaline conditions, the calcium phosphate is
present as an alkaline amorphous phase along with
calcium phosphate, referred to as casein phospho-
peptide-amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP).
They have the ability to stabilize high concentrations
of calcium and phosphate in metastable solutions.
CPP-ACP is a useful cariostatic agent as it contains
calcium and phosphate in a bio-available form. CPP-
ACP has been developed as a remineralizing agent
(GC Tooth Mousse); can remineralize enamel
subsurface lesions. Because of its remineralizing
capacity, it is also recommended as desensitizing
agent.
CPP-ACP can be used as an:
• Adjunct preventive therapy to reduce caries in
high-risk patients
• Reduce dental erosion in patients with gastric
reflux
• To repair enamel involving white spot lesions
• Repair orthodontic decalcifications
