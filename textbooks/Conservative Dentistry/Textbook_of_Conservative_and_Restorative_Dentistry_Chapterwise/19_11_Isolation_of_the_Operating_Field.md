Isolation of the Operating Field
Any operative procedure necessitates the need for
adequate control over the operating field. It is
imperative that there should be proper moisture control,
good accessibility and visibility as well as adequate
space for instrumentation around the working area.
Such an environment is necessary for easy manipulation
of the restorative materials. Isolating the working area
includes isolation from moisture like saliva, blood and
gingival crevicular fluid and isolation from the soft
tissues like lips, cheeks, gingiva and tongue. A number
of methods can be employed either singly or in
combination to obtain isolated environment for the
operator. Isolation is studied under two heads:
I. Isolation from moisture
II. Isolation from the soft tissues
I. ISOLATION FROM MOISTURE
Various aids available for this purpose are:
A.Direct methods
a. Rubber dam
b. Cotton rolls and cotton roll holder
c. Gauze pieces
d. Absorbent wafers
e. Suction devices (evacuation system)
i. High volume evacuator
ii. Low volume evacuator
f. Gingival retraction cord
B. Indirect methods
a. Comfortable position of the patient and relaxed
surroundings
b. Local anesthesia
c. Drugs
i. Antisialogogues
ii. Antianxiety drugs
iii. Muscle relaxants
A. DIRECT METHODS
a. Rubber Dam
Dr SC Barnum (1864) introduced rubber dam, one of
the best methods for providing isolation from saliva
and soft tissues. Initially, the placement of rubber dam
was cumbersome; however, with the improved varieties
of rubber dam, the process is simple and effective.
Purpose
• Isolation of the operating site from moisture, i.e.
saliva, sulcular fluid and blood.
• Retraction of the soft tissue, i.e. cheeks, lips, tongue
and minimally, gingiva.
• Increases accessibility and visibility to the working
area.
• Improves efficiency of the operator (intermittent
expectoration and rinsing by the patient is avoided).
• Improves properties of the dental materials and
hence the final outcome of the restoration.
• Protection of the patient and the operator. The
patient is protected against accidental aspiration
of instruments, debris, medicaments or irrigating
solutions. Protects against soft tissue injury when
using rotary instruments. Additionally, both the
patient and the operator are protected against any
cross-infection.
• Reduces chair time for the restoration.
Use of rubber dam should be avoided in:
• Asthmatics and mouth breathers; they may not
tolerate the dam.
• Partially erupted and mal-positioned teeth; may
not receive a retainer easily.
• Psychological reasons (personal disliking).

Rubber Dam Application
The important features to be considered during
rubber dam application are:
• Isolate at least three teeth at a time (avoid single
tooth isolation).
• For working on central incisors, lateral incisors or
on mesial aspect of canines, isolate first premolar
from one side to first premolar of the opposite side.
• For working on the distal aspect of canines and
premolars, isolate two teeth posteriorly and two
anteriorly for convenience.
• For working on molars, isolate from posterior most
tooth to the premolars.
• Spacing between two holes in the dam should be
adequate (approximately ¼th''). If spacing is
inadequate, the rubber dam sheet may move to the
mesial/distal side of the papilla, thereby exposing
and injuring the gingiva. This also increases the
chances of tear of the dam. If the holes are
overspaced, rubber dam will bunch in between the
teeth thus interfering with the operative procedure.
• When cervical retainer is to be applied, the hole
for particular tooth is punched a little facial to the
arch, so as to accommodate for the extension of the
dam gingivally.
• When thin sheets are used, the holes punched
should be of the smaller size so as to obtain proper
fitting around the tooth.
The routinely used techniques for rubber dam
application are:
a. Bow technique: The bow technique can be
employed without an assistant. It provides a good
view of the oral cavity as the clamp is being placed.
A disadvantage is that the rubber must be stretched
over the wings, which may lead to its tear. The
largest punch should be used for making the hole
to avoid tearing.
b. Wing technique: In this technique, the rubber dam
is to be inserted along both wings of the clamp into
the hole punched in the dam. The preliminary steps
can be accomplished by the assistant and the
placement is completed by the operator. The
assistant inserts the selected clamp into the hole in
the rubber sheet at an angle of 45° with the bow
towards the distal, relative to the dental arch. Then
the attachment to the rubber dam frame is carried
out stretching the rubber diagonally.
The disadvantage of this technique is the reduced
visibility, especially on the more distal teeth. In
addition, care must be taken not to pinch or injure
soft tissues, such as tongue, lips, or cheeks. As the
clamp is carefully placed into undercut areas of the
tooth, the patient is instructed to signal, if there is
any discomfort in the gingiva. Once the rubber dam
is released from the wings of the clamp, the
placement is completed.
c. Rubber sheet first technique: The rubber dam clamp
and forceps should be kept ready and the rubber
sheet is stretched over the frame. A special rubber
dam is provided with frame, which has guiding
grooves for a saliva ejector at the lower right and
left borders. The next step is fitting of the clamp. If
it fits well, the rubber dam attached to frame is
carried to the oral cavity. The hole may be spread
wider with the fingers, and the rubber is pulled
down around the tooth. The clamp is then placed
over the isolated tooth (Fig. 11.14A, B).

Procedure
The stepwise procedure of rubber dam application is
as follows:
• Make the patient comfortable. Remove any debris
or calculus around the teeth before placing the
rubber dam. If rubber dam is to be placed around
a tooth, which is restored with a proximal
restoration or a crown, check for any overhanging
margins or sharp edges. These are corrected before
rubber dam placement to avoid tear of dam.
• Check for tightness of the proximal contacts by
passing the floss from buccal or lingual. Tight
proximal contacts may not allow the passage of
rubber dam; consider other forms of isolation in
such cases. However, minimally tight contacts that
are difficult to floss can be wedged apart to allow
the passage of rubber dam.
• Select a rubber dam clamp depending on the type
of the tooth to be isolated. It should be stable
without hurting the surrounding tissues and the
restoration. A clamp forceps is used to seat the
clamp onto the tooth; first on the lingual cervical
region, then onto the buccal cervical region. Before
trying the clamp, dental floss is tied around it. The
length of the floss should be sufficiently long.
Check for the stability of the clamp; slight
instability may be overcome by using impression
compound to stabilize the clamp.
• Take a rubber dam sheet. Punch a hole on its upper
right corner or mark it with 'R' for identifying the
patient’s right side. The sheet is then placed on a
template and the position of the holes marked on
it with a pen. (With a template, only fixed positions
can be obtained.) When variations are required,
manual template can be utilized.
• A lubricant is applied on both sides of the punched
hole to facilitate the passage of dam over the tooth.
The patient’s lips and the corners are also coated
with a lubricant (Fig. 11.17).
• The rubber dam is now placed on the tooth. The
placement can follow different patterns like:
i. First place wingless clamp on the tooth. Stretch
the lips of the appropriate hole in the rubber
dam sheet and then slide it over the bow and
jaws of the clamp and around the cervical of
the tooth.
ii. Place the wingless clamp and the rubber dam
together around the tooth; the rubber dam is
passed over the bow of the clamp. The clamp
forceps engages the holes in the jaws beneath the
dam and is used to place the clamp along with
the dam over the tooth. The dam is stretched and
slid over the jaws onto the cervix of the tooth.
iii. Use of winged clamp: Both the clamp and the
dam can be placed together; the rubber dam is

The placement of cotton rolls is usually carried out
by the operator’s assistant. He should continually
remove drenched cotton rolls and insert dry ones.
Loose cotton can be rolled manually into a cotton roll;
prefabricated cotton rolls are also available
(Fig. 11.19). Prefabricated rolls are more compact and
can absorb a greater amount of moisture.
They are available in varying diameters and
lengths; usually no. 2 (small) and no. 3 (medium) sizes.
The surface of the cotton roll can be smooth or woven
to improve their compactness.
Cotton rolls can be placed into position and
stabilized with cotton roll holders. The disadvantage
with the use of cotton roll holders is that they have to
be removed from the oral cavity for changing the
cotton rolls. The advantage is that they provide
slightly more retraction of the cheeks, lips and tongue;
thus, improving accessibility and visibility of the
working area.
For isolating maxillary anterior area, small-sized
rolls are placed on either side of the labial frenum.
For isolating mandibular anterior area, the cotton roll
is placed in lingual sulcus along with one roll on either
side of the mandibular labial frenum.
The maxillary posterior teeth are isolated by
inserting a cotton roll in the adjacent vestibule. This
will not occlude the parotid duct opening; hence, a
cheek pad or cellulose wafer is additionally laid over
this area of the cheek. Such placements are aided by
the use of saliva ejectors to remove saliva from the
lingual sulcus.
The mandibular posterior teeth are isolated by
inserting one cotton roll in the buccal vestibule;
usually the medium-sized roll and the larger one
between the teeth and the tongue.
In the lingual sulcus area, two cotton rolls can be
used, one inserted to the depth of the sulcus and the
other one laid above it. Cheek pads should be applied
and use of saliva ejector in the opposite lingual sulcus
aids in completing the isolation.
Avoid removing dry cotton rolls. They should be
slightly moistened before removal to prevent pulling of
the epithelial covering of the mucosa along with it.
c. Gauze Pieces
Gauze sponges are mainly supplied in pieces of 2" ×
2" (Fig. 11.20). They perform the same function as
cotton rolls and are generally used for isolating larger
areas. Additionally, they may be used as throat
screens when minute instruments are being used
without rubber dam or when indirect restorations are
being inserted, so as to avoid accidental aspiration
(Fig. 11.21). The gauze sponges are better tolerated
by the soft tissues, more acceptable and have less
chances of adhesion to dry tissues.
d. Absorbent Pads/Wafers
Absorbent pads are generally made up of cellulose;
also called cellulose wafers (Fig. 11.22). They are
available in different shapes to fit various locations
in the oral cavity. They are commonly used inside
the cheeks to cover the parotid ducts. These are more
absorbent than the cotton rolls or gauze pieces.

i. High Volume/Vacuum Evacuators
When using a high speed handpiece, both air and
water emerge from the head of the handpiece to wash
the working area and to act as a coolant for the bur
and the tooth. High volume evacuators are preferred
to remove this collected moisture and debris in the
oral cavity (Fig. 11.23). High volume evacuator has
the ability to clear 150 mL of water in approximately
one second.
The high volume evacuator tips are usually made
of disposable plastic or autoclavable metals. The tip
is usually beveled and is placed intermittently in the
oral cavity during the operative procedure. The
evacuator tip is placed as near as possible to the tooth
being prepared, but should not interfere with the
operator’s access or vision. It should not be positioned
close to the handpiece head; water supply may get
diverted away from the rotary instrument. The tip of
the evacuator should be placed distal to the tooth
being prepared. For ease of manipulation, the
assistant holds the evacuator tip in his right hand and
the air water syringe in his left hand.
Advantages
• Removes shavings of tooth and restorative material
as well as other debris from the working site.
• Toxic material is readily removed.
• Decreases treatment time as intermittent rinsing
and washing is avoided.
ii. Low Volume/Vacuum Evacuators
Low volume evacuators are basically saliva ejectors,
which are meant to remove the saliva that collects on the
floor of the oral cavity. They are available with
disposable plastic tips (Fig. 11.24) or autoclavable
metallic tips (which should have a rubber end to prevent
irritation to the soft tissue). They may be shaped by
bending with fingers and are most often used along with
cotton rolls, cheek pads and rubber dam.
Saliva ejectors should be placed with their tips,
directed backwards and not directly in contact with
the tissues. This is to prevent aspiration of the delicate
mucous membrane into the holes of the tip and getting
traumatized by the vacuum energy. When using
saliva ejector along with rubber dam, the saliva ejector
should be passed through a hole punched in the

B. INDIRECT METHODS
The above mentioned measures are helpful in
managing the moisture/saliva directly in the oral
cavity. The measures that reduce the amount of
salivation and indirectly aid in isolation are:
a. Comfortable and relaxed position of the patient:
The patient should be comfortably seated in the
dental chair. The surroundings should be pleasant
and relaxing. These features along with comforting
attitude of the dental staff reduce the anxiety levels
of the patient and aid in reducing salivation.
b. Local anesthesia: Using a local anesthetic helps in
reducing the discomfort and also the anxiety;
subsequently controlling moisture by decreasing
salivation.
The vasoconstriction caused by local anesthetic
helps in reducing hemorrhage at the operating site.
The salivary flow is reduced, helping in moisture
control.
c. Drugs: Drugs can reduce salivation, but are rarely
indicated. These include antisialogogues, anti-
anxiety agents, sedatives, etc.
i. Antisialogogues: Premedication may be indicated
using an anticholinergic agent to depress
salivation. Atropine can be given half an hour
before the appointment, but should be avoided
in patients with high ocular pressure or with
cardiovascular problems.
ii. Antianxiety agents and sedatives: Premedication
with these drugs is quite helpful in apprehensive
patients; for example, diazepam or barbiturates,
24 hours before the appointment. Because of
psychological dependence on these drugs, these
should be given only for short periods and to
selected patients.
iii. Muscle relaxants: Muscle relaxants are also
helpful.

Need for Gingival Deflection
• To provide access for impression material into the
subgingival margins, recording the finish line
properly.
• Undermine caries can be treated easily.
• The margins on the prepared tooth can be

• Helps in easy removal of cement without damaging
the gingiva.
• The marginal fit of the restoration can be assessed.
• To gain surface area, it may be necessary to extend

• Facilitates finishing and polishing of the

Indications
• Presence of subgingival caries
• Cervical abrasion and erosion
• Gingival polyp at the margins
• Subgingival finish line
• Decreased crown:root ratio
Contraindications
• Poor oral hygiene
• Gingival recession
• Bone loss
• Systemic diseases affecting gingiva
The features to be considered prior to gingival retraction

• The periodontium should be sound, especially
around the prepared tooth and the adjoining teeth.
• The crest of free gingiva should be at its normal

recession.
• Any hyperplastic/fibrosed tissue, if present, should
be treated prior to restoration.
• Crevicular fluid and bleeding should be arrested

• The retraction at the gingival crevice area should

• Gingival retraction should not cause any

• Cardiovascular disorders
• Hypertension
• Hyperthyroidism

ulceration and necrosis, when applied for
prolonged time.
iv. Concentrated zinc chloride/concentrated

zinc chloride is used to chemically cauterize the

Various types of electrosurgical electrode are used

electrosurgical actions can be:
i. Electrosectioning/cutting (precise cutting with

ii. Electrocoagulation (create coagulation of
tissues, their fluids and oozed blood)
iii. Fulgeration (involve deeper tissues, accompanied
by carbonization)
iv. Desiccation (massive tissue involvement;
uncontrolled action)

• The operational area should be moist.
• Use fully rectified, undamped current with minimum
energy output.
• Use light pressure touch and rapid deft strokes with
a five seconds lapse between two strokes (always keep
cutting electrode in the internal wall of sulcus,
maintaining biologic width).
• Metallic restorations should not be touched (can create
short circuit and damage surrounding structures).
• Always clean debris on the electrode tip with alcohol-
soaked gauge.
• After the impression/restoration procedure creates a
blood clot with curetting the area.
• Avoid electrosurgery in patients with pacemaker.

• Immediate hemostasis
• Better healing of the tissues
• Improved accuracy of the impression (margins

• Decreased chair time and stress
