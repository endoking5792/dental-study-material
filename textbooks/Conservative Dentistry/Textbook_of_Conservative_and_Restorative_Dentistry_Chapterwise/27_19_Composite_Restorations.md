Tooth-colored restorations have always been in
demand. The earlier used acrylics did not last long
because of inherent drawback of higher coefficient of
thermal expansion and higher polymerization
shrinkage. To improve upon these drawbacks, filler
particles were added to the acrylic resin matrix.
The course of inventions and developments led to
the introduction of composites by RL Bowen in 1962.
Composite is basically a botanical term, where the
clusters of flowers are clubbed giving shape to a
different flower. Metallurgically, composite is a
combination of two or more materials, having
chemically distinct interface between them.
The accepted definition is of Anusavice, which
says, “Composite material is a compound of two or more
distinctly different materials with properties that are
superior to or intermediate to those of the individual
constituent”.
Over the years, composite as a material has seen
tremendous improvement; modifying the basic
constituents and also the restorative modalities.
COMPOSITION
The components of composite are:
a. Resin matrix
b. Fillers
c. Coupling agents
d. Initiators and accelerators
e. Coloring agents
a. Resin Matrix
The initial resin matrix was bisphenol glycidyl
methacrylate (BisGMA) and urethane dimethacrylate
(UDMA).
The BisGMA/UDMA was viscous and blending of
filler particles was difficult. To overcome problem
of viscosity, other matrices were tried having
lower viscosity, viz: TEGDMA (Triethylene glycol
dimethacrylate). BisGMA and TEGDMA in the ratio
Composite Restorations
of 3:1 is preferred; increase in TEGDMA substantially
increases the polymerization shrinkage.
The polymerization shrinkage has been of concern
during composite restorations. As the material
polymerizes, it shrinks and no longer remains
attached to the cavity walls. This further creates
stresses at the tooth–restoration interface and open
the gaps; subsequently leading to debonding, cracks,
secondary caries, staining and postoperative
sensitivity.
Polymerization shrinkage leads to:
• Stresses at tooth–restoration interface
• Debonding (loss of attachment)
• Opening of gaps
• Microcracks
• Staining
• Secondary caries
• Postoperative sensitivity
Expanding matrix composites are introduced
with the idea to compensate for the polymerization
shrinkage. Spiro-orthocarbonates are the expanding
matrix used. It is preferably used with epoxy resin,
because when used with BisGMA/TEGDMA, it
could not produce the required expansion. A newer
matrix system containing a diepoxide monomer and
polyol has substantially reduced the polymerization
shrinkage.
Hydrophobic monomers are also used as matrix.
Recently, thiolene methacrylate, a low-shrink resin
matrix, is being tried. It leads to delay in conversion
and gelation. As the gelation is delayed, the impact of
stresses is reduced; subsequently, gap formation and
other deleterious effects are minimized.
A new hydrolytically stable, ether-based monomer
(triethylene glycol divinylbenzyl ether: TEG-DVBE
and urethane dimethacrylate: UDMA) has shown
substantial improvement in mechanical properties in
composites. These composites generated much less
stress than conventional resin composites.

b. Fillers
Various types of fillers, with varying sizes, have been
used in different composites. Depending upon the
size of filler particles, the composites are categorized
as follows:
• Microfill (40–50 nm)
• Medium fill (40 nm + 1.0–10 m)
• Hybrid (40 nm + 10–50 m)
• Microhybrid (40 nm + 0.6–1.0 m)
• Nanohybrid (50–100 nm + 0.6–1.0 m)
• Nanofiller (5–50 nm)
The routinely used fillers are:
i. Quartz: Quartz were used in early composites,
which were difficult to polish and even abraded
the opposing tooth structure.
ii. Silica: Pure silica, fused silica and colloidal silica
(pyrogenic and precipitated forms) have been
used. Glasses such as aluminosilicates and
borosilicates are also used.
iii. Tricalcium phosphate and zirconium dioxide
iv. Fillers containing fluoride, such as Yttrium
trifluoride and Ytterbium trifluoride
The size of filler particles has continuously
decreased from the traditional to nano-composite
materials. (Inorganic phase is nano-sized.) Nanofillers
are usually invisible and offer improvement in optical
properties. Additionally, nanofillers are capable of
increasing the overall filler load due to small particle
sizes.
Composites reinforced with 2.0–5.0% ceramic
nanofillers (zirconia–silica or zirconia–yttria–silica)
have significantly improved mechanical properties of
composites, especially the fracture toughness.
However, higher percentage of these fillers could not
show better results.
Fillers provide:
• Strength
• Rigidity
• Hardness
• Increase in modulus of elasticity
• Decrease in coefficient of thermal expansion
• Decrease in contraction
Mostly, present day composites contain combination
of macro- (barium glass, particle size 0.7–2.0 m) and
micro- (pyrogenic silica, particle size 0.04–0.07 m)
fillers.
The advantages of using these fillers are as follows:
• Better polishing
• Kindness to antagonist tooth
• Good esthetic and chameleon effect
Recently, wrinkled mesoporous silica (average
particle size 496 nm) has been added along with
smaller silica particles to achieve superior mechanical
properties.
c. Coupling Agents
The coupling agents are used to bind filler particles to
the resin matrix. Apart from binding, these agents
allow flexible polymer matrix to transfer stresses to
stiffer filler particles. The coupling agents also provide
hydrolytic stability by preventing the water from
penetrating along the filler–resin interface.
Organic silanes, such as -methacryloxypropyl-
trimethoxysilane, are commonly used as coupling
agents. (Silane molecule undergoes hydrolysis and
forms a silanol group, which forms a bond with filler
particles and carboxyl group; subsequently, facilitates
polymerization with the resin matrix.)
Presently, 10-methacryloxydecyl-trimethoxysilane
(more hydrophobic and better suited as coupling
agent) is being used.
Organosilane acryltriethoxysilane (ATES) is used
in titanium oxide reinforced nanocomposite; increases
the dispersion and linkage of titanium oxide nano-
particles within the resin matrix.
Recently, the process of sol-gel surface modification
of filler (quartz) particles, substantially improved
bonding of fillers with the resin matrix.
d. Initiator and Accelerator
Benzoyl peroxide is used as initiator in chemically
cure composites. (Earlier composites were chemically
cure.) An accelerator, a small amount of tertiary amines,
such as dimethyl-p-toluidine, is added in the monomer.
The polymerization is initiated by the release of free
radicals from the reaction between benzoyl peroxide
and the amine.
Since amines were mostly unstable, a stable
accelerator, p-toluidine sulfinic acid, showed effective
results.
Light cure composites, which utilizes benzoin
methyl ether (UV light activated) and camphorquinone
(visible light activated) as initiator.
Dimethylamino ethyl methacrylate is used as
accelerator. The differences between chemically-cure
and light-cure composites are presented in Table 19.1.
The differences between the two lights used for
curing composite are summarized in Table 19.2.

ADVANCES IN COMPOSITES
i. Flowable Composites
The flowable composites are characterized by
substantially reducing the filler contents, so as to
decrease the viscosity of composite material
(Fig. 19.1). They serve as liner to absorb the shrinkage/
contraction of the overlying composite restoration.
The characteristic features are:
• Filler content is 20–25% less than that of the
traditional hybrid composites.
• Because of lesser amount of fillers loading, the flow
is increased.
• Depth of cure is appropriate (approximately 6.0 mm).
• Stickiness to the instrument, which makes it
difficult to smoothen the material.
• Mechanical properties, like compressive strength,
tensile strength, flexure strength and toughness
values, are less as compared to conventional
composites.

ii. Packable/Bulk-fill Composites
Packable/bulk-fill composites are based on the
concept, called PRIMM (polymer rigid inorganic
matrix material). The filler/inorganic phase, instead
of being incorporated as ground particles, is present
as a continuous network/scaffold of ceramic fibers.
The fibers are composed of alumina and silicon dioxide.
The diameter of the individual ceramic fiber is less
than 2.0 m.
Examples of condensable composites include
Solitaire (Heraeus-Kulzer), Alert  (Jeneric/Pentron),
Surefil  (Caulk, Dentsply), Filtek P60 (3M), etc.
The consistency of PRIMM-based composites is
similar to that of a freshly triturated mass of silver
amalgam. The composite is inserted into the prepared
cavity by ejecting from a carrier whose nozzle is
preferably made from/coated with wear-resistant
teflon polymer (Fig. 19.2A, B). The use of a conventional
amalgam carrier is not advised as the hard alumina
fibers can scratch/damage the nozzle easily. Each
ejected increment is then condensed and light cured.
Most bulk-fill composites are placed in 4.0 mm
increment/one bulk and can be cured to that depth
with improved curing lights and techniques. Many
studies comparing increment versus bulk-fill
placement have reported no difference in cuspal
deflection or marginal integrity.

iii. Antibacterial Composites
To minimize accumulation of plaque on the restorative
surface and development of secondary caries, various
antibacterial agents have been incorporated in the
composite resins.
The agents commonly incorporated are:
i. Chlorhexidine: Chlorhexidine was tried in an
attempt to reduce plaque accumulation; however,
that was not successful. The disadvantages of
using chlorhexidine are:
• Population shifts of the micro-organisms
• Antibacterial activity is short-lived
• Deterioration of the physical and mechanical
properties of the material.
• Toxic effects of the released material
Other soluble antibacterial agents added to
composite include chitosan and cetylpyridinium
chloride. [Benzalkonium chloride (quaternary
ammonium compound) could achieve antibacterial
effects without changing the physical properties.]
ii. Silver: The antibacterial effect is attributed to the
direct contact of silver with bacteria and not
because of release of silver ions. The catalytic
action of silver and the hydroxyl radicals results
in the structural damage of bacteria. (Phenomenon
is referred to as the oligodynamic action.)
The inclusion of silver into composite does not
adversely affect the mechanical properties, like
strength, translucency, color stability and depth
of cure. Silver does not disrupt the polymerization
of composite monomers. Silver-silica glass
prepared by the sol-gel method has shown
promising antibacterial efficacy.
iii. Methacryloxydodecyl pyridinium bromide
(MDPB): Methacryloxydodecyl pyridinium
bromide has been incorporated into the resin
composite. The methacryloyl structure of the MDPB
molecule co-polymerizes with other methacrylate
monomers and is bound to the matrix resin. After
curing, there occurs no elution of the antibacterial
components from the material. MDPB was found
effective against streptococci, but other plaque
bacteria remained ineffective. Inclusion of MDPB
into composite had no adverse effects on the
mechanical properties of the composites.
iv. Zinc oxide powder: Zinc oxide powder displays
antimicrobial properties. Zinc oxide nanoparticles
have been found to be more effective than larger
particles, both against gram-negative and gram-
positive micro-organisms. The antimicrobial
properties of zinc oxide nanoparticles are because
they generate active oxygen, which inhibits
growth of planktonic microbes. Toxicological
mechanisms of zinc ions play an important role
in biofilm inhibition by inhibiting the active
transport and metabolism of sugars. Zinc also act
by disrupting enzyme systems of dental biofilms
by displacing magnesium ions essential for
enzymatic activity of plaque.
v. Antibacterial polycations: The bacterial cell
structure is negatively charged. The polymers
retaining positive charge/cationic on their surface
act as contact disinfectants. Such polymers
include, pyridinium type polymers and polymers
activated with polyethyleneimine.
Quaternary ammonium polyethyleneimine has
been reported to be highly effective (antibacterial
activity is attributed to the hydrophobic and
positively charged long polymeric chains). It is
effective against gram-positive and gram-negative
bacteria.
Nanoparticles of polyethyleneimine, even in
traces, when added to composite, have shown high
antibacterial activity.
vi. Newer agents: A few agents have been incorporated
into resin monomers to have antibacterial activity.
Dimethyl-hexadecyl-methacryloxyethylammo-
nium iodide (DHMAI), a quaternary ammonium
derivative, when added in 7.5% concentration,
provides strong antibacterial effect. The strength
and hardness of the composite remain unaffected
by its addition. Another such compound,

iv. Expanding Resin Matrix for Composites
Composite resins that would expand during
polymerization are desirable to minimize failure of
the restoration due to polymerization shrinkage. Spiro-
orthocarbonates (SOCs) have been tried as expanding
matrix in composites. It has been established that
SOCs were compatible with BisGMA-TEGDMA
mixtures. These formulations exhibited less shrinkage
than the conventional resins, but shrinkage could not
be completely eliminated.
Epoxy resins have shown favorable results. The
epoxy resins contract approximately 3.4% and SOCs
expand approximately 3.6%; therefore, combining
these two will achieve a net polymerization expansion.
It is observed that spiro-orthocarbonate in combination
with epoxy resin decreases polymerization shrinkage,
increases toughness and decreases water permeation.
v. Bioactive Composites
Calcium phosphate and its modified varieties, when
used as filler, serve as bioactive liners and bases,
enhancing the remineralization process. Amorphous
calcium phosphate hybridized with glass forming
agents is also used as filler in composites, known as
‘smart composite’.
vi. Ormocer
Ormocer (acronym of organically modified ceramic),
is an improved version of composite; can be used for
both anterior and posterior teeth.
Chemically, it differs from composites in matrix
constituent. Traditional composites are based upon
BisGMA, TEGDMA, etc. The ormocer matrix consists
of ceramic polysiloxane (silicon-oxygen chains). The
polysiloxane is biocompatible and exhibits low
shrinkage. Ormocer contains 20–25% matrix and the
rest are fillers and coloring agents.
Ormocer is considered to be more biocompatible
than the currently available composite resins owing
to its crosslinked structure with least amounts of
residual monomer. The bonding agent used with
ormocers is an ormocer-based agent with functional
calcium group for enhancing the bond strength to the
tooth structure.
The commercial preparation, Admira Fusion
(VOCO) with nano-silicon oxide (SiO) particles
exhibited polymerization shrinkage up to 50%. The
degree of conversion, being more than 95%, the
material is identical to indirect composite; sustain
high degree of lustre and is stain resistant.

viii. Fiber-reinforced Composite
Fiber-reinforced composites can be divided according
to the reinforcement of polymer fillers used. Fiber-
reinforced composites have two distinct constituents.
The reinforcing component provides strength and
stiffness, while surrounding matrix provides
workability. Glass fibers are the most commonly used
reinforcing fiber in dental applications. Carbon/
graphite/metal fibers are also used. Fiber-reinforced
composites (FRCs) can also be divided into groups
based on length and orientation of fibers; long fibers
containing FRCs and short fiber containing FRC.
Unidirectional and bidirectional fiber variants are
also available.
Fiber reinforced composites have improved
mechanical properties and their strength to weight
ratio is superior to most alloys used in dentistry.
Glass fibers have documented reinforcing efficiency
and good esthetic qualities as compared to carbon
fibers. The effectiveness of fiber reinforcement is
dependent on many variables, including the type of
resin used, quantity of fibers in the resin matrix, fiber
length, form, orientation and impregnation with the
resin.

A light curing unit with a minimal light output of
550 lux is considered appropriate for dental use. The
amount of light radiation reaching a given point in the
material depends on the following factors:
i. Lamp output intensity: The energy output from the
lamp; governed by lamp power rating.
ii. Exposure time: The exposure time is adjusted to
maximize energy output.
iii. Distance from light source to material: It is established
that part of the emitted light is absorbed by air
through which it passes; the source of light should
be close to the target surface (without touch).
iv. Curing depth: Light is gradually absorbed within
the material. For the depth of curing, the amount
of energy required will be equal to the intensity
and duration of exposure.
v. Presence of elements between light source and target
material: The amount of light absorbed by
intervening elements varies according to the
thickness and optical behavior of the elements.
Enamel is transparent and allows the passage of
ample amount of light; however, dentin is
considerably less transparent and allow virtually
no light to pass through. The passage of light
through bands and preformed crown is similarly
governed by transparency and thickness.
LIGHT-CURING UNITS
Various modalities of light and light cure units are as
follows.
1. Ultraviolet light cure units: Ultraviolet light cure
unit utilizes ultraviolet light in the range of 365 nm;
however, these units are no longer used.
small fan is employed to dissipate unwanted heat
from the filters and reflector. Usually, filters
degrade with time due to the heating and cooling

Techniques of Curing
The techniques of light curing composites are:
a. Soft-start curing: In soft-start technique, initially
low intensity curing is utilized, followed by a high
intensity curing. Various light-curing units provide
soft-start exposure sequences (100 mW/cm2 for 10
seconds, followed by 600 mW/cm2 for 30 seconds).
Soft-start polymerization is divided into three
techniques—stepped, ramped, and pulse-delay.
i. Stepped: The restoration is initially cured at low
intensity to contour and shape the restoration. It
is followed by second exposure to completely cure
the finished restoration.
ii. Ramped: In this method, intensity is gradually
increased or ‘ramped up.’ The intensity is
increased with time either by bringing the light
closer towards the tooth or using a curing light
designed to increase in intensity with time.
iii. Pulse-delay: In the pulse-delay method, a series of
exposure pulses is used, each separated by a rest
period. An initial exposure of up to 1 J/cm2 is
effective in reducing shrinkage stresses. During
the rest period, polymerization reaction occurs at
a reduced rate.
b. High-intensity curing: High-intensity curing allows
for shorter exposure times for a given depth of cure.
A depth of 2.0 mm can be cured in 10 seconds with
a plasma arc light and 5 seconds with an argon
laser-curing light, as compared to 40 seconds by a
quartz- tungsten-halogen lamp. The relationship
between energy density and shrinkage may
translate into increased stress levels, but do not
result in high degrees of conversion or superior
mechanical properties.
Disadvantages
• Short exposure times cause accelerated rates of curing
and insufficient time for stress relaxation (lead to
greater shrinkage stresses).
• High-intensity light curing has a narrow wavelength
range (the wavelength range of the light source must
be coincident with the photoinitiator); subsequently,
inadequate curing.
• Considerable heat is generated.
• May not produce the same type of polymer network
during curing.
• Result in more cytotoxicity than a longer curing  time
with lower intensity.

Types of Cavity Preparation
The cavity preparation for composite is of two types:
a. Conventional cavity preparation: The conventional
cavity preparation follows Black’s principles. The
outline form includes extension of susceptible
lesions, proper uniform depth and the external
walls in accordance with enamel rods.
Bevels are usually recommended on labial surfaces
of anterior teeth so as to merge the color of the
composite and the enamel. Lingual bevels can be
avoided, since that might lead to stresses; also the
color merging is not important in that area. In Class
V cavities, bevels are given only on occlusal walls
and the gingival walls are spared. In posterior
restorations, bevels are not indicated, since the thin
layers of composites might chip off under stresses
leading to marginal gaps.
b. Modified cavity preparation: Modified cavity
preparation does not follow Black’s principles. The
retention part is totally dependent upon the etched
enamel and bonding. The carious lesions are
excavated and restored separately. This type of
cavity preparation conserves more tooth structure
and maintain the strength of the tooth. No definite
walls and line angles are visible and usually appear
saucer shaped. There can be more than one cavity
in a single tooth.
Class I Cavity
The conventional Class I cavity is prepared following
Black’s principles (Fig. 19.8). The depth of the cavity
can be kept only in enamel, but for better retention
and function, extension into the dentin is advisable.
The total depth can be kept as 1.5 mm. The buccolingual
width of the cavity is kept as small as possible (usual
width is ¼th of the intercuspal distance).
The cavity walls may not be kept perpendicular
and parallel.
The cavosurface margins are kept at right angle to
the cavity walls, without my bevel. The butt joint
preparation minimizes marginal breakdown and
ditching of the composites.
The pits and fissures on the occlusal two-thirds of
buccal and lingual surfaces of posterior teeth and
lingual pits of anterior teeth can be restored with
composites in a similar way. Butt joint preparation is
preferred; however, a short bevel can be given for
merging the color of composites. The depth of such
preparation is 1.0–1.5 mm (as shallow as possible).

The occlusal part of the cavity is prepared as described
for Class I cavity. The proximal box is prepared keeping

load.
The procedure followed for composites is as for
conventional box formation. The buccal and lingual
walls of the proximal box are kept slightly away from
the contact area (might not be in self-cleansing areas).
The occlusal convergence, though not indicated, can
be given to achieve better retention.

COMPOSITE LAMINATES AND VENEERS
The laminates and veneers are interchangeable terms;
the only difference is in laminates, the overall shape is
not changed, while in veneers the shape of the tooth is
changed. Laminates are given only where the teeth
are discolored but their shape, size and position in the
arch is normal; whereas, veneers are given where the
teeth need change in their shape, size and the position
in the arch.
The laminates are preferred in maxillary anterior
teeth since these teeth play a predominant role in
smile and esthetics of a person. Mandibular anterior
teeth are rarely laminated because of lack of space and
more so, the teeth are hidden under the lower lips.
Composite laminates can be direct and indirect
depending upon whether the laminate is fabricated
inside or outside the oral cavity.
Indirect laminates are advantageous since they
provide better esthetics, less fatigue to the patient and
easily manipulated. However, the procedure is
completed in two steps. Direct laminates, though save
patient’s visits, are tiring and also technique sensitive.
a. Direct Laminates/Veneers
Tooth Preparation
Preparation varies depending upon whether the
discoloration envelopes the marginal ridges and the
incisal edge or not. In deep dark stains, which extend
towards the incisal enamel, the preparation involves
the incisal edge. Otherwise, the preparation ends
slightly away from incisal edge. Similarly whether to
extend the preparation below the gingival crest is also
dictated by the extent of the stain at these areas.
Preferably, the preparation should be kept 0.5 mm
supragingivally to avoid any impingment to the
gingival crests.
The tooth is prepared using fissure burs. The depth
of the preparation is 0.5 to 0.7 mm and is kept in
enamel only. In case, the underlying portion is dark,
further cutting is initiated, total depth being 1.0 to
1.25 mm. The extra depth is utilized for the opaquer to
mask the dark color.
A few authors advocate use of cross-cut burs for
cutting labial enamel, so as to achieve undercuts and
large surface area for etching. Though with this
technique, retention is increased, but utmost care is
necessary not to overcut the tooth.
Placement of Composite
After shade selection, the tooth is isolated using rubber
dam. Rubber dam is preferred, since laminates are
time consuming and the cotton rolls will not be effective
for such a long time.
In case, the preparation extends beyond the gingival
crest into the subgingival area, use of retraction cords
is mandatory.
The area is etched for 15–20 seconds, if the preparation
is in enamel, and 10 seconds, if it is in dentin. The
etchant is thoroughly washed and dried using air
from chip syringe. A thin coat of bonding agent is
applied, made uniform by blowing air without
pressure and cured for 10 seconds. The non-uniform
application of bonding agent should be avoided.
The selected composite is placed in small increments
from cervical area to the incisal. Blending of color
from one shade to other is very important. Two shades
can be mixed and applied at the junction of cervical-
middle third areas; and also at middle-incisal third
areas. Slightly dark shades are required at the line
angles. Finally, the incisal edge is restored using
translucent composite. Coloring tints (available in
various shades) can be used to have the reflection of
different shades. In case the tooth is darkly stained
and the preparation is in dentin, the middle area of the
preparation or the ‘body’ of the preparation should be
filled with opaquer.
A slight overcontoured tooth is preferred at this
stage, since at the time of final finishing, the exact
contour can be re-established. In case, only one tooth
is laminated, the final contour can be checked with
adjacent tooth and if more than one tooth is to be
laminated, overall patient’s smile requirements and
esthetics should be kept in mind (Fig. 19.40A, B).

COMPOSITE INLAYS
A composite inlay is defined as “a restoration fabricated
with composite material and is cemented into a cavity
prepared specifically for the composite”.
Need for a Composite Inlay?
Direct posterior composite restorations have certain
limitations, viz.
• Despite the incremental build-up techniques,
polymerization shrinkage is a major problem, which
contributes to marginal defects, cuspal distortion,
crack formation and propagation within the tooth
tissues, resulting in postoperative sensitivity.
• Inadequate polymerization in deep interproximal
areas; may lead to partly cured restoration.
• Difficult to achieve proper bond with dentinal
surface.
• Increased wear in load-bearing situations.
• Water sorption with resultant hydrolytic instability.
• Contour, contacts are not proper.
To overcome these clinical problems, the indirect
composite inlay system was introduced. It has been
established that inlays enhance the hardness and wear
resistance of the material and also reduces the
microleakage providing better adaptability at the
tooth–restoration interface.
Indications
• Conventional tooth-colored restoration of better
quality.
• Moderate- to large-sized lesions, where sufficient
tooth tissue remains following preparation.
• Where there is no evidence of excessive tooth wear.
• Restoration not overloaded occlusally.
• When better control over contacts and contours is
desired.
Contraindications
• Inadequate remaining tooth tissues to create proper
resistance and retention forms for the purpose of
bonding.
• Teeth, which show excessive wear.
• Situations, where moisture control cannot be
secured and maintained.
• Teeth that experience heavy occlusal forces, like in
bruxism, clenching, etc.
• Un-cooperative patients.
• Patients who do not maintain oral hygiene.
Advantages
• Better compressive strength, tensile strength and
hardness
• Better viscoelastic stability
• Increased creep resistance to occlusal stresses
• Better control of contacts and contours
• Improved marginal adaptation
• Soft and gentle to nearby tooth
• Less technique sensitive
• Saves chair time.
• Can easily be adjusted in the oral cavity
• Easy manipulation in the oral cavity
Disadvantages
• Fabrication requires two appointments and a
temporary restoration.
• Preparation of the cavity may necessitate removal of
sound tooth structure to create parallelism and other
features.
• Temporary restoration, if not proper, may lead to
bacterial contamination, etc.
• Durability is not established.
• In case fitting is not proper, luting agents do not help.
Classification of Composite Inlays
Composite inlays can be classified on the basis of
curing protocols as:
a. Heat cured: The inlays are cured at elevated
temperature and under pressure in one stage. The
composite employed is heat cured rather than light
cured, e.g. SR Isosit system. (Inlays are cured at
120°C and under pressure.)
b. Light cured: One mode of curing (Inlay is cured by
light only.)
c. Dual cured: Initial curing at room temperature by
light followed by additional curing by heat and
light, e.g. Coltene Brilliant system.

Method of Fabrication
The composite inlays can be fabricated either in office
or in laboratory.
a. In-office Fabrication
The fabrication of composite inlay in office involves
following steps:
• The prepared cavity is isolated preferably using
rubber dams. The cavity walls are painted with a
lubricant compatible with the hybrid composite
material, which facilitates easy removal of inlay.
• A retainerless, contoured, clear matrix is adjusted
around the proximal area of the prepared cavity.
A clear reflecting wedge is placed at the inter-
proximal gingival margins. The wedge is firmly
placed to create rapid separation of the teeth.
(Wedge compensates for thickness of the mylar
matrix band facilitating developing contact area
with the adjacent tooth.)
• The hybrid composite material is placed into the
prepared cavity starting with the proximal box. The
material is gently condensed with a ball burnisher.
The occlusal portion of the preparation is also filled
and gently condensed. The ball burnisher used for
condensation should be coated with a resin
adhesive to avoid sticking of the material.
• The pressure of the wedges helps rapid separation
of the teeth and also do not allow excess material
at the gingival area. The interproximal area is cured
for 30 seconds both from facial and the lingual
aspects; followed by curing of occlusal surface for
30 seconds (soft polymerization).
• After the completion of light curing, the inlay is
gently eased out of the prepared cavity. (A gentle
lift from the proximal area using hand instruments
is usually required.) If the inlay resists removal, a
loop of dental floss along with a small increment
of the composite material is placed in the central
fossa area of the inlay and light cured. The inlay is
then pulled along the path of withdrawal.

REPAIR OF COMPOSITE RESTORATIONS
Repair implies partial replacement of the composite
restoration allowing preservation of rest of the
restoration, which presents no clinical or radiographic
evidence of failures. The repair procedure will preserve
the tooth structure and maintain function of the
restoration.
Rationale
• Preservation of tooth structure
• Increased longevity of the restoration
• Reduction of potentially harmful effects on the
dental pulp
• Reduction in treatment time
• Reduced cost to the patient
• Less risk of iatrogenic damage to the adjacent tooth
The possibility of obtaining a chemical bond
between resin in the old substrate and the bonding
agent decreases with time. The resin does not release
the radicals for bonding. (Hydrolytic degradation is
higher in old composites.) The inorganic filler particles
of old composite need to be resilanized to improve
bond to organic monomer of the repair material.
Various methods have been suggested to establish
adequate bond strength between the existing
composite and the new composite. These treatments
include, surface roughening and the use of intermediate
bonding agents to enhance repair bond strength. The
roughening of the surface through acid etching, burs
or abrasives, promotes mechanical interlocking
through microretentive features and application of
bonding agent improves surface wetting and chemical
bonding with the new composite. In addition to surface
roughening, silane treatment of the exposed filler
particles can be carried out as silane acts as a
