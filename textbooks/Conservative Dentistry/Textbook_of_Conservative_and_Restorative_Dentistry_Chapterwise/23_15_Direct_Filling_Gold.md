Gold foil is undeniably the permanent restorative
modality. The term ‘gold foil’ and ‘direct filling gold’
is used interchangeably; gold foil is one form of pure
gold, other forms are also available.
In teeth with sound pulpal and periodontal health,
gold foil is a superior restorative material, especially
for small cavities. The proper case selection, adequate
cavity preparation and material manipulation may
lead direct gold restorations to last for a lifetime.
Direct gold are those gold restorative materials that
are manufactured for compaction directly into the
prepared cavities.
PROPERTIES OF PURE GOLD
• Pure gold is soft, malleable, ductile and does not
oxidize under the normal atmospheric conditions.
One grain of gold can be drawn into a wire of nearly
500 feet long. Malleting produces a sheet as thin as
0.13 mm.
• It is yellow in color with a strong metallic lustre.
• Fuses at a temperature of 1063°C and boils at
2200°C.
• Density of pure gold is 19.0–19.3 g/cm3. (True
density is difficult to achieve in restorations because
of the voids, which tend to decrease the density of
the restoration. The apparent density is in the range
of 14–15 g/cm3.)
• Brinell’s hardness number of pure gold is 25. This
would seem to contraindicate its use as a restorative
material. However, cold working while building
the restoration increases its hardness to almost 58–
82; known as ‘work hardening’ or ‘strain
hardening’.
• Coefficient of thermal expansion is 14.4 × 10–6/°C,
which is close to that of tooth structure, i.e. 11.4 ×
10–6/°C.
• It has a high thermal conductivity of 0.710 cal/ sec/
cm2 (°C/cm).
• Gold is the noblest of all metals. Air or water at
any temperature does not affect or tarnish gold. It
is not soluble in sulfuric acid, nitric acid or

hydrochloric acid; however, readily dissolves in
aqua regia (a combination of nitric acid and
hydrochloric acid) to form trichlorides of gold
(AuCl3).
• Small amounts of impurities have a pronounced
effect on mechanical properties of gold, e.g. 0.2%
lead makes gold very brittle. Bismuth and mercury
have also shown adverse effects on gold; whereas,
addition of small amounts of calcium, palladium
and platinum improve its properties.
• Able to be cold worked and welded at room
temperature. (If two clean pieces of gold are
pressed firmly together and a sufficient force
applied, they unite at their point of contact and the
gold is welded together without the application of
heat.) This process is known as ‘cold welding’.
• Provide efficient sealing, when used as a restorative
material.

I. Gold Foil
i. Gold Foil Sheets
Gold sheets are manufactured by a process called
‘gold beating’ or ‘rolling’. The light weight sheets are
formed by beating and heavy weight sheets by rolling.
Heavier mallets are used for beating gold ingots
followed by the lighter ones when the gold gets
thinner. In the rolling process, gold is passed
continuously through the rolling mills until the
desired thickness is achieved. The processes
mentioned above render gold non-cohesive. To use
as a restorative material, gold should be turned
cohesive by annealing.
Gold foil sheets are bound and contain 12 sheets;
each sheet has the dimensions of 4" × 4" (Fig. 15.1).
No. 4 gold foil is the standard size, which is usually
used. It weighs 4 grains (0.259 gm/259 mg) and is
0.51 mm thick. Similarly, No.3 gold foil weighs 3
grains, No.2 gold foil 2 grains and so on.
ii. Gold Foil Pellets
An ideal pellet is produced from a No. 4 gold foil by
the operator. The procedure for fabrication includes
removing the No. 4 gold foil, held onto the paper,
marked (Fig. 15.2) and cut along with it. The paper
supports the thin gold foil and prevents it from tearing
and wrinkling during manipulation. Sizes, which are
commonly used for rolling into pellets, are 1/16
onwards. Larger sizes like 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 are used for
forming cylinders. The desired piece of gold foil is
grasped from the center and held between the thumb,
index finger and middle finger of the left hand. Ends
are tucked and rolled lightly into round balls of gold.
iii. Gold Foil Cylinders
Gold foil cylinders are custom made by rolling cut
segments of No. 4 gold foil usually 1/2, 1/4 and 1/8
into desired widths.
iv. Corrugated/Carbonized Gold Foil
Corrugated gold foil is manufactured by placing thin
sheets of paper in between the gold foil sheets, which
are then ignited. The paper burns and gets charred;
however, the gold foil remains unharmed, but
becomes corrugated because of the shrivelling of
paper.
v. Laminated Gold Foil
When a gold foil is beaten from an ingot, its crystals
are elongated in a specific direction. When two or
three foils with crystals running in different directions
are combined together, it results in a material that
has fibers running in all directions, making it more
resistant to the applied forces. Such a combination is
known as laminated gold foil.
vi. Platinized Gold Foil
This is a sandwich of gold and platinum with the
platinum content being 15%; formed in two ways:
• One sheet of pure platinum foil is sandwiched
between two sheets of gold foil. These are then
hammered until the thickness of one No. 4 gold
foil is obtained.
• Layers of platinum and gold are rolled over
together so that there occurs fusion of the two, even
before the beating procedure has begun.
Platinum increases the hardness and wear
resistance of the restoration; used in stress
concentration areas.

i. Mat/Crystal Gold
The earlier crystal gold is electrolytic precipitated gold
obtained by a process similar to electroplating, but at
an accelerated rate. During subsequent heating

tend to weld themselves together. These are available

cavity. However, its use is restricted to forming only
the internal bulk of the restoration because of its large
surface area. Since mat gold is loosely packed, is
friable and contains numerous voids, a gold foil is
recommended for the external surface of the
restoration.
ii. Mat Foil
Mat foil is a sandwich of mat gold placed in the sheets
of gold foil. The sandwich is sintered by heating below
the melting point of gold and cut into strips of
different widths. The idea to use mat foil was to
eliminate the need to veneer the restoration with a
layer of gold foil.
iii. Electralloy (Gold Calcium Alloy)
Electralloy is an alloy of electrolytic gold and calcium
(0.1–0.5% by weight). The purpose of adding calcium
is to produce stronger restorations. The product is
sandwiched between two layers of gold foil.
III. Powdered/Granulated Gold and its
Combination with Gold Foil
Powdered gold is in the form of minute particles,
which are obtained by chemical precipitation (gold is
dissolved in aqua regia and precipitated by oxalic acid,
sulfur dioxide or sodium nitrate). The average
particle size is 15 . The powder is gathered into a
conglomerate mass having a diameter of 1.0–3.0 mm.
The mass is pre-condensed to facilitate adhesion
between the particles and ease of handling. Because
powdered gold produces a less porous surface as
compared to mat gold, veneering with a gold foil is
not necessary.
The pellets of powdered gold wrapped in a gold
foil are known as ‘Goldent’ (Fig. 15.4). The powdered
particles held in a gold foil, which acts as a container
for the powdered particles and facilitates their
condensation. Each pellet contains approximately ten
times more gold than a pellet of gold foil of
comparable size. The ratio is 95% powder and 5.0%
foil.
IV. Cohesive and Non-Cohesive
Pure gold can be classified into cohesive, semicohesive
and non-cohesive forms. It is emphasized that two
gold surfaces can cohere by welding at room
temperature, only if the surfaces are atomically clean.
The clean foil can be placed directly into the cavity.
Such a form of gold is called the ‘cohesive gold’. It is
difficult to maintain the cohesive state as some gases
may get adsorbed during storage. It is, therefore,
recommended to degas even the cohesive forms
before placing them in the cavity preparation.
The ‘semicohesive gold form’ is one in which a
protective gas film, like ammonia, is deliberately
adsorbed onto the surface of gold by the manufacturer.
This is beneficial as it minimizes the adsorption of other
less volatile substances.

ANNEALING/DEGASSING
‘Annealing’ a gold foil refers to the removal of
surface contaminants. (Degassing’ or ‘desorption’ is
the preferred term.) The primary purpose is to
produce an atomically clean surface and render the
material cohesive and workable. For non-cohesive
gold, degassing removes the protective ammonia
film. In cohesive gold, various contaminants like,
sulfur and phosphorus compounds, oxides or water
vapour may have been adsorbed onto the surface
during storage. Temperatures in the range of 600–
1300°F is required to volatilize gases or other
contaminants.
The commonly used methods for degassing are:
i. Alcohol flame: This is the preferred method for
degassing. Absolute/90% ethyl alcohol is used to
produce a clean blue flame. Regular cohesive gold
is held only momentarily for a few seconds in the
flame until the color changes to dull cherry red.
Powdered gold must be held in the flame until all
the wax is burnt out and the pellet reaches a dull
red glow.
Degassing may be carried out by piece method or
bulk method. In the piece method, a single piece
is annealed at one time. The gold is held with a
suitable instrument, usually a nichrome wire
(Fig. 15.5A).
Advantages
• Minimum wastage
• Ability to select a piece of desired size
• Eliminates contamination amongst different
pellets during their heating and use
‘Bulk degassing’ is the degassing of several gold
pellets at the same time. Mica trays are used to
hold the gold over the flame. Bulk annealing has
the advantage of taking less time in the whole
process compared to the piece method. However,
gold pellets may stick together, if the tray is
accidentally moved during heating.
Precautions taken during flame degassing are:
• The lamp should not run out of fuel during the
procedure.
• There should be no surface contaminants and
waxes on the lamp and wick.
• Wick should be properly trimmed and rounded
to produce a tear drop flame.
• Exercise care during lighting as sulfur in the
matches could adhere to the wick and
contaminate the gold (ignite with the other end
of the match stick).
ii. Electric annealer: The desired amount of gold is
placed in the tray of the annealer and the lid
closed. The gold is heated at a temperature of
800°F for 10 minutes and then allowed to cool
before it is placed in the cavity.
Disadvantages of electric annealer:
• Inadequate/excessive amounts may get degassed.
• Any remaining gold needs to be discarded
because of possible irreversible contamination.
• A temperature of 800°F may not be adequate
(require a temperature of 900°F–1200°F).
Care should be taken to avoid underheating or
overheating during degassing. ‘Underheating’ does
not adequately remove all surface impurities. This
results in incomplete cohesion, because of remaining
contaminants leading to overall inferior properties of
the restoration. ‘Overheating’ leads to excessive
sintering and possible contamination from the tray,
instruments or flame.
CONDENSATION/COMPACTION
Condensation is the procedure used to condense and
harden gold inside the cavity preparation. Proper
condensation results in a dense, non-porous gold
restoration. Each pellet must be thoroughly
condensed before the next is added and condensed,
otherwise may result in porosity.

The objectives of compaction are:
• Cohere two pieces of gold to each other. This
process is known as welding.
• Adapt the gold intimately to the walls and margins
of the prepared cavity.
• When the gold is condensed, slip planes are
developed and the resulting stresses harden the
restoration.
• To drive away air from in between the pieces of
gold and gold–tooth interface.
The compaction techniques vary with the types of
gold. The features affecting compaction are:
i. Force of the blow: The average force required to
condense gold with a condenser point of 1.0 mm
diameter is 15 pounds. It has been found that this
much amount of force can be obtained only when
the operator applies maximum finger thrust. This
would imply that the operator gets rapidly tired,
if hand pressure alone is used for condensation.
The requisite force from a mallet is recommended
because it is rapid, easier and less strenuous. Blows
from the mallet should neither be too strong nor
too light. Stronger blows deliver heavier forces
which could perforate the gold and damage pulp
and supporting structures. With lighter blows,
inadequate force is applied, which could result in
imperfect compaction, poor adaptation to cavity
walls and a restoration full of voids. Factors which
control the ultimate force delivered in a blow are:
• Weight of the hammer and the velocity with
which it is brought down on the condenser
• Resistance of the substance receiving the blow
• Area of the condenser point in contact with the
restoration.
For the first two characteristics, force is directly
proportional to the weight and velocity of the
mallet.
Size of the condenser nib, i.e. diameter and area
also influence the amount of total force needed.
Larger diameter condenser nibs require greater
forces for compaction as compared to smaller nibs.
It is established that when the diameter of the
instrument is doubled, forces required are
increased four times.
ii. Angulation of condenser (line of force): Force
applied with a condenser parallels the long axis
of the instrument shaft regardless of the angle of
the working point. While compacting in the center
of the cavity, forces are usually directed at 90° to
the pulpal floor. Black advocated an angle of 45°
to the cavity walls at the periphery, such that the
line of force bisects the line angles and trisects the
point angles.

masticatory stresses would be able to bear heavy
malleting forces. Multi-rooted teeth withstand greater
condensation pressure than single-rooted teeth.
Restorations placed on areas of heavy occlusal
stresses, such as incisal edges and cusp tips, require
heavy condensation. It is suggested that regardless
of the type of condensation method used, the desired
compaction should be achieved with forces that do
not damage the tooth structure.
Hand condensers have a long handle (six inch) and
a blunt end; whereas, mechanical mallets have short
shanks of one inch in length. Condenser shanks may
be straight, monoangle or offset. Nibs of the pluggers
can be of various shapes like round, rectangular and
parallelogram. The latter two condensers compared
to the former, require three times more force during
compaction because of their large size.
The wedge-like serrations present on the face of
the condenser nibs increase their surface area. The
serrations prevent slipping of the gold and to cut
through the outer layers to allow entrapped air to
escape. When using angled pluggers for condensation,
the effective force is decreased, because the force does
not act at right angles to the tip surface. The lateral
force may displace the placement of tip or displace
the gold.
Compaction/Condensation Technique
The gold foil cut to the size and shape of the cavity
dimension is delicately spread out by hand. Gold is
adapted lightly to the cavity walls, line angles and
point angles by hand pressure. A condenser of
desirable size is used to begin malleting in the center
of the mass. Slowly, the condenser is moved to the
periphery along straight lines using the stepping
process. As it reaches the walls, the condenser is
turned from 90° to 45°, such that it bisects the line
angles and trisects the point angles (Fig. 15.7A, B).
While building the restoration, forces of condensation
must be directed at 90° to the previously condensed
gold; prevent shear components from displacing the
already condensed pieces of gold.
The compaction of 1st layer produces a building
shelf and thereafter a gold bank is developed onto it.
The gold bank is kept against the walls, until the
cavosurface margins are reached, thus creating a
concave surface while building the restoration
(Fig. 15.8). The central concave area left in the
restoration is filled last. In case the gold buldges in
the center, it may prevent proper application of the
force and hence, poor adaptation. Improper
condensation could lead to ‘bridging’ of gold.
Bridging is the covering up of small crevices and pits
present in the deeper portions of the cavity, especially
adjacent to the cavity walls.

Crushing the cavosurface enamel is a frequent
mishap. To avoid this, some amount of gold should
invariably be present between the condenser point
and the enamel margins (Fig. 15.9). If such an injury
is recognized early in the process, removal of the
sheared components could re-establish a clean
margin allowing condensation to be completed.
Retention in a direct gold filling is best obtained by
harnessing the elasticity of dentin. During
condensation, forces tend to compress the dentin,
so that when the filling is completed the dentin
returns back to its original position, thereby tightly
holding the restoration.
Condensation of Gold Foil
Because of the toughness and smaller mass per unit
volume, gold foil can be easily manipulated in smaller
cavity preparations and against cavity bevels. The
property of the material to roll out of the undercuts
towards the condenser may pose problem. This
requires making sharp line and point angles to serve
as retentive locks and using holding instrument to
secure the gold in position while it is being condensed.
If pellets are used, the first gold pellet is condensed
into a point angle (Fig. 15.10). A holding instrument
is used to stabilize the previously condensed gold,
while additional pellets are added.
Condensation of Mat Gold
Mat gold is cut to the shape and size of the cavity and
placed on the pulpal/axial walls of the preparation.
It is condensed against the walls using two
condensers. One condenser stabilizes the gold, while
the other performs condensation. Two layers of gold
are of sufficient thickness to resist curling of the gold
during subsequent condensation. Best results are
obtained when hand pressure condensation is
followed by malleting. Mat gold is easy to start
because of their loose spongy nature (known as
‘starter gold’). The thickness of mat gold may create
problems. If the gold is too thick, it may get strain
hardened earlier, preventing the condensation forces
affecting the deeper portions.
Condensation of Powdered Gold
The cavity preparation for powdered gold differs
slightly; (i) It does not require sharp line angles and
point angles, (ii) bevels need to be minimized, because
powdered gold condenses more readily against butt
cavosurface margins. Upon contact with a condenser,
the pellet of powdered gold tends to break apart and
wedge itself into the opposing undercuts. It does not
require a holding instrument for stabilization.
Powdered gold should be firmly wedged between
two opposing walls for proper condensation. A pellet
of larger size or two/three pellets may be placed
together to wedge the space. Pressure is then exerted
with a rocking motion to adapt and harden gold
initially in the line angles (Fig. 15.11). After the
periphery has been built by powdered gold, the center
of the preparation is then filled.

Indications for Direct Gold Restoration
i. Incipient carious lesions: The direct gold should
be limited to only small cavities. Because of its
property to get easily deformed under forces, pure
gold does not bear the masticatory stresses
adequately. The indications include:
• Class I lesions in premolars (lingual pits on
maxillary anteriors and facial pits on molars)
• Small Class II lesions (premolars)
• Class III lesions (maxillary and mandibular)
• Class V lesions (small lesions on the facial
surfaces of premolars and canines). Not advised
in maxillary incisors and molars, because of
limitations of esthetics in the former and poor
accessibility in the latter.
ii. Erosion lesions: Same as those mentioned for
Class V lesions.
iii. Atypical lesions
• Proximal lesions on teeth adjacent to crown
preparations.
• Vent holes in crowns and defective inlay (crown
margins can be effectively repaired with direct
gold).
• Class VI lesions (cusps tips and incisal edges)
(Fig. 15.12A, B).
• Retrograde root canal filling.
Contraindications for Direct Gold Restoration
• Teeth with very large pulp chambers (susceptibility
to get damaged during condensation).
• Periodontally weakened teeth (unable to withstand
the condensation forces).
• Large carious lesions (remaining dentin may be
inadequate).
• Handicapped, elderly or young patients (unable to
withstand long sittings).
• Psychologically weak patients who fear the
malleting process.
• Inaccessible/poorly accessible areas.
• Isolation difficulties.
• Areas of undesirable occlusal stresses.
• Patients with a high caries index.
• Hypoplastic areas, as forces required to condense
gold against the enamel margins might fracture the
latter.
• Financial constraints.
Advantages of Direct Gold Restoration
• A permanent method for repairing teeth, and the
restoration can last as long as the tooth itself.
• A noble metal, hence does not tarnish and corrode
in the oral cavity.
• Insoluble
• Coefficient of thermal expansion is close to that of
tooth.
• Small cavity preparation is atraumatic to dental
pulp and the supporting structures.
• Tooth discoloration does not occur around the
margins because of good material adaptation and
its noble and inert qualities.
• No cementing medium is necessary for retention
of the restoration.
• The surface can be effectively polished and the
smoothness can last indefinitely reducing the
chances of plaque accumulation.

It is mandatory to apply a rubber dam for proper
isolation during gold foil restoration. It should be
noted that even the breath of the patient could
contaminate the surface of gold and render it non-

Class I Cavity
The cavity is kept as small as possible consistent with
suitable access for instrumentation and manipulation.
In case of a pit defect, the outline may be simply
triangular, oblong or circular (Fig. 15.13).
The basic form of cavity simulates that for silver
amalgam (Fig. 15.14). Internal outline form includes
extending the pulpal floor to 0.5 mm below the
dentinoenamel junction.
Retention is provided by the parallelism of walls
and sharp line angles and point angles. The angles
can be accentuated using No. 6½–2½–9 hoe.
Additional retention, if desired, is given in the form
of undercuts in dentin by suitable inverted cone bur

Restoration
The cavity is usually filled with mat gold and
veneered with gold foil; may be entirely filled with
gold foil or powdered gold also.
i. Restoration utilizing mat gold veneered with
gold foil: Cavity varnish is applied on all the
preparation surfaces. A piece of mat gold is cut
to a size slightly larger than the cavity
preparation, degassed and placed into position
by a carrying instrument. Using two condensers,
gold is spread out in the form of a blanket and
intimately adapted to the preparation surfaces.
Malleting is not advised at this stage as the
purpose of this step is to simply adapt the gold.
It is only after the gold has been properly
adapted, malleting is begun in the center at 90°
to the pulpal floor. The condenser is moved by
stepping process towards the line angles to
complete the compaction. On reaching the
periphery, the line of force is changed to 45° to
the cavity walls. Another increment is then added
and condensed in a similar manner until the
cavity is half to two-thirds full.
The remaining cavity is completed with gold foils.
The gold foil pellet (size 1/16, 1/32, 1/64) is
degassed and placed into the cavity along the wall-
restoration interface. It is first spread and secured
against the already condensed mat gold with hand
pressure and then mallet compacted. Cavosurface
margins are covered with gold as soon as possible.
Gold should always be present between the
condenser face and marginal enamel, otherwise a
direct blow from the plugger might injure the
enamel. The central concave area is finally filled
with gold to a slightly higher level. An excess on
the surface allows for finishing and polishing
procedures.
Burnishing is performed with a special spratley
burnisher/a flat beaver tail burnisher/ball
burnisher that is run with considerable pressure
from the restoration to the tooth surface (Fig.
15.16). Contouring is done with a green stone/
garnet coated discs (Fig. 15.17). Gross excess at
the margins is removed with a cleoid discoid
carver or a gold foil carver (Fig. 15.18). After the
excess has been contoured and removed,
burnishing is again performed. A high lustre is
imparted to the restoration by using powdered
abrasives, like tin oxide, pumice, etc.
ii. Restoration utilizing only gold foil: When gold
foil pellets are used to completely fill the cavity,
the first pellet is placed in the most distal point
angle (e.g. disto-bucco-pulpal point angle). Using
a holding instrument, the gold is firmly wedged
with hand pressure between the three
surrounding walls. Malleting forces are delivered
to compact the gold. The second pellet is added
onto the first one along the distopulpal line angle.
This pellet is also first adapted by hand pressure
followed by malleting in the disto-bucco-pulpal
direction. Additional pellets are similarly
condensed until two-thirds or more of the line
angle is covered.
The rest of the pellet is wedged into the
distolinguopulpal point angle using forces in a
distolinguopulpal direction. This creates a bar of
gold running between two point angles.
The concavity in the center is filled with medium-
to large-sized pellets by directing forces in the four
corners separately. Additional increments are
similarly added in a concave form and the
cavosurface margins covered with gold. The
remaining depression in the central area is finally
restored to a slight excess. The completed
restoration is burnished, contoured and polished
(Fig. 15.19A to G).

Class II Cavity
The outline form is similar in size and shape to the
preparation for amalgam. The occlusal portion of the
Class II preparation is similar to the Class I cavity.
The proximal box is placed in dentin and is in the
form of a cone. The width of the proximal box at the
gingival portion is only slightly greater than at the
occlusal portion. All the walls of the proximal box
are extended minimally in their respective
embrasures.
Retention is provided by sharp line angles and
point angles (differs from an amalgam preparation,
which has slightly rounded angles). These angles are
accentuated with Hoe and gingival marginal
trimmers. Cavosurface bevels of 30–40° are placed on
all the margins (compared to an amalgam
preparation, which ends in butt joints) (Fig. 15.20A).
Restoration
A matrix is necessary for restoring Class II
preparations which would serve as a proximal
support. A small shim steel matrix band, which
extends 1.0 mm above the marginal ridge and 2.0–3.0
mm beyond the proximal contact is placed
interproximally. The matrix is secured with a wedge.
Acrylic resin in the doughy stage is adapted and
wedged into the facial and lingual embrasures, such
that the matrix does not move.
Restoration of the cavity is carried out using a
combination of mat gold and gold foil.

Class III Cavity
For preparing Class III cavity, numerous designs, such
as Ferrier design, Loma Linda design, Ingraham
design, Lund, Baum and Woodbury design have been
suggested. Prior to any instrumentation or restoration
of the cavity, separation is carried out with a Ferrier’s
separator. A minimal separation of 0.25–0.50 mm is
considered adequate.
The routinely followed designs are as follows.
i. Ferrier Design (Fig. 15.21)
This design is indicated for small carious lesions with
sufficient amount of labial, lingual and incisal walls.
Ferrier’s design is commonly used on the distal
surfaces of anterior teeth, which are less conspicuous
as compared to mesial.
The general outline form is roughly triangular in
shape. With a No. 1 round bur, the carious lesion is
approached from the facial surface. A suitable
inverted cone bur is used to extend the marginal
outline form.
From the facial view, gingival 4/5th of the facial
margin is straight and parallel to the external contour,
and in the incisal 1/5th, it curves gently to meet the
incisal margin. When viewed proximofacially, the
facial outline follows the buccal contour of the tooth
and meet the gingival margin in a slight obtuse angle.
Gingival margin is straight faciolingually and placed
beneath the free gingiva, if possible.
The incisal margin is extended incisally to the
contact area, providing access for instrumentation.
The axial wall extends 0.5 mm into dentin and is
flat faciolingually and incisogingivally. Retention is
provided by the inwards sloping dentinal portion of
the gingival wall, i.e. an acute axiogingival line angle
and an incisal undercut placed into dentin directed
facioincisally. A small bibeveled hatchet is used for
establishing the incisal undercut. Small angle formers
are used to sharpen the linguoaxiogingival and
facioaxiogingival line angles and for creating the acute
axiogingival line angle.
A bevel is placed on all the enamel margins using
a Wedelstaedt chisel.
ii. Loma Linda Design
The Loma Linda design favors a lingual access. It is
indicated in those areas where esthetics is the major
concern.
Cavosurface bevels and flares are not needed for
these preparations and the walls should terminate in
butt cavosurface margins. Retention is provided by
grooves in three opposing directions using a suitable
round bur. A suitable inverted cone bur may be used
for placing incisal retention area in the incisal point
angle. The other two grooves in the form of a cylinder
(1.0–1.5 mm deep) are given on the gingival wall.
iii. Ingraham Design
Ingraham design is indicated primarily for the
incipient proximal lesions in anterior teeth where
esthetics is the major concern. Maintenance of oral
hygiene and low caries and plaque indices are
essential requirements when using this design.
The general form of the cavity is parallelogram in
shape.
Retention grooves are placed on the incisoaxial and
gingivoaxial line angles, such that their depth
decreases towards the lingual surface. This would

Restoration
The prepared cavity is filled with mat gold along with
veneering gold foil as described.
After the cavity is filled two-thirds, the incisal
portion is restored. Building the incisal area is known
as ‘making the turn’ and is accomplished as follows:
(i) Sufficient gold is compacted on the lingual wall,
such that it is near to the incisal angle, (ii) Small-sized
pellets (1/128) are used to fill the incisal area with
hand condenser, (iii) Gold foil pellets are compacted
into the linguoincisal and incisal area with an offset
condenser.
Polishing is avoided as a surface devoid of high
lustre will reflect less light and be more esthetic
(Fig. 15.21).
Class V Cavity
The most commonly followed Class V cavity design
is the Ferrier’s design. External outline is trapezoidal
in form. Straight lines are preferred in the preparation
as these are readily discernible when removing excess
gold at the margins.
Occlusally, the margin is straight and parallel to
the occlusal plane of the tooth. It extends beyond the
carious lesion to sound tooth structure and is limited
mesiodistally by the proximal line angles. Similarly,
the gingival margin is straight and parallel to the
occlusal plane of the tooth but shorter than the
occlusal margin. It extends gingivally beyond the
carious lesion and mesiodistally limited to the line
angles of the tooth.
The axial wall is straight occlusogingivally and
parallel to the facial surface of the tooth. Mesio-
distally, it has a slight curvature similar to the external
contour of the tooth. Axial wall forms a sharp right
angle with the occlusal wall, an acute angle with the
gingival wall and obtuse angles with the mesial and
distal walls. The divergence of mesial and distal walls
towards the surface prevents undermining the
enamel.
The acute axiogingival line angle is the key to
retentive feature. It is prepared with a No. 6½–2½–9
hoe, working from the cavosurface to the axial wall.
Sharp line angles and point angles aid in retention.
Starting points or convenience points in the form of
coves or triangles are prepared incisally and
gingivally at the point angles using suitable inverted
cone bur. A slight cavosurface bevel is given on all
the margins using a Wedelstaedt chisel. A bevel on
the gingival wall is avoided, especially when gingival
wall extends to the cementum.

the gingival contour (Fig. 15.22A).
