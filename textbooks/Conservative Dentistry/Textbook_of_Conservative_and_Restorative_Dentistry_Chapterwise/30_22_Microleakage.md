The goal of operative dentistry, undoubtedly, is to
restore the tooth to its form and functions. One of the
requisites of a restorative material is to adapt itself to
cavity walls. Among the various restorative materials
currently used, and despite of the tremendous
improvements in means and technologies, none of the
material could actually bond chemically with the
tooth surface. The gap left between the cavity walls
and the restorative material plays an important role
in the prognosis of the restorative treatments. In the
past, pulpal reactions to dental procedures were
thought to be induced by mechanical irritation, like
heat, vibration, galvanism, etc. and/or chemical
irritation by the restorative material and its
components. Various authors have demonstrated that
the bacterial leakage was a greater threat to the pulp
than the toxicity of restorative materials. Since then
the concept of microleakage has drawn widespread
attention. Different authors have termed ‘leakage’ as
marginal percolation, liquid diffusion, fluid exchange,
capillary penetration, etc.
Microleakage can be defined as “the clinically
undetectable passage of bacteria and bacterial products,
fluids, molecules or ions from the oral environment along
the various gaps present in the cavity–restoration interface”.
This interface can be composed of tooth, smear
layer, filling and the cementing medium (if any).
There are three possible routes of microleakage:
(1) Within or via the smear layer, (2) between the
smear layer and the cavity varnish/cement, and
(3) between the cavity varnish/cement and the
restoration (Fig. 22.1). The cavity–restoration interface
is not a fixed, inert or an impenetrable border; but a
dynamic microcrevice, which allows free movement
of  ions  and  molecules.  It  has  been established that
a minimum of 10.0 m space is left at the tooth–
restoration interface even after employing the
adhesive liners and materials.
Microleakage is hence a serious clinical problem
that requires thorough analysis and discussion
because all restorative materials exhibit varying
degrees of leakage at the margins.
CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS OF MICROLEAKAGE
1. Postoperative sensitivity: Microleakage often
results in dentin sensitivity by creating a direct
communication between oral fluids and the pulp.
Change in local ionic concentrations, caused by
leakage of acids and other substances, facilitates
movement of fluid in the dentinal tubules, leading
to pain. Postoperative sensitivity is more pronounced
in proximal/cervical cavities and more common
with resin restorations.
2. Secondary caries/recurrent caries: Carious lesions
that exist along the walls of a restored cavity can
be attributed to the existing microleakage at the
tooth–restoration interface. Bacteria with an
average diameter less than 1.0 m can easily penetrate
such gaps. A usual gap of more than 10 m
provides enough space to house nutrients for
thriving of bacteria; hence, inviting recurrent caries.
3. Pulpal pathology: Marginal gaps around
restorations may become sufficiently wide to allow
in-growth of bacteria. These bacteria may produce
a number of inflammatory components, which

RESTORATIVE MATERIALS AND MICROLEAKAGE
Microleakage is a useful indicator of the microscopic
and anatomic aspects of bond between restorative
material and the tooth. Black has stressed that the
filling materials should be closely adapted to the
cavity walls and should withstand thermal changes
once placed in the oral environment. However, no
restorative material can chemically bond to the tooth
structure providing perfect seal, which is capable of
withstanding the temperature fluctuations of oral
environment. Good marginal adaptation requires
adequate physical and mechanical properties of the
filling material and its careful manipulation. The
properties of restorative materials that contribute
largely to microleakage are:
i. Coefficient of thermal expansion
ii. Polymerization shrinkage
iii. Adhesion
Other properties like creep, elasticity, resistance to
fatigue failures, solubility, etc. also contribute to a
minor extent.
i. Coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE): It is the
change in length per unit length of a material per
degree change in temperature. Both linear and
volumetric coefficient of thermal expansion are
important in restorative materials. On cooling
(reduction in temperature), there occurs contraction
of the material; conversely, heating (increasing
temperature) leads to expansion. Ideally, a
restorative material should have a CTE, which
closely matches that of tooth structure, so that
there occurs an equal expansion and contraction
of both. In case of varying CTE between the two,
one may expand/contract more than the other
during a change in temperature, leading to a leaky
restoration margin, i.e. microleakage. The greater
the difference between CTE of two materials,
greater will be the microleakage. From the
Table 22.1, one can evaluate that an acrylic
restoration will show a greater microleakage as
compared to glass-ionomer or direct gold
restorations. It has also been emphasized that
marginal percolation can be because of the changes
in volume of fluid occupying the space between
tooth and the restoration, subsequent to thermal
changes.
ii. Polymerization shrinkage or volumetric shrinkage:
It occurs with polymeric materials. When the
monomer chains are polymerized to form polymer
chain, there occurs a decrease in volume and an
increase in density. (For the same mass, if density
increases, volume has to reduce since Density =
Mass/Volume). The shrinkage tends to pull the
material away from the cavity walls. If an
intermediate adhesive resin is used to bond the
restorative resin to the tooth and the contraction
stresses generated are very high, there may occur
a break in the adhesive bond resulting in
promotion of microleakage. The polymerization
shrinkage of different polymeric materials is
depicted in Table 22.2.
iii. Adhesion: It is the attraction of the molecules of
two different substances to each other, when they
are brought in close contact. Lack of adhesion
between restorative material and the tooth may
allow penetration of the external materials at a
microscopic level. Adhesion is influenced by the
wetting capabilities, surface energy, presence of
water and smear layer, difference in composition

Role of Smear Layer
During cavity preparation, the microcrystalline debris
may get embedded within the denatured collagen of
dentin; these deposits formed on the cut surfaces is
known as ‘smear layer’. It is generally 1.0–2.0 m thick
and may consist of blood, saliva, bacteria, enamel and
dentin particles. Some of the initial cutting debris may
also be pushed into the dentinal tubules by almost
1.0–5.0 m forming the ‘smear plugs’. The smear layer
and the smear plugs serve as a functional unit
(Figs 22.3 and 22.4) to reduce dentin permeability and
thereby protect the pulp. However, the role of smear
layer in preventing or promoting microleakage is still
unclear. There is divergence of opinion as regard the
role of smear layer in microleakage.
One opinion is to leave the smear layer intact on
the tooth surface, because it may act as a physical
barrier in preventing the ingress of bacteria and their
products into the underlying dentin. Pashley (1991)
has reported a decrease of 86% in the permeability of
dentin, when the smear layer is present. Clinicians
supplementing this view should keep in mind that
the smear layer is acid labile at a pH of 6.0–6.8 and
less. It is inadvertently destroyed when the pH drops,
e.g. around plaque accumulated areas, or by
proteolytic enzymes excreted by certain micro-
organisms or by acid etching. Smear layer on an
exposed root surface dissolves more readily as
compared to one present on the occlusal surface.
A few authors opine removal of the smear layer
because of the belief that the smear layer may contain
many bacteria. It has been demonstrated that the
smear layer may be capable of preventing diffusion
of bacteria, but not the bacterial products. A decrease
in microleakage was observed when the smear layer
was absent (Removal of smear layer permits a good
adaptation of the restorative material to the cavity
walls).
It is preferred to remove the natural smear layer
and replace it with a ‘sterile, inert and non-toxic’
synthetic smear layer. Research in this area has
shown promising results. The smear layer is partially
removed with weak acid leaving smear plugs. Weak
acids, such as, tannic acid and polyacrylic acid may
remove only the smear layer, leaving behind intact
smear plugs, thereby allowing better adaptation of
the restorative material to the cavity walls. The
newer generation dentin bonding systems removes
only the smear layer while retaining intratubular
plugs.

Factors Controlling Bacterial Penetration at
Tooth–Restoration Gaps
Bacterial entry into the tooth–restoration gaps is
affected by the size and nature of the gap, defence
mechanisms of the host and also quality of the
restorative material. A schematic representation of
this relationship is shown in Fig. 22.5.
1. Size and nature of the gap: The size of the gap at
the tooth–restoration interface varies, ranging from
10–50 m. Even a small gap of 10 m is sufficient
for the entry of oral micro-organisms. These gaps
are usually sufficiently wide to house bacteria,
provide continuous nutrition and facilitate their
growth. After establishing  themselves  in  this  area,
the organisms may produce a number of
components, which on penetration through the
dentinal tubules could adversely affect the
underlying dental pulp.
Nature of the gaps cannot be described in
physiochemical terms. However, the self-sealing
capabilities of the restorative material to a certain
extent can reduce bacterial penetration. Self-sealing
may occur because of deposition of mineral salts
of low solubility in the space, accumulation of
corrosion products, calcification of the debris
around the margins, etc.
2. Host defense factors: Natural defense mechanisms
of the tooth, like sclerosis of dentinal tubules and
reparative dentin formation, decrease bacterial
penetration. The hydrostatic pressure of the pulp
is higher as compared to the outside pressure of
the oral cavity. This pressure difference pushes the
dentinal fluid outwards, which opposes the
bacteria and their products moving inwards,
though only to a negligible extent. Some plasma
proteins in the dentinal fluid may act as antibacterial;
whereas, large molecular weight proteins, like
fibrinogen, make dentin less permeable to bacteria.
Presence of smear plugs also decreases the diffusion
of bacteria to the pulp. Hence, the process of acid
etching, which removes these plugs and opens
dentinal tubules, increases their chances of
penetration. Alteration of the chemical structure
of dentin by leaching of tin/mercury ions from
amalgam or fluoride ions from glass-ionomer also
checks bacterial diffusion.
3. Quality of the restorative material: Certain
restorations, like glass-ionomers, compomers, etc.
may release fluorides into the gaps. Fluorides have
an antimicrobial effect and thereby reduce ingress
of bacteria into the gaps. The elements released
from amalgam (corrosion products) significantly
close the tooth–restoration gap. Resin cements used
in indirect restorations effectively close the gap.
MICROLEAKAGE AND RESTORATIVE MATERIALS
a. Microleakage around Amalgam Restorations
Amalgam when freshly condensed does not adapt
closely to the walls of the prepared cavity. Generally,
a gap of 10–15 m exists around the restoration.
However, the adaptation to the cavity wall improves
with time; referred to as a ‘self-sealing restoration’. This
is by virtue of accumulation of corrosion products in
the interface with the passage of time. The corrosion
products are the oxides and chlorides of tin, mostly
occurring in the low copper alloys. However, high
copper alloys confer a greater resistance to corrosion.
Because of this increased resistance and a slower rate
of forming corrosion products, microleakage in the
high copper restorations continues for a longer period
of time.
Microleakage, as a result of dimensional changes
in amalgam, is minimal. During setting of the
conventional mercury/alloy mix, a small contraction
is seen initially, when the mercury is consumed. This
is followed by a small expansion as the crystalline
matrix is formed. Modern alloys including high
copper alloys undergo very little net dimensional
change (±0.2% by volume) (ADA specification No.1
allows dimensional change of 20 m/cm for a set amalgam).
Measures to Reduce Microleakage
i. Different types of silver alloys exhibit different
levels of microleakage. Spherical alloys display
more microleakage as compared to lathecut/
admixed alloys (Spherical alloys do not adapt
properly and show shrinkage during setting).
ii. Condensation of amalgam should be carried out
immediately after trituration. A longer time
lapse results in the loss of plasticity and
increases the chances of internal voids. Insertion
of amalgam should be carried out in increments.

b. Microleakage around Glass-Ionomer Restorations
Glass-ionomer cement adheres to the tooth structure
by forming chemical bond between carboxyl groups
of the cement and calcium ions of the tooth. Since
glass-ionomer is hydrophilic in nature, it can bond
even in the presence of a slightly wet surface. Other
properties, which contribute to minimize leakage, are:
(i) A coefficient of thermal expansion, closely matches
to tooth structure, (ii) fluoride releases into the
surrounding environment, and (iii) the ability to
renew broken ionic bonds.
Glass-ionomer cement is highly technique sensitive;
isolation from moisture, at least 30 minutes after
placement is important. On exposure to water, the
matrix forming ions are easily leached out during the
initial set, which could interfere at tooth–restoration
interface. Solubility in oral fluids continues to be a
problem for 24 hours following insertion of the
material. Excessive dehydration/desiccation can
result in a crazed/cracked surface, which if extends
to the margins, could result in considerable marginal
leakage. Coating the restoration with surface
protective agents, like cavity varnishes or unfilled
resins, is recommended for protecting against
dehydration. Once the cement has attained maturity,
it becomes resistant to solubility and disintegration
in the oral fluids. Another factor that may harm the
marginal integrity of the restoration is the use of sharp
hand instruments for finishing, before the material
has completely set.
Measures to Reduce Microleakage
i. Proper manipulation and placement of glass-
ionomer cement on clean dentinal surfaces (Lower
powder: liquid ratio may increase the solubility
of the cement).
ii. Rotary instruments are preferred for finishing
restoration margins. Hand instruments may tear
away the material at the margins resulting in
marginal ditching.
iii. During finishing, restoration surfaces should be
coated with vaseline or petroleum jelly to protect
from moisture contamination and desiccation.
iv. Two coats of varnish or a layer of unfilled resin
should be applied over the restoration. Varnish is
semipermeable in nature as compared to unfilled
resin, which is more resistant to water contami-
nation. Despite of this fact, a varnish application
is preferred as it closely adheres to the surrounding
surface of enamel, preventing any moisture from
reaching the glass–ionomer–tooth interface.
v. Prior conditioning of the tooth surface before
insertion of the cement increases the bonding and
reduces the microleakage. Various conditioning

Resin-modified glass-ionomer cements, because of

Moreover, less water and less carboxylic acid content

marginal leakage. To a certain extent, expansion due
to water uptake, which is a function of the resin
component of cement, compensates for the initial resin
shrinkage and reduces microleakage.
c. Microleakage around Composite Restorations
Composite resins do not possess the quality of
bonding to either enamel or dentin. The inability to
bond, coupled with marked polymerization shrinkage
and thermal expansion/contraction predispose to
marginal leakage. Prior treatment of the tooth surface,
like acid etching/conditioning and the use of bonding
agents, facilitates margin adaptability for composite
restorations. In enamel, such treatment leads to an
unparalleled success in preventing microleakage.
Conversely, the bond to dentin, though substantially
reduces microleakage, but does not protect the
dentin–restoration interface completely. The adequate
adhesion is limited by a number of factors, like vitality
of the dentin, difference in physical and chemical
composition of dentin as compared to enamel,
presence of dentinal fluid, smear layer, etc.
Development of internal stresses from polymerization
shrinkage and thermal effects also have a detrimental
effect on the bond.
The most notable factors that contribute to
marginal leakage of composites are:
1. Composite restorations are technique sensitive.
Any inadvertent step can lead to failure, including
an increase in marginal leakage.
2. Marginal gaps at the tooth–restoration interface are
primarily due to polymerization shrinkage of the
setting resin. After the setting, the gaps are further
affected by masticatory forces, thermal changes and
water sorption.
The features affecting gap formation are:
i. The volumetric polymerization shrinkage in
composite restorations is in the range of 1.67–
5.68% (the lesser being for the light-activated
ones).
In case of using bonding agents, shrinkage
results in the development of tensile/shear
stresses at the tooth–restoration interface.
Within certain limits, the adhesive bond is able
to withstand these stresses. Once the stresses
exceed the bond strength and the plastic or
elastic deformation of the combined system, a
separation at the tooth–restoration interface
may occur leading to microleakage. It is usually
the bond with dentin which is compromised
during shrinkage; whereas, the bond with
enamel is sufficiently strong to withstand the
same amount of forces.
ii. Functional occlusal stresses may enhance
because of repeated plastic or  elastic
deformation of the restoration.
iii. The marked difference in the coefficient of
thermal expansion of restorative resins (22–25
× 10–6/°C) and tooth structure (11.4 × 10–6/°C)
has a detrimental effect on adhesion.
iv. Composite resin restorations have a tendency to
absorb water from the environment, causing the
restoration to expand (water sorption counteract
polymerization shrinkage to a little extent). In
contrast to the polymerization contraction
stresses, which are generated at a rapid rate, relief
of stresses by hygroscopic expansion proceeds
slowly. The resins with the largest quantity of
filler have the least water sorption (Water uptake
is the property of the resin component of the
matrix). Though water sorption may improve
marginal adaptation of composite resin, it
impairs its mechanical properties.
v. Marginal gaps are higher on the cervical
margins of a Class II restoration; reasons being:
• Placing the restorative material at these areas
is difficult.
• Entrapment of air during placement.
• Difficulty during condensation because of the
pull back of sticky material.
• Inadequate bonding to the gingival wall due
to polymerization shrinkage.

Prepolymerized composite balls, substituted for
the glass inserts, however, showed significantly
greater microleakage.
ii. Cavity design: Cavity design for composites
should be as conservative as possible. Being
adhesive to the underlying tooth structure,
retentive and extensive preparations are not
required. Placement of bevels, reduced depths and
rounded internal angles are effective in providing
good marginal adaptation and reducing leakage
(Fig. 22.6).
Not only the size, but the shape of the cavity is
also an important factor in determining the width
of the marginal gap. This is illustrated in Fig. 22.7,
which shows three dentin cavities of identical
diameter but of different shapes. The cavity to the
right is V shaped and the cavity to the left is box
shaped. When the cavities are filled with
composite resin without any  bonding agent, the
marginal gap is reduced from 13 m to 4.0 m from
left to right; whereas, when dentin bonding system
is applied, the gap width is in the order of 3.0 m
to almost nil. This can be explained on the
decreasing ratio of volume/bonded area from 1.0
for the box-shaped cavity to 0.4 for the V-shaped
cavity.
The role of bevels on cavosurface margins in
reducing leakage remained controversial. The
principle favoring bevels is to increase the surface
area for enamel bonding and reduces
microleakage. Occlusal beveling is usually not
required because of the enamel rod direction and
the possibility of extending the material to load-
bearing areas. Beveling of cervical margins in
extensive Class II preparations may result in
removing the remaining enamel. Placement of
bevels is recommended mainly on the accessible
facial and lingual margins of the proximal box.
iii. Acid etching and bonding: Acid etching removes
surface contaminants, raises the surface energy
and reactivity of enamel and increases the surface
area for bonding. The bonding agent is drawn by
capillary attraction into the microporosities
created by acid etching. These polymer tags
provide micromechanical interlocking with
enamel, thereby reducing microleakage to almost
nil.
A shortcoming with this technique is its
dependence on the presence of thick enamel at the
cavity walls. The effectiveness of marginal seal is
compromised by the position and surface
structure of enamel as it anatomically varies in
different areas of the same tooth. The results are
obvious when the restoration margins are located
in the cervical enamel. Cervical enamel is thin,
irregular in prism structure and devoid of
characteristic prism markings. Therefore, the bond
to cervical enamel is not very intimate as
compared to the occlusal enamel.

iv. Restorative procedure: Placing composite resin in
small increments is recommended to control
polymerization shrinkage and the contraction gap
at the margins (Bulk placement shows poor
adaptation). Thickness of one increment should
preferably be limited to 1.0–1.5 mm. Different
incremental placement designs have been
described in Chapter 19.
The concept of soft start polymerization has been
advocated  to  reduce  marginal  gap  and improve
marginal integrity. The procedure involves a short
prepolymerization at low intensity followed by the
final cure at high intensity. This allows composite
resin to flow during the initial setting period,
thereby minimizing the stresses induced at the
interface. Prolonging the curing time of a
composite resin improves upon the microleakage
by allowing a greater inversion rate of the
monomer component.
v. Direction of the light source: It is hypothesized
that the polymerization shrinkage of setting
composite resin is directed towards the light
source. Because of the inadequate access of the
light source in proximal areas and also since the
curing tip is usually directed from the occlusal
aspect, the gingival increment shrinks occlusally.
Use of curing aids like light directing wedges,
flexible light guides and focusing tips can facilitate
better curing in areas of poor access. A three-sited
light curing technique for better adaptation of
conventional Class II composite restorations has
been advocated (Fig. 22.8).
vi. Sealing the marginal gaps: After finishing and
polishing of composite restoration, an unfilled low
viscosity resin is applied to the margins by slowly
brushing the resin laterally from one side to
another. Materials used for the purpose of sealing
gaps are the commercially available pit and fissure
sealants. Sealing marginal gaps has shown
promising results in reducing marginal leakage.
vii.Finishing process: Finishing techniques and their
timings affect the ability of restorative materials
to resist leakage. Most of the authors advocate
finishing of composites to be delayed for 24 hours,
until the polymerization is complete. If done
before the initial setting, it may break the bond
between resin and the tooth. Finishing after one
day was considered acceptable as it resulted in
almost no microleakage. However, the delayed
finishing after one week can be detrimental to the
already formed marginal seal of the restorations.
Stresses, which disrupt the adhesive bonds, are
always generated during the finishing process.
Variations in the finishing techniques does affect
leakage. Dry finishing techniques lead to increased
leakage. It is, therefore, recommended that rotary
instruments should be used at slow speeds using
light intermittent strokes and with generous  air
coolants.  Use of Soflex discs for contouring and
finishing has shown the best marginal quality for
composite restorations.
viii. Use of sandwich technique: Glass-ionomer
cements under composite resins have been
successful in reducing microleakage. Bond of
glass-ionomer cement to enamel is weaker as

1. Penetration Methods
a. Dye Penetration
The use of organic dyes is one of the oldest and most
commonly used methods for detection of micro-
leakage. The dye has a contrasting color vis-à-vis the
tooth and the restoration (Fig. 22.10A, B). Agents most
frequently used for this purpose are: Methylene blue,
India ink, crystal violet, fluorescein, rhodamine  B,
eosin, basic  fuchsin, erythrosin, etc. These dyes are
available either as solutions or particle suspensions
of different particle sizes. They should not bond either
with the tooth substance or the restoration and should
be color stable under all conditions of investigation.
The technique involves immersion of extracted and
restored tooth in a dye solution for a predetermined
period. After the stipulated time, the tooth is removed,
washed, sectioned and examined under a microscope
to establish the extent of penetration of dye. The
results are quantified by assigning numbers to the
defined depths of penetration.
The validity of using dyes is, however, questioned
because of the reasons, such as:
• With a variety of dyes having different particle
sizes, it cannot be expected that consistent results
will be obtained even if standardized techniques
are used. It has been shown that different
concentrations of two dyes can vary in penetration
times from 5 minutes to 1 hour.
• A potential source of error exists when the dye
binds to the tooth substance or restorative material,
e.g. basic fuchsin dyes have shown to preferentially
bind with the carious dentin. Therefore, when
attempting to demonstrate a space between the
tooth and restoration, an area of stained carious
dentin can potentially be mistaken for a larger gap
than actually exists.
• It is recommended that dye penetration studies
should be carried out in vacuum; however, it is
difficult to eliminate trapped air in small spaces.
• Dyes that are not color stable may lead to
misinterpretation of results. For example, aniline
blue becomes colorless in alkaline conditions, such
as in presence of calcium hydroxide.
The assessment of results is only subjective, which
is a major disadvantage with this method.
b. Chemical Tracers
Chemical tracers rely upon the reaction between one
or more chemicals used. The technique involves
reaction of two colorless compounds to produce an
opaque precipitate, usually a silver salt. It is essential
that both the chemicals penetrate the margin,
(Precipitation does not occur, if only one of the two
molecules penetrates.)
The procedure followed is the same, i.e. extracted
tooth with restoration is immersed in a 50% silver
nitrate solution, which is subsequently reacted with
photographic developer (benzene hydroquinone).
1.0% silver chloride has also been used. The limitations
of chemical tracers are similar to dye penetration
studies.
c. Radioactive Isotopes
Radioactive isotopes, such as 45Ca, 131I, 32P, 14C, 35S,
86Rb, etc. are used in a manner similar to that of dyes
for assessing microleakage. The specimens are
autoradiographed to detect the tracer. The isotope can
detect even minute amount of marginal leakage, since
the size of the isotope molecules is only 40 nm
compared to the smallest size of the dye particles
(120 nm).
Limitations of this technique are:
• Subjective assessment of results; however, with the
use of stereomicroscope, the subjectivity can be
minimized.
• A high energy isotope produces scattering on the
film leading to an increase in apparent leakage.
Hence, isotopes with low energies should be
preferred for resolution.

The possibility of bacteria penetrating through or

The restored teeth are immersed in cultured broths;

2. Air Pressure Technique
The air pressure technique (device used to test marginal

Compressed air is introduced towards the tooth-

margins provides a subjective view of marginal

Advantages
• Tooth need not be destroyed.
• Result can be quantified.
Disadvantages
• Inability to use in-vivo
• Does not represent the clinical situation and also
does not take into account the drying effect of
compressed air.
• It is possible that some air may leak before it enters
the tooth.
3. Fluid Filtration Method
The pressurized-liquid is applied onto the restored
tooth with a constant pressure generated by a gas
system. The teeth are sectioned and connected to a
plastic tube, filled with de-ionized water. A standard
glass capillary tube is connected to the plastic tube at
the outlet of the specimen. The water is sucked back
from the capillary tube creating air bubbles. The whole
set-up is placed in water bath at a constant
temperature. An adjusted pressure is applied from
the inlet side to force the water through the void along
the filling, thereby displacing the air bubble in the
capillary tube. The volume of fluid transported is
measured by observing the movement of the air
bubble (Movement of air bubbles are best measured
by computer-controlled diode laser beam) (Fig. 22.12).
Advantages
• Samples are not destroyed.
• Results are automatically recorded; no bias.
• Accurate results, because very small volume can
be recorded.
• No need of any tracer; overcomes the problem of
molecular size.
Disadvantage
• Standardization of pressure, vis-a-vis, movement
is difficult.

4. Electrochemical Method
The method involves the insertion of an electrode into
the extracted tooth in such a way that it contacts the
base of the restoration. The tooth is suitably sealed to
prevent any electrical leakage through natural tooth
structure and then immersed in an electrolytic bath.
A potential is applied between the tooth and the bath,
and the leakage is assessed by measuring the current
flowing across the interface.
Drawbacks
• Not suitable for metallic restorations
• Inability to use in vivo.
5. Microscopic Examination
Sequential microscopic sections are required to study
dental tissues in three dimensions. Routine sectioning
is time consuming and may lead to wastage. In order
to overcome these limitations, acetate peel technique
is used, wherein tooth tissue is ground with
carborundum powder on a glass plate. A thin layer
so produced is dried and mounted for examination
under microscope.
a. Scanning Electron Microscopy
The scanning electron microscope (SEM) provides
direct visual observation of the tooth–restoration
interface because of its high magnification and depth.
The low vacuum SEM evaluates the rubber base
impression directly, decreasing the number of steps
and the possible chances of inaccuracy.
Limitation
• Potential to induce artefacts during specimen
preparations.
b. Fluorescent Microscopy
Fluorescent microscopy uses UV radiation on selected
dye, which is capable of absorbing these radiations at
one wavelength and emitting at different wavelength.
Fluorescent dyes are detectable in dilute
concentrations sensitive to UV light, easy to photo-
graph, permitted more reproducible results and not
expensive. The contrast of the natural fluorescence of
the tooth against that of dye provided a contrast that
made it easy to detect the path of dye penetration
under UV light.
c. Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT)
Optical coherence tomography (OCT) has successfully
evaluated restoration margins and cracks at the
margins (width and depth of the gaps). The amount
of reflection of infrared light from the margin gaps
can be quantified.

a. Artificial Caries
Artificial caries like lesion is produced in vitro using
bacterial cultures. The lesions produced along the
cavity walls and under the  cavity walls are assessed.
The histological appearance of such lesions under
polarized light shows two parts: An ‘outer lesion’ and
a ‘cavity wall’ lesion. The outer lesion exhibits the
features of primary attack, while the cavity wall lesion
was formed by microleakage of ions from the caries-
inducing medium into the tooth–restoration interface.
Advantages
• Microleakage can be directly related to the
development of artificial lesion.
• Quantification of results is possible using depth of
the lesion as the measurable parameter (If degree
of demineralization is used as a measure, it
provides only subjective assessment).

The suspected margins of the restorations are

• Subjectivity regarding the pain perception of the

NANOLEAKAGE
Nanoleakage implies leakage around the collagen
fibrils within the hybrid layer that have not been
completely infiltrated by resin. Leakage may be seen
at the bottom of the hybrid layer and/or scattered
along its entire width, depending upon the bonding
system employed. It appears that bonding systems
that etch deeper into dentin are likely to show higher
degrees of nanoleakage. This could be the possible
reason for reduced degree of leakage observed with
self-etching/self-priming system than with systems
that use an acidic conditioner as a separate step. The
clinical significance of nanoleakage indicates the
inability of the adhesive resin to completely infiltrate
the demineralized dentin, thereby leaving behind
pores and spaces. Penetration pathways in
nanoleakage are porosities in a range less than 50 m.
Poorly infiltrated hybrid layer contains voids that
predispose to accumulation of water and oral fluids,
which may accelerate the degradation of the bond.
When demineralized dentin is infiltrated with resin,
its modulus of elasticity is much higher than the
demineralized dentin. If the hybrid layer fails to be
penetrated completely, its modulus of elasticity might
fall in between that of adhesive resin and the un-
infiltrated dentin collagen. Contact with water and
oral fluids would provide a gradual decrease in the
modulus of elasticity of the bonded interface reducing
the stress concentration in this area. Due to slow
hydrolysis, the integrity is not preserved for a longer
periods. Therefore, dentin bonding agents, may
reduce microleakage initially, their long-term
performance under stress and continued exposure to
oral fluids is questionable.
The techniques used to determine nanoleakage are as
follows.
1. Tracer (Dyes)
Several silver dyes have been used to test the sealing
ability of dentin adhesives; such as, silver nitrate,
silver methenamine and ammonical silver nitrate. It
is shown that the hybrid layer is somewhat porous
and is accessible to a silver dye even in the absence of
marginal gap. 50% aqueous solution of silver nitrate
is commonly used. Nanoleakage is a significant
problem even with the newer adhesives. A few
authors have confirmed that the penetration of tracer
is because of incomplete resin penetration within the
hybrid layers; leakage may be because of increased
porosities and high hydrophilicity of the adhesives.
The tracer dyes are analyzed within the hybrid layer
using the microscopes.

2. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)
The scanning electron microsocpy (SEM) provides a
mean of direct visual observation of the hybrid layer
because of its high magnification and depth of focus.
Even the submicron defects can be observed at
required magnification, such as 200× or 1000×.
Limitations
• Drying of specimens, dehydration and embedding,
which is needed for SEM analysis may result in
artefacts.
• Cutting/breaking the sample may influence the
surface morphology.
3. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) is an
imaging technique, whereby a beam of electrons is
transmitted through a specimen; the image so formed
is magnified and directed to appear either on a
fluorescent screen or layer of photographic film, or  a
CCD  sensor. TEM can resolve object details smaller
than 1.0 nm, effectively analyse nanoleakage.
4. Field Emission Scanning Electron
Microscopy (FESEM)
A field-emission scanning electron microscope
provides narrower probing beams, resulting in
improved spatial resolution and minimal sample
damage. FESEM produces clearer, less electro-
statically distorted images with spatial resolution
down to 1.5 nm, i.e. 3 to 6 times better than conventional
SEM.
5. Confocal Laser Scanning
Electron Microscopy (CLSEM)
Confocal laser scanning electron microscopy (CLSEM)
merges the capabilities of the scanning, transmission
and X-ray microscopes, and achieves unprecedented
resolutions in optically dense materials, by
implementing the technology of confocal imaging into
an electron microscope.
CLSEM has also been used in combination with
fluorescent dye (rhodamine B) to determine
nanoleakage. The  advantages of CLSEM are that, it
is a non-destructive examination and drying of the
samples is not necessary.
