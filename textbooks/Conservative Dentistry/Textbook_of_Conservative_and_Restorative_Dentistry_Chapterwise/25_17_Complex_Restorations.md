The restoration of extensively damaged tooth is
always challenging. Many a times, the damage
involves half or more than half of the tooth structure.
The remaining tooth structure is not sufficient to
retain the restoration and require modification of the
classic preparation design. The earlier authors defined
complex restorations as the restorations involving
more than two tooth surfaces. The lesion on two or
more than two surfaces, if small, the restoration can
not be designated as ‘complex’. The revised and
accepted definition is lesions, which require extra
retentive device for their retention, are labeled as ‘complex
restorations’.
The destruction of tooth structure, vis-à-vis, the
remaining tooth structure is the decisive factor.
Location, as well as extent of coronal destruction,
plays an important role in selection of restorative
material and design of the cavity preparation.
Location of destruction is categorized as ‘peripheral’
(axial surfaces of the teeth, Fig. 17.1); ‘central’ (central
portion of the tooth, Fig. 17.2); and ‘combined’
(incorporates both the surfaces, Fig. 17.3).
Minimal destruction of tooth structure in central,
peripheral and combined locations can be restored
with an amalgam or composite resin restoration. An
amalgam or composite resin restoration can also be
employed on moderately damaged teeth. However,
combined destruction may require onlay, if the
potential for fracture of unsupported cusp is evident
(Table 17.1).
Severe destruction of the crown of posterior teeth
can be treated by placement of pin-retained amalgam
or composite resin core; followed by restoration of
veneer crown.

created on the pulpal and/or cervical wall. This

ii. Grooves/locks: The locks are given in direct
restorations while the grooves are given in indirect

groove and the lock is that the lock extends up to
the pulpal wall (from cervical to pulpal) and the
groove extends up to the cavosurface margin
(from cervical to cavosurface). The grooves
maintain the parallelism; while, the locks converge
occlusally. The grooves/locks are given at
axiobuccal and axiolingual line angles, inside
dentinoenamel junction (Fig. 17.4B).

to lengthen the crown surgically are followed in
routine (Fig. 17.5). The exaggerated apical
placement of finish line may result in apical
migration of epithelial attachment and alveolar
crest of bone. (Alveolar crest may migrate to a level
of approximately 3.0 mm apical to the margin of the
restoration.) The crown-root ratio may also be
compromised.
iv. Pins: Pins have long been utilized for retention
purposes in restorative dentistry.
A complete knowledge about characteristics of
retentive devices is desirable for better results.

PIN-RETAINED RESTORATIONS
Conventional cavity preparation in badly mutilated
cases requires removal of large amounts of tooth
structure for obtaining retention and resistance forms.
Pins provide efficient and adequate retention to the
restorations with the least sacrifice of healthy tooth
structure.
It is emphasized that pins are auxiliary aids of
retention; should be used only if primary retentive
features are not sufficient to provide the requisite
retention.
Indications
• Badly broken down/mutilated teeth; large cavity
preparations.
• Used in foundations for full veneer restorations.
• Pin-retained cores facilitate rubber dam
application; subsequently, facilitating restorative
procedures.
Advantages
• Provide retention without the need for extensive
preparation of tooth structure.
• Increase resistance form of the preparation (some
extent).
• Relatively less time consuming than cast restorations.
• Comparatively cost-effective.
Disadvantages
• Do not increase the compressive strength of the
overlying restorative material; may reduce the
transverse and tensile strengths.
• Induce stresses in dentin, which may increase the
potential for fracture of tooth.
• Increase the chances of perforations into pulp or
on external tooth surface.
• Chances of microleakage exist at the pin–dentin
interface, if the overlying restoration leaks.
Classification
Pins can be classified as:
1. Direct pins: Direct pins, also known as non-parallel
pins, are usually made of stainless steel, silver,
titanium, etc. These pins are inserted directly into
the tooth structure; hence, need not be parallel.
2. Indirect pins: Indirect pins, also known as parallel
pins, are an integral part of a cast restoration. The
pins are held in the pinholes by means of a cement.
The pinholes are created in the dentin and the
impression of pinhole along with the rest of
preparation is cast (pin and cast in one unit).
1. Direct Pins
The direct pins are of three types:
a. Cemented pins: Cemented pins are 0.0013"–0.0023"
smaller than their pin channels and the difference
in diameter provides space for the cementing
medium. Cemented pins are generally indicated
in cases where least crazing and stresses are desired
in the remaining tooth structure, e.g. endodontically
treated teeth. It is the preferred technique for Class
IV preparations, where the pins need to be bent.
Technique: The method utilizes pins in the form of
stainless steel wires, which are threaded to the
required length using wire cutter or Dial-A-Pin
cutter. Pinholes extending 2.0–4.0 mm into dentin
are prepared using twist drills. The length should
be such that after seating pin into the pin channel
completely, the pin extends not more than 2.0–3.0
mm above the base. If necessary, the pins can be
bent with contouring pliers to keep them within
the contours of the restoration. Any roughness on
the protruding end of the pin is smoothened.

permanent marker. The pin is inserted into a pin

to apply force parallel to the axis of the pin. Do not
use cement under any circumstances.
c. Threaded pins: The diameter of threaded pins is

engage the tooth structure through their threads

pins are indicated in vital teeth and where
maximum retention is desired. These pins should
be given only when sufficient amount of dentin is

• Cemented pins are approximately 0.001"–0.002"

• Minimal microleakage at the pin–tooth interphase.

PINHOLE PREPARATION
Pinhole is prepared using a device called ‘twist drill’
(Fig. 17.13); an instrument that performs cutting when
rotated clockwise at low speeds. The recommended
speed for drilling channels is 300–500 rpm (ultralow
speed) or 1000 rpm (low speed). Very little heat is
generated at these speeds; hence, water or air cooling
is not required.
The proposed pinhole location is marked with a
marker before the pin channel is prepared with the
twist drill. A No.1/4 round bur at low speed is dipped
to about half the diameter at these marks to create
starting points, known as lead holes or pilot holes.
The purpose of these holes is to permit more accurate
positioning of the twist drill and to prevent its
skidding out laterally into unfavorable locations.
Slight pressure is applied in an apical direction
when the drill starts cutting into dentin until it
penetrates to its marked length. The drill should be
dipped only once in dentin and quickly removed
allowing only a brief period of rotation at the bottom
of the pinhole. It should be rotating when it enters
and leaves the dentin.

PINS, STRESSES AND TOOTH
Stresses are always associated with insertion of
friction locked and threaded pins in dentin. Insertion
of larger pins into smaller pin channels is possible
because of elastic properties of dentin. Once the elastic
limit of dentin is exceeded, plastic deformation occurs.
If the stresses sum up to a point where the dentin’s
elastic limit is exceeded, microscopic craze lines are
generated.
Friction-locked pins act as wedges and lateral
stresses are the main type of stresses present around
them, compared to threaded pins, which are
associated mainly with apical stresses.
The factors, which increase the stresses in dentin, are:
• Pin morphology:
– Large diameter of the pin
– Large difference between the pin and pin
channel
– Narrow-pitched threads (greater number of
threads per unit distance)
– Excessively long threads

COMPLICATIONS DURING PIN PLACEMENT
1. Drill breakage: The drill may break in following
circumstances:
• Lateral stresses during drilling
• Use of dull drills
• Drill is allowed to stop rotating while entering
or exiting from dentin.
2. Pin breakage: The factors causing pin breakage are:
• Excessive force during driving the pin into its
pin channel
• Pin is rotated after seated in the channel
• Careless bending the pin.
3. Loose pin: The pin can be loose in the channel,
because:
• Repeated insertion and removal enlarge the pin
channel
• Poor quality control between pin drill and the
pin sizes
• A self-shearing pin fails to shear; resulting in
stripped out dentin.
(A well-fitted pin in the channel may also get
loosened while shortening at a later stage).
4. Twist drill dulling: Cutting edges of a twist drill
are dulled when the drill is repeatedly used. Dull
drills have decreased cutting effectiveness, induce
more stresses in dentin, and are more prone to
breakage.
5. The pin fails to bind and shear, but keeps rotating
within its channel.
6. Heat generation: Rise in temperature, subsequent
to the use of twist drills, may be related to cutting
deep channels, high rotational speed used during
channel preparations, large-sized twist drills and
repeated insertion of pin drills into dentin.
7. Microleakage: Microleakage in dentin is evident
under all types of pins and is related to the leakage
potential of different restorative materials. With
the use of amalgam, leakage generally diminishes
in time due to accumulation of corrosion products.
On the other hand, resin restorations show a
gradual deterioration in the margin seal as a
function of time.

• Bleeding in the pinhole following removal of pin

• Lack of resistance.
• Pin continues to thread greater than its intended

• Pain is felt by the patient when the pin hole is

Radiographs can verify an external perforation
when the pin seems to project outside the tooth.
Additionally, observing the direction of a pin drill or
pin indicates a possible lateral perforation.
The incidence of pulpal and periodontal
perforations can be greatly reduced, if the clinician is
thorough with the pulpal and external anatomy of
the tooth. Correct angulation of pin drill is shown in

EFFECT OF PINS ON PULP
Pins may be placed close to the pulp or there may be
a minute pulp exposure during drilling.
Generally, the pulp responds positively to the pin
and accepts its presence without any adverse effects.
The proximity of pin channel to the pulp tends to
induce initial pulpal response, like destruction
of odontoblastic   nuclei,   vascular   dilation   and
inflammatory cell infiltrations. Inflammatory
reactions have been observed under all types of pins.
The response under cemented pins is attributed to
the type of cement used and its effect on pulp. Zinc
phosphate cement without an underlying varnish
layer induces a more severe inflammatory response
as compared to Zinc oxide eugenol or Glass-ionomer
cement. Pulpal response under friction-locked and
threaded pins is probably because of the microcracks
generated in communication with the pulp or
disruption of the odontoblastic processes. The pulpal
response is succeeded by normal defense reaction, like
formation of reparative dentin under all three pins.
