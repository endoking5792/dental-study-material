The primary goal of operative dentistry is to provide
functional stomatognathic system along with pleasing
esthetics. Such a goal can only be achieved with sound
foundation, which in any treatment regime is
‘treatment planning’. In operative dentistry, the
treatment planning is restricted to management of
dental caries, surrounding periodontium and
improving esthetics of the individual. Prior to any
treatment plan, the operator must arrive at proper
diagnosis and the probable prognosis of the disease;
the same should be made clear to the patient. Efficiency,
profitability and also patient’s satisfaction are the
results of well-planned procedures. The treatment
results are a byproduct of the clinicians’ knowledge,
planning and execution of the procedures. It is essential
to consider patient’s needs, attitude and willingness
to undergo the specific treatment regime. The patient’s
general health and the medical conditions should also
be taken into account during planning a case for
operative procedure. Once mutual understanding is
reached, treatment can be executed, instructing the
patient regarding the maintenance phase.
CASE SELECTION
Case selection implies thorough conversation with the
patient prior to any operative procedure. It includes
personal, medical and dental history. Conversation
is an art. We must learn how to converse and to extract
patient feelings. One must be a patient listener. First,
do not ask any question. Let the patient feel
comfortable and reveal his/her problems in detail.
Second, during conversation, the operator should be
very attentive; he/she should not indulge in cross-
talk with the assistant or the nurse. Let there be one-
to-one conversation. Once the patient feels comfortable,
other questions can be asked.
Personal History
Personal and family history includes patient’s habits,
awareness towards general and oral health. Attitude
of parents towards oral care is also important. Certain
conditions, which are likely to be inherited, should
be explored. The socioeconomic status of the family
should also be noted.
Medical History
The detailed information regarding systemic diseases
and treatment taken, history of X-radiations and
allergies is important prior to start of any restorative
treatment. Physiological changes associated with
aging also influence treatment regime. Intake of
certain medicines, which reduce salivary flow and/
or alter the basic defense mechanisms of the body,
any allergy to drugs, past history of drugs taken
should be noted.
Dental History
Dental history provides information about the patient’s
awareness towards oral health. Any allergy to
anesthetics and local antiseptics should also be noted.
Past treatment of any tooth and its complication, if any,
has an important impact on the treatment plan. The
frequency of patient’s visit to dental clinic is also noted.
FORMULATING TREATMENT PLAN
Formulating treatment plan implies providing
masticatory system that is functionally adequate and
esthetically pleasing. The appropriate plan maintains
the balance between patients expectations and the
ethical commitment of the operator. When in doubt,
or patient cannot be reconciled, choose the ethical plan
for the long-term benefit of the patient. The patient
has to be convinced that the plan selected will
definitely resolve his/her problem with long-term
success. The probable barriers, such as cost, time, etc.
should be managed accordingly [informed consent
should be obtained from the patient (guardian)]. No
treatment plan is considered ‘concrete’ and may
evolve over time to suit patients’ expectations and the
changed circumstances.

v. Attrition, abrasion and erosion: Attrition is the
wear of enamel due to masticatory forces, which
may be physiologic due to aging or pathologic

surfaces usually associated with mechanical
means when continued for longer duration.
Erosion is the loss of enamel and may be dentin,
subsequent to acidic dissolution or can be
idiopathic. The etiology of erosion should be
looked for, since continuous erosion leads to
failure of restoration.

The deviation from normal occlusion has its

Premature contacts and excessive stresses

should be noted during clinical examination

findings and the radiographs.

CARIES RISK ASSESSMENT
Caries risk assessment is the procedure to predict the
future caries development before the classical onset
of disease.
‘Caries susceptibility’ refers to the inherent
tendency of the host and the target tissue (the tooth)
to be affected by caries.
Factors affecting evaluation of caries risk:
• Morphology and chemistry of teeth (arrangement
and number of teeth, occlusal relations, retentive
areas, enamel maturation, etc.).
• Biological and chemical factors (localization and
composition of plaque, microbial activity, etc.).
• Salivary factors (buffering capacity, immunological
factors, oral clearance, etc.).
• Diet (frequency, amount and quality of carbo-
hydrates taken, intake of fluorides, etc.).
• Epidemiology (family caries experience, caries
experience in deciduous teeth).
• Systemic conditions (diseases that may influence
oral health).
Risk assessment is carried out by:
• Dental saliva pH indicator
• Plaque pH check kit
• Saliva buffer check kit
• Clinpro caries diagnosis L-pop
• Caries risk assessment tool
• Traffic light matrix model
Clinpro caries diagnosis L-pop measures the lactic
acid production and evaluates the microbial flora of
the mouth by sampling the microflora of the tongue.

A traffic light matrix (TL-M) model offers a
systematic approach to the assessment of all the risk
factors, which contribute to caries activity.
Information is used to produce a risk profile from
which a treatment regime can be developed. The
model is based on the fact that the caries process is
multifactorial and it will be driven by changes in the
oral environment. The concept of using the colors red,
yellow and green in a traffic light color system to
convey the different levels of risk has been routinely
used. The traffic light does not attempt to predict
caries incidence, but rather it alerts the clinician to
the presence of risk factors that are capable of
changing the oral environment. The TL-M model
allocates a threshold value for each risk category. If
the information elicited from questioning or by clinical
testing yields results, which exceed the predetermined
threshold values, the model alerts the clinician to a
possible problem. The model investigates nineteen risk
factors and scores a red, yellow or green light for each
risk factor depending upon predetermined criteria.
The system uses a specially designed form to record
risk factors.

CARIES ACTIVITY TESTS
Caries activity tests are primarily undertaken to
evaluate the susceptibility of the individual towards
caries. ‘Caries activity’ refers to the increment of active
lesions (new and recurrent) over a period of time.
Caries activity is the measure of the speed of
progression of caries lesion. Caries activity tests
measure the degree to which the local environmental
challenge (dietary and microbial factors) favours the
probability of initiation of caries.
The tests are based on the hypothesis that oral
micro-organisms and fermentable carbohydrates are
causative agents in dental caries and the retention of
carbohydrates in oral cavity favour growth of micro-
organisms.
Caries activity tests help
• Identify high-risk individual/groups
• Determine the need of preventive regimes for
particular individual/groups.
• Ensuring low level of caries activity before
extensive restorations
• Monitor the effectiveness of oral health education
programs
Objectives
For clinicians
• To determine the need for caries control measures.
• To determine optimal time for expertise restoration.
• Aid in determining the results of the preventive
measures undertaken.
• Orthodontists can be cautioned during the treatment.
For research workers
• Aid in selection of cases for the study of caries.
• Helps in screening of patented therapeutic agents.
• Serves as an indicator of period of exacerbation.
Ideal requirement of caries activity tests
• Should have sound theoretical basis
• Should be accurate as regards duplication of results
• Should be simple and inexpensive
• Should be less time consuming

A. Lactobacillus Colony Count Test
This test is of a historical interest; limited mainly to
research. It quantitatively evaluates lactobacilli
bacteria in the saliva.
Procedure
The patient is asked to chew paraffin before breakfast
and the saliva is collected in a bottle. A 1:10 dilution
is prepared by pipetting 1.0 mL of this saliva sample
into 9.0 mL tube of sterile saline solution. This is
shaken and 1:100 dilution is made by pipetting
1.0 mL of 1:10 dilution into another 9.0 mL tube of
sterile salt solution.
Then 0.4 mL of each dilution solution is spread on
the surface of an agar plate. The plates are labeled
and incubated at 37°C for 3–4 days. Quebac counter
is used to count the number of colonies.

D. Alban Test
The Alban test eliminates the need for melting the
medium as the medium used is soft and allows easy
penetration of saliva. The procedure is convenient as

Procedure
60 gm of Snyder test agar is placed in one litre of water

adequately melted, the molten agar is distributed in

The tubes should be allowed to cool in the refrigerator.
The patient is asked to expectorate directly into the

I. Salivary Buffer Capacity Test
Salivary buffer capacity test can be quantitated using
either pH meter or color indicator. The test measures
the quantity of acid in milliliters required to lower
the pH of saliva through an arbitrary pH interval (say
from pH 7.0 to 6.0) or the amount of acid/base
necessary to bring color indicators to their point.
Procedure
Ten milliliter of stimulated saliva is collected at least
one hour after eating. 5.0 mL of this is taken in a beaker.
The pH of saliva is adjusted at 7.0 by addition of lactic
acid or base. Lactic acid is further added until pH 6 is
reached. The amount of lactic acid needed to reduce the
pH from 7.0 to 6.0 is a measure of buffer capacity. The
number is converted to milliequivalent per liter.
There is an inverse relationship of buffering
capacity of saliva and caries. The saliva of individual
having sufficient number of carious lesion frequently
has lower acid buffering capacity then the saliva of
those who are relatively caries free.

Case Selection: Treatment Planning
Communicable diseases, which may affect treatment
planning are to be identified, viz.:
• Herpes
• Hepatitis-B
• HIV
• Tuberculosis
• Mumps
• Oral candidiasis
(Transmissible through body fluids, such as blood
and saliva, pose great risk to the doctor and the staff).
Systemic conditions affecting operative treatment are:
• Cardiovascular diseases
• Asthma
• Bleeding disorders
• Diabetes
• Renal diseases
• Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)
• Pregnancy
The patient’s physician should be consulted before
initiating any procedure, especially elderly patients
and others suspected of any systemic involvement.
The systemic conditions, which warrant extra
attention before executing treatment planning, are:
a. Cardiovascular diseases: When a patient has a
history of hypertension or taking anticoagulants,
such patients are to be treated with utmost care. In
any case, where the patient is at risk, the operative
procedures should be carried out under antibiotic
premedication.
Mouth wash with 5.0% betadine/chlorhexidine is
advised before rubber dam placement to avoid any
possibility of bacteremia due to injury to gums.
Vasoconstrictors should not be administered to
patients with unstable angina, recent myocardial
infarction, recent stroke, recent arrhythmias and
hypertensive patients. Local anesthetic agents
without vasoconstrictor or minimal vasoconstrictors
are preferred.
Patients with artificial heart valves are considered
highly susceptible to bacterial endocarditis. Such
patients should be given prophylactic antibiotics
before initiating operative procedures.
It is mandatory to check whether hypertension is
under control or not, before administering local
anesthesia [even though adrenaline is contraindicated
in hypertensives, amount available in 1:100000 solutions
is safe. As anesthetics without vasoconstrictors have a
less profound effect and shorter duration of action, slow
injection (one minute for 1.8 mL) using 26 or 27 gauge
needle is preferred].
b. Asthma: Asthma, hay fever, skin rashes, etc.
indicate hypersensitivity problems and the patients
should be managed accordingly. The patient’s
medication and nebulizer should be kept ready.
The rubber dam application should be modified
and removed after short interval of time. Clinic set
up should be aerosol free.
The patient is thoroughly explored for:
• Whether patient is on medication: If yes, for
which problem?
• Whether the medication was prescribed by any
qualified doctor?
• Any allergic reactions in the near past.
This helps in identifying patient attitude towards
his/her ailments (some medication may be
interfering with the antibiotics and analgesics, need
to be analyzed).
c. Bleeding disorders: The bleeding disorders (blood
dyscrasias) are not definite contraindications for
executing operative procedures; however, the
operator should be conscious of the implications
of these diseases.
• Check bleeding and clotting time
• Less traumatic procedure should be planned.
(Most challenging is to treat a patient with
hemophilia as there is always the risk of puncturing
blood vessel and inducing internal bleeding,
particularly with inferior alveolar nerve blocks. Care
should be exercised in placing rubber dam clamps so
that they do not impinge on gingival tissue).
d. Diabetes: The controlled diabetic patients, free
from complications like renal failure, hypertension
and/or coronary atherosclerotic disease, can be
treated in routine. The prophylactic antibiotics are
prescribed as a standard protocol. The appointments
should be scheduled considering the patient’s
normal meal and insulin schedule.
• Check whether diabetes is under control or not
(stage of diabetes).
• Patients with diabetes are prone to infection and
delayed healing; hence, necessary precautions
should be taken.
• Schedule early morning appointments where the
patient has had medication and meals, so that
the risk of hypoglycemia is minimized.

also safe but care has to taken with chair position as

the right hip elevated 10 to 12 cm or placing the patient
in a 5.0 to 15% tilt on her left side can relieve pressure
on the inferior vena cava. If hypotension is still not
relieved, a full left lateral position may be needed.
MAINTENANCE PHASE
After executing the treatment plan (final restoration),
the patient is allowed some holding time for re-
evaluating the treatment along with time for healing
and resolution of inflammation (if any). The patient
is kept motivated for maintenance of the treatment
opted and also caring for abnormal habits, if recorded
earlier.
A regular recall examination is necessary; which
provide opportunity to reinforce home care and
may need minor adjustments after functional use.
Recall is important especially in patients with high
caries risk and also with unstable periodontal
health.
Finishing/polishing, if required, should be carried
out periodically, during the maintenance phase.
Many features can be traceable, which might have
been overlooked during initial examination.
Summarily, the phase maintains the oral health of
the patients.
