Pulp, a specialized connective tissue, is very sensitive
to external stimuli. All dental procedures, extent may
vary, threaten the integrity of the pulp. Any stimulus
over the exposed dentinal tubules affects the pulp.
The reaction of pulp, most of the times, is physiologic;
however, depending upon the intensity of the
stimulus, pathological changes do occur in pulp.
It is established that the contact of a dental
material with dentin can induce odontoblastic
alterations and transient inflammatory reactions.
Earlier, the acidic components from zinc phosphate
and unreacted monomers from composite
restorations were considered injurious to the pulp.
It is established that leakage of bacteria and their
byproducts from the oral environment along the
gaps in the cavity–restoration interface are more
toxic than the material itself.
It is mandatory to know the comparative toxicity
of dental materials, and techniques so that necessary
measures can be undertaken to reduce their toxic
effect. The operator should have thorough knowledge
of the materials and techniques so as to use them
cautiously; subsequently, minimizing pulpal
reactions.
TERMINOLOGY
The commonly used terms in the context of safety of
materials are as follows:
i. Biocompatibility
The term ‘biocompatible’ is defined as being
harmonious with life and not having toxic or injurious
effects on biological functions. In general, bio-
compatibility is measured on the basis of localized
cytotoxicity (such as pulp and mucosal response),
systemic responses, allergenicity and carcinogenicity.
The dental materials should not be harmful to the
pulp and soft tissues. It should not contain toxic
substance that can be absorbed into the circulation to
cause a systemic response. It should have no
carcinogenic potential and also should be free of
potentially sensitizing agents that could cause an
allergic response.
ii. Biomaterial
Biomaterial is defined as any substance, other than a
drug, that can be used for any period as a part of a
system that treats, augments or replaces any tissue,
organ or function of a body. Dental materials, used
in humans for short or long periods, fall in the
category of biomaterial. The host environment for
dental biomaterials is especially complicated because
of the presence of bacteria and debris in the oral cavity
and the corrosive properties of saliva and other fluids.
iii. Hazardous
A material is considered hazardous; when placed in
contact, it has the potential to create harmful reaction
in the body tissues. Hazardous materials are generally
identified through screening tests; the material is
placed in direct contact with either cultured cells or
animal tissues.
iv. Risk
Risk implies the probability that the material would
cause sufficient damage. A material, which is
hazardous but do not pose a significant risk to the
body (e.g. zinc oxide eugenol), is hazardous but not
risky. Risk may be reduced because of diffusion or
dilution of the offending components, biological
barriers, or a lack of sufficient time of contact between
the offending components and susceptible tissues.

organ (the pulp–dentin complex) because of the
intimate relationship between the cellular tissue
within the dentin and the peripheral pulp tissue.

Physiology
The pulp is a connective tissue, which is derived from
the mesenchyme, the dental papilla. The pulp is
characterized by its specialized odontoblasts on the
periphery beneath the predentin. Beneath the
odontoblastic layer is the ‘cell-poor zone’ or the ‘Weil’s
layer’ composed principally of young fibroblasts. The
central portion of the pulp consists of many young
fibroblasts and occasional lymphocytes. It is richly
supplied by thin walled but wide capillaries. Bundles
of myelinated nerve fibers accompany the blood
vessels. Fibers predominantly present are collagenous
and reticular types. Lying in close proximity to the
blood vessels are many undifferentiated mesenchymal
cells and histiocytes. These are believed to transform
into secondary odontoblasts that form reparative
dentin following the death of primary odontoblasts.
The interstitial fluid of the pulp and the dentinal
tubules are continuous and extend from dentino-
enamel and dentino-cementum junction to the soft
connective tissue of the pulp.
The pulp is vascularized as other organs of the
body. A large arteriole passes through the root pulp
to supply the coronal pulp. They branch as capillaries
in the coronal subodontoblastic region. The blood
capillaries have discontinuity in the endothelial walls.
Such types of arrangements facilitate easy exchange
of nutrients and the waste fluid. This exchange is
important in case of pulp injury. The fluid flow from
the pulp to exposed dentin is dependent on the
hydraulic conductance of the dentinal fluid. Any
reduction in this conductivity will reduce the dentin
sensitivity.
Myelinated and unmyelinated nerves enter the
pulp through apical foramen (Fig. 31.1). Usually the
myelinated fibers and unmyelinated A-fibers carry
pain impulses. The sensory and the sympathetic nerve
endings are activated at an early stage of the
inflammatory process and are the initiators of
vasodilatation. This is the start of the protective
response to the injury by increasing blood volume and
vascular permeability in the affected areas. Both the
sympathetic and sensory nerve fibers have effect on
the pulpal circulation. The number of nerve fibers
decreases with age, which explains the reduced
sensitivity in older adults.
Dentin has an average of 45,000–90,000 tubules/
mm2 near the pulp, 30,000–35,000/mm2 in the middle
and 10,000–25,000/mm2 at the periphery. At the pulp
surface, the diameter of the tubules ranges from 2.5–
3.0 m and at the periphery, the diameter may be less
than 1.0 m. Each tubule is about 1.0–3.0 m in
diameter and 2.0–3.0 mm in length. The tubules have
highly mineralized lining, the peritubular dentin,
which is not found in the pulpal part of the dentin of
the newly erupted teeth. The highly mineralized
peritubular dentin should be distinguished from the
other mineralized components of dentin, the
intertubular dentin that has much less collagen than
the peritubular dentin. The growth of peritubular
dentin, may be age related, or due to restorative
procedures, leads to obliteration of the tubules. The
occluded dentinal tubules, referred to as dentin
sclerosis, react differently to acid etching. (Etching
time is modified to provide an adequate hybrid layer
of collagen and resin.)

dentin, forms a barrier between the physiologic
secondary and tertiary dentin. This barrier reduces

PULPAL RESPONSE TO RESTORATIVE MATERIALS
It is established that pulp reactions to the same irritant
may vary not only between different persons but also
between different teeth and between opposing teeth
of the similar anatomic form.
The factors influencing pulpal responses are as
follows:
a. Dentin Permeability
A variety of features affect dentin permeability; the
main are:
i. Location: Dentin permeability varies in different
areas of the same tooth; for example, it increases
towards the pulpal side. This is because both the
tubule diameter and the number increase
towards the pulp chamber.
ii. Tubule diameter (diffusional area): The tubule
diameter directly influences the wetness;
subsequently, the hydrolytic dissolution of the
restorative material.
iii. Smear layer: The presence or absence of the 1.0–
5.0 mm thick layer of microcrystalline debris on
the cut dentin surface (smear layer) controls
dentin permeability. Presence of smear layer
reduces permeability.
iv. Intratubular calcifications: Intratubular
calcifications, viz. mineral deposits, collagen
fibrils, proteins, etc. may reduce permeability.
v. Concentration and solubility of diffusing
solutes: The substances with high molecular
weight and size are less penetrating than the
substances with low molecular size and weight.
Solutes that are water soluble would show a
rapid rate of penetration.
vi. Patency of the dentinal tubules: Sclerotic dentin
is less permeable than the physiologic tubular
dentin.
vii. Reparative dentin: Reparative dentin reduces
the penetration of the solute.
viii. Remaining dentin thickness: The remaining
dentin thickness of 2.0 mm provides an adequate
insulating barrier against almost all the
techniques and restorative materials. It has been
shown that a 0.5 mm thickness of dentin reduces
the toxicity level of a material to 75% and a
1.0 mm thickness may reduce over 90%.
b. Dentin Conditioning
Pre-conditioning of dentin by acids (etchants)
increases dentin permeability by removing smear
layer and enlarging tubule orifices. The effect depends
upon the concentration and the duration for which
the etchant is applied.
c. Age of the Patient
It is hypothesized that the inflammatory response of
the older persons is less extensive and the reparative
dentin formation is also less as compared to the
younger individuals. The older pulps are far less
defensive in resolving a lesion and resisting infection.
However, a few authors have observed that age might
not directly affect the pulpal response.
INTENSITY OF PULPAL RESPONSE
The pulpal reactions, as depicted histopathologically,
are as follows:
1. Cellular Displacement
It is characterized by movement of odontoblasts and
leukocytes into the dentinal tubules. This occurs in
the first few days and may persist over 30 days. It
provides evidence of the acuteness of initial response
after the leukocytes are degenerated and other
characteristics of acute inflammation have resolved.
2. Initial Infiltration of Inflammatory Cells

The response usually takes 1 to 6 days; however,
may take 20 days in some cases.
4. Special Histopathologic Features
a. Abscess formation: Occasionally, beneath cavity
preparation, certain abscess-like conditions (dense
accumulations of leukocytes between the

develop, which usually resolve the tissue. These
characteristics are not necessarily localized, but
sometimes they can be quite focal.
b. Foci of necrosis: Cavity preparation and placement
of toxic material lead to loss of cellular detail,
collapse of vascular channels and a paucity of
inflammatory cells. These lesions get infiltrated by
inflammatory cells; usually resolve with
granulation tissue replacement and sometime
undergo abscess formation.
c. Regeneration of odontoblasts: In case the lesion is
resolved, the inflammatory cells may disappear
and leave behind an atrophic or degenerated
odontoblastic layer [foci completely lacking in
primary odontoblasts (Fig. 31.2)]. In some
instances, only regenerated odontoblasts are found
and a distinction needs to be made between these
two types of layers.
d. Reparative (tertiary) dentin formation: The greater
the degree of initial response due to irritation
caused by cutting and placing of a restorative
material, the greater the incidence of reparative
dentin formation (Fig. 31.3).
Reparative dentin seldom develops earlier than
30 days.
e. Eosinophilic staining: The tissues involved are
inundated by an amorphous material similar to that
seen in capillaries. It is assumed that this is a plasma
transudate, which escapes through mildly injured
capillaries.
This characteristic is found either in operative
techniques capable of producing a low order of
irritation or on the periphery of many larger pulpal
lesions away from the center of inflammation.
STAGES OF PULPAL INFLAMMATION
The reaction of the pulp to external stimuli is reflected
in two broad processes: Inflammatory changes and
secondary dentin formation.
Whenever the pulp is subjected to external stimuli,
the immune system triggers inflammatory response
to limit the tissue damage. The inflammation in pulp,
the pulpitis, is similar to inflammation of any
connective tissue in the body. The polymorphonuclear
leukocytes are associated with acute reaction and also
the mononuclear leukocytes, plasma cells and
macrophages. Mast cells are only found in inflamed
pulp, however, not in normal pulp. The inflammatory
processes have been quantitatively divided into slight,
moderate and severe.

EVALUATION OF BIOCOMPATIBILITY
The aim of evaluating biocompatibility is to eliminate
any product or a component of product that can
damage the oral tissues. The term ‘irritation’ and
‘sensitization’ must be understood and differentiated.
‘Irritation’ is defined as an inflammation brought
about without the intervention of an antibody or
immune system; whereas, ‘sensitization’ is an
inflammatory response requiring the participation of
antibody system.
The restorative materials should pass all levels of
biocompatibility testing before it is approved for final
usage in the human oral cavity.
The tests are categorized as follows:
A. Primary Tests
a. Cytotoxicity tests: Cytotoxicity tests include the
placement of a dental material directly onto the
tissue culture cells. The cells react depending upon
the components that leach from these materials.
The toxic response can be quantified by
measurement of biologic function, such as, DNA
synthesis, protein synthesis, or the concentration
of components that cause a decrease in cellular
activity by 50% (TC 50 concentration). TC 50
concentration is inversely proportional to the
potency of the cytotoxic response, i.e. lower the TC
50 concentration, higher is the potency.
b. Genotoxicity tests: Mammalian/non-mammalian
cells, bacteria/yeasts/fungi are used to determine
whether gene mutations, changes in chromosome
structure, or other DNA changes are caused by the
restorative materials.
B. Secondary Tests
a. Systemic toxicity tests: The test sample is
administered daily to rats for 14 days, either by
oral lavage or by dietary inclusion. If 50% of the
animals survive, the product is considered safe.
b. Inhalation toxicity tests: These tests are performed
on rats or guinea pigs in an exposure chamber. The
spray material is released around the head of the
animals for 30 seconds, each at 30 minutes intervals.
After 10 consecutive exposures, the animals are
observed for 4 days. If any animal dies within this
period, the agent is considered toxic, otherwise
safe.
c. Skin irritation and sensitization tests: The test
material is held in contact with the shaved skin of
albino rats for periods ranging from 24 (one
exposure) to 90 days (with repeated exposures) for
evaluating irritation effects.
d. Implantation tests: Various physical properties of
the material, such as density, hardness and surface
finish influence the character of the tissue response.
The implant material is packed in plastic tubes and
put into subcutaneous tissues or directly into bones
in drilled holes. The reactions are evaluated.
C. Preclinical Usage Tests
a. Pulp and dentin usage test: The material is placed
in contact with open dentin 1.0 mm from the pulp
of rodents, and evaluated after a given period of
time. The specimens are examined regarding
inflammatory response, reparative dentin
formation and the number of micro-organisms
entrapped in the surrounding cavity walls and cut
dentinal tubules. If an inflammatory response is
produced, the time required for its disappearance
is also measured.
b. Pulp capping and pulpotomy usage tests: The
procedure is similar to the previous ones, except
that the pulp is exposed. The animals are evaluated
for dentin bridge formation, its quality and
structure.
c. Endodontic usage tests: The pulp is completely
removed and replaced by the obturating material.
The degree of inflammation is evaluated in the
periapical tissues.

A. Zinc Oxide Eugenol
Zinc oxide eugenol (ZOE) is frequently used material;
considered bland, and even therapeutic to the pulp.
Since zinc oxide eugenol has a low compressive
strength, different additives are added to reinforce
the original formulation. These are having better
physical properties; however, more irritating to pulp.
Zinc oxide when mixed with eugenol leads to
formation of zinc eugenolate matrix. The zinc
eugenolate units are held together by van der Waal’s
forces and particle interlocking. When exposed to
aqueous media, such as saliva or dentinal fluid,
hydrolysis of zinc eugenolate occurs, yielding eugenol
and zinc hydroxide. Eugenol liberated from zinc
eugenolate can diffuse through dentin and into the
saliva. The concentration of 102 mol/L of eugenol can
be observed below the fillings in dentin and 104 mol/
L in the pulp; remain maintained for more than one
week. Calcium in the dentinal tubules chelates
eugenol, limiting its ability to diffuse through dentin.
Eugenol also binds with the organic matrix of dentin,
especially collagen, which slows the diffusion rate.
Modified ZOE cements have demonstrated less
release of eugenol and fewer cytotoxic effects. It has
been observed that acid-etched dentin may facilitate

cells underlying the cavity floor with a little reparative
dentin formation. The effect of eugenol is dose

B. Zinc Phosphate Cement
Zinc phosphate cement when used as a base, has a
low toxicity level; but quite toxic when used as a luting
agent. Zinc phosphate cement is irritating because of
its low pH and the rapid penetration of its lower
molecular weight phosphoric acid into the dentinal
tubules. The hydraulic forces, which are induced
during the seating of the restoration or during
functional movements, facilitate pushing of
phosphoric acid into the dentinal tubules. In deep
preparations, a moderate to severe localized pulpal
damage is produced within the first three days,
probably because the cement has an initially low pH
on setting. The pH of the set cement approximates
neutrality only at 48 hours. Resolution of inflammation
occurs by 5 to 8 weeks.
The chemical toxicity of zinc phosphate cement
materials may not explain the harmful effects on pulp.
It is established that bacterial leakage would be an
important factor for inducing pulpal damage.
When zinc phosphate is used as a luting agent, an
intense diffuse infiltration of neutrophils occurs
throughout the pulp tissue (Fig. 31.7). The initial pH
of luting mixes ranged from 2.0–3.3, which changes
to 3.0–4.2 after one hour. It has been established that
such a low pH may induce vascular thrombosis and
necrosis in pulp. The pH for zinc phosphate rises
during the first 15 minutes. When the cement is used
as a base with 1.0 mm remaining dentin, there occurs
lifting of the odontoblastic layer and also the
inflammatory cells become countable. In case the
remaining dentin thickness is 1.5 mm, there occurs
only moderate reaction with segmented odontoblastic
layer and some reparative dentin formation.

C. Composites
The bond strength of composite resin is linked to the
etching of the normal mineralized tissue. The effect
of etching on pulp is minimum or negligible, since
the process is carried out for 10–15 seconds only
(Fig. 31.8). To achieve optimal bonding to dentin, the
adhesive material (bonding agents) must penetrate
the demineralized dentin, enter the dentinal tubules
and their branches (Fig. 31.9A, B). The resin monomer
enters the collagen fibers to completely infiltrate the
demineralized dentin, forming the hybrid layer
(Fig. 31.10). Over this, the composite is filled.
The bonding materials have been tried as ‘pulp
capping agents’ with almost satisfactory results. It
imparts healing process; however, the polymerization
shrinkage may create vacuum in between the
remaining dentin and the restoration, subsequently
affecting the pulp. It has been established that even
after curing, the monomer is leached from composites.
This leached monomer is managed by the remaining
dentin thickness. In case the dentin thickness is less,
the leaching monomer can affect the underlying pulp.
D. Glass-Ionomer Cements
The glass-ionomer cement along with its modified
forms is considered biocompatible; mainly indicated
in cavity bases and liners. The bonding of glass-
ionomer material to dentin involves chemical and
mechanical bonds. The chemical bond is based on
exchange of ions between carboxyl group of substrate
and calcium ions. The mechanical interlocking is
based on the demineralization of exposed dentin by
polyacrylic acid.

PULPAL REACTION TO TOOTH PREPARATION
The high-speed cutting instruments utilizing light
pressure with intermittent cooling can minimize
temperature increase during tooth preparation.
When the cooling is inadequate, the injury leads to
displacement of odontoblastic nuclei into the dentinal
tubules (Fig. 31.12). The odontoblasts appear
disorganized. The burning of dentin becomes evident
(Fig. 31.13), if excessive pressure is used and that too
without cooling.
The most common feature is the outward
movement of contents of the tubules (Fig. 31.14). This
movement is dependent on the tubules’ openings.
Many a times, the smear layer may obturate the

PULPAL REACTION TO CARIES
The dental caries, may be at any site, affects the
underlying pulp. The acidic environment of carious
lesions cause both tissue dissolution and in aggressive
lesions, cellular injury as well. The initiation of pulpal
inflammation also stimulates the repair process. A
balance is usually maintained between repair and
injury and deviation from the balance leads to changes
in the dentin–pulp complex.
Caries create acidic environment by diffusion of
bacterial acids. The ability of the cells to withstand
this acid environment depends upon hydrogen ion
concentration. Local lymphatic drainage may
contribute to clearance of acid, but the pulpal
lymphatic system is not well developed. The
odontoblasts are significantly reduced in size beneath
the active enamel lesions; whereas, at the site of
arrested lesions, such reduction is not exhibited. As
the caries lesion reaches dentinoenamel junction, a
significant reduction in cytoplasm to nucleus ratio of
odontoblasts is observed. An increase in activity of
phosphate enzyme is reported in odontoblasts and
in predentin beneath the carious lesions.
The reaction in pulp varies according to intensity
and progress of caries; however, the extent of pulpal
infection is not systematized.
Pulpal reactions have been reported even with the
formation of white-spot lesions. There are marked
changes in dentin with ‘acute’ or rapidly progressing
caries than with ‘arrested’ or slow progressing caries.
The odontoblasts in active lesions were significantly
smaller than with other lesions. The cellular
proliferation of the cell-free zone is also observed in
active lesions only. Changes in the subodontoblastic
region might include onset of neurogenic inflamma-
tory reactions.

PULPAL REACTION TO TRAUMA
Physical trauma may lead to fracture of enamel,
dentin and exposure of pulp. Orthodontic tooth
movements can be performed without any pulpal
involvement, but quick orthodontic movements may
lead to changes in the pulp–dentin complex.
The extensive trauma involves avulsion or
displacement of the jaw bones. The blood supply to
the pulp is severed off. Either the pulp is necrosed
with time or characterized by formation of hard
tissues in the pulp chamber. These hard tissues are
referred to as ‘osteodentin’, because it has cellular
infiltration similar to bone. In case of reimplantation,
there occur degeneration of odontoblast and
simultaneous loss of the adjacent cell-free zone. This
degeneration is accompanied by reduction of the
width of predentin. In teeth with open apices, the
odontoblasts can survive and produce reactionary
dentin. An interface-dentin is formed separating
primary dentin and the reparative dentin.
The orthodontic movements may lead to increased
blood flow in the pulp. Severe hyperemia has been
seen in such teeth.
ORTHODONTIC MOVEMENT AND PULPAL REACTION
The magnitude of rapidity of orthodontic movement,
which may cause pulpal damage, has not been
properly documented. The pulpal degeneration may
occur after tipping forces or rapid tooth movement,
owing to loss of collateral supply to the pulp. The
‘tipping force’ concept has been refuted, claiming
successful rotation of 72°–108° without any pulpal
damage. The ‘force’, however, do affect pulpal vitality.

ii. Blood Flow to the Pulp
It is hypothesized that dynamic mechanisms exist in
the control of blood flow in a tooth undergoing
orthodontic movement. When an orthodontic force
is applied, pulp tissue reacts with initial pulp
hyperemia, subsequently proceeding to degranulation
of mast cells characterized by cell damage and allied
biochemical reactions. This is a classical feature of
acute inflammation (Fig. 31.16). The initial stage of
reactive hyperemia usually reverts back to normal in
due course of time. When the pulp of teeth subjected
to orthodontic forces was grown in culture, it showed
sprouting of new vessels. Angiogenic changes have
been observed after orthodontic movement (increase
in number of microvessels), expressing rise of
angiogenic growth factor in pulp. The factors
responsible for such changes are platelet-derived
growth factor, vascular endothelial growth factor and
transforming growth factor-. Transforming growth
factors (TGF-1 and TGF-3) are responsible for
stimulation of reactionary dentin and also reparative
dentin after orthodontic tooth movement. Since dental
pulp is limited in narrow area, the increase blood flow
(dilatation of arterioles) causes increase in pulp
pressure and compression of venous flow. It is
established that most of the changes in pulpal blood
are reversible (during tooth movement); only if pulp
is not irritated earlier because of deep caries, trauma,
etc. It is also asserted that fully developed (mature
apex) teeth are sensitive to irreversible pulp
inflammation and immature apex teeth are not
sensitive. It is established that application of severe
force for a longer period may affect pulpal blood flow
than short-term application of same force. The two
variables of decrease in pulpal respiration and an
altered blood flow interplay to bring about dystrophic
mineralization with deposition of reparative dentin
in coronal and radicular portions of the tooth. It is
established that such changes beginning at the peri
apex influence the odontoblasts in the formed teeth
and Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath (HERS) in the
developing teeth (Fig. 31.17). Such changes, especially
when accompanied with a history of previous trauma,
contribute to the mechanism of loss of pulp vitality
after orthodontic movement.

PULPAL REACTION TO VITAL BLEACHING
The vital tooth bleaching incorporates carbamide
solution in different modifications. Initial reaction of the
pulp to bleaching includes less distinction of cell-free
zone. In areas on facial surface of these teeth, the cells
migrate to these areas. Scattered leukocytes are observed
along with irregularities in the pseudostratified
odontoblastic layer. Only slight pulp reaction is observed
(Fig. 31.18A, B). In any case even with high concentration,
carbamide does not produce moderate or severe
reactions. A few authors have opined that higher
concentration of hydrogen peroxide and heat is
potentially harmful to pulp. The changes in pulp tissue
are reversible within two weeks. Therefore, two weeks
of treatment with 10% carbamide peroxide used for
nightguard vital bleaching is considered safe for the pulp.
A 3.0% solution of hydrogen peroxide is capable
of causing transient reduction in pulpal blood
circulation and occlusion of pulpal blood vessels. The
most common side effect experienced by patients
using home bleaching technique is mild, transient
temperature sensitivity. This sensitivity appears to be
dose related rather than pH related.
