Glass-ionomer cement, developed by Wilson and Kent
(1972), was popularized as ASPA (aluminosilicate
polyacrylate) in early years.
The glass-ionomer cement was evolved as a hybrid
from silicate and the polycarboxylate cement; mainly
to overcome deficiencies of silicate cement. The term
glass-ionomer cement was coined by Wilson and
Kent, though the ISO terminology for the cement was
polyalkenoate cement (Fig. 20.1). Because of the
extensive use of this cement as a dentin replacement
material, it has also been referred to as ‘man made
dentin’ and ‘dentin substitute’. The greatest advantage of
this cement was its adhesion to enamel  and  dentin
and  the  fluoride  release  for anticariogenic effect. The
most useful clinical applications of glass-ionomer
cements have been in restoring erosion lesions without
cavity preparation and sealing of pit and fissure defects.
Though, glass-ionomer was originally aimed to be a
restorative material, soon it was modified as luting
cement also. The composition of the powder particle,
particularly the type of glass, could be altered to
achieve different characteristics. The early cement
was slow setting and highly technique sensitive. Over
the years, it has undergone many changes. The

COMPOSITION
The powder contains silica, alumina and fluorides of
calcium, sodium and aluminum. Fluorides are used
as flux. The mixture is fused at high temperature of
about 1,100 to 1,500°C and the molten mass is shock
cooled. This mass is finely powdered to required
particle sizes. Restorative cements have a particle size
of about 50 m; whereas, it is about 20 m for luting
cements.

1. Acid–Base Reaction
The acid–base reaction involves three components, viz.
fluoroaluminosilicate glass powder, an ionic polymer
of polyacrylic acid and water. The reaction occurs
between glass powder and the ionic polymer. Water is
an essential medium through which ion transfer takes
place. While mixing the powder and liquid, the acid
slowly degrades the outer layer of glass particles
(calcium ions and aluminum ions). During the early
stages, divalent calcium ions are released more rapidly
and are primarily responsible for reacting with the
polyacid to form a reaction product, i.e. calcium
polysalts. The initial set is due to this reaction. At this
stage, the relationship with water is critical. Hence,
the cement is to be protected from coming in contact
with external moisture as well as drying. If the cement
comes in contact with moisture, the aluminum ions
may leach out of cement, before it combines with the
main network. This will result in a weak cement as
well as a more soluble cement, which will be rapidly
eroded by the oral fluids. If the cement is dried, then
the water, which is essential for ion transfer, will
prevent the reaction to completion;  resulting in weak
material, which may crumble easily. It also appears
dull and white and exhibits poor esthetics. Trivalent
aluminum ions are released more slowly and become
involved in setting at a later stage often referred to as
‘secondary reaction stage’. The aluminum ions also
replace divalent calcium ions and form tight network
of crosslink between polymer chains. The set material
consists of unreacted glass cores embedded in a matrix
of cross-linked polyacid (Fig. 20.3). The second stage
of setting reaction involves the incorporation of
significant quantities of aluminum in the matrix
structure resulting in adequate physical properties.
This stage requires about 24 hours. Although the
material may appear to be hard within the first 5
minutes, it takes at least 24 hours before the material
is set to a stable form.
For a complete reaction to take place, it may take as
long as 7 days (Fig. 20.4). Once the replacement reaction
is complete, the solubility of the cement in water also
decreases. Silica ions react with the available water

2. Light-Activated Polymerization
The setting reaction in light cure glass-ionomer is
basically a two-stage process. Since the powder
component of light cure glass-ionomer cement consists
of initiators for light curing and the liquid component
is modified with hydroxyethyl-methacrylate (HEMA),
these ingredients are responsible for polymerization
by light. The initial reaction leads to the polymerization
of methacrylate groups and subsequently followed
by acid–base reactions of the glass component of
powder and polyacrylic acid of the liquid. This is also
known as ‘dual cure cement’.
3. Tri-Cure Activation
In addition to the light curing mechanism and the
acid–base reaction, some of the resin-modified glass-
ionomer cements harden by a chemical ‘dark cure’
catalysed by a reduction–oxidation reaction. The dark
cure is perhaps important in deeper restorations in
which light beam penetration may not be sufficient
for completion of the light-hardening phase and
chemical reaction is also insufficient. These materials
are termed ‘tri-cure’ as these resin modified glass-
ionomer cements set by three phenomena.
i. Acid–base reaction between the conventional
components
ii. Light-cure reaction stimulated by light application.
iii. Autocure reaction, which is a reduction–oxidation
(Red-Ox) reaction that takes place between the
initiator-catalyst system of resin that gets activated
when the powder and liquid components are
mixed together. This reaction ensures that with
time, there will be a complete cure of the entire
restoration with no free-resin remaining.
PHYSICAL AND MECHANICAL PROPERTIES
The working time of traditional glass-ionomer cement
is 2–3 minutes. The water settable systems tend to
have longer working time. Solubility in water during
the first 24 hours is high. Glass-ionomer has a co-
efficient of thermal expansion close to that of dentin.
It inhibits bacterial growth, due to fluoride release by
the cement. The glass-ionomer cement is a mild irritant
to pulp tissue, especially when placed over dentin.
The biocompatibility of the cement is due to the large
molecular size of the cement, which does not travel
through the narrower dentinal tubules. The initial pH
of the mixed cement is close to 2 but soon reaches a
value between 6 and 7. However, glass-ionomer should
not be placed over a direct exposure of the pulp. In
case, where the remaining dentin is less than 0.5 mm,
it is advisable to protect dentin and the pulp with
calcium hydroxide.

OTHER PROPERTIES
1. Sensitivity to Moisture
Glass-ionomer cements are very sensitive to moisture,
especially during initial setting phase. Both sorption
of water and over drying are deleterious to the
mechanical properties of the set cement. Absorption
of water lead to weak cement and the over drying will
lead to cracks in the cement. Therefore, protection of
the cement during and after placement is mandatory.
2. Postoperative Sensitivity
Postoperative sensitivity is usually associated with
poor manipulation and/or poor powder/liquid ratio.
This is also related with moisture contamination during
setting of the cement, leading to hydraulic effect on
dentinal fluid. However, light cure glass-ionomer and
compomer exhibit less postoperative sensitivity.

penetrate the tooth structure, releasing phosphate
ions. These phosphate ions take up calcium ion from
the tooth surface to maintain electric neutrality. These
ions will combine with the surface layer of cement and
form an intermediate layer of new material, which is
firmly attached to the tooth surface. This has been
described as a ‘diffusion-based adhesion’. The adhesion
to dentin is also through hydrogen bonding or metallic
ion bridging between the carboxyl groups of the
polyacid and the collagen of dentin.
Summarily, adhesion of glass-ionomer cements can
be attributed to two inter-related phenomena:
• Micromechanical (polyacid components of glass-
ionomer make it self-etching).
• Chemical bonding (ionic bonds formed between
carboxyl group or polyacid molecule and calcium
in the tooth surface).
As the cement matures, a diffusion process occurs
in which ions from the cement and ions from the tooth
move into the interfacial zone and create an ion-
exchange layer. This layer causes the tooth and the
cement to adhere strongly.
It has been established that failure in glass-ionomer
is cohesive in nature (occur within cement, rather at
cement–tooth interface). The boning values can differ
at different sites of the restorations.
Adhesion aids in retention and reduces marginal
leakage. (Harmful micro-organisms cannot enter the
tooth–restoration interface.)
4. Fluoride Release and Recharge (Anticariogenicity)
Incorporation of fluoride in glass-ionomer acts as a
flux during sintering of the powder particle. Fluoride
also prolongs the working time of the cement. Fluoride
release in glass-ionomer can continue for years after
the cement hardens. There is an initial rise in fluoride
release from the cement for about a week and then
gradually decreases within a few months. The most
advantageous phenomenon of the cement is that it
can also take up fluoride from the environment and
replenish itself, functioning like a reservoir of fluoride
in the oral cavity.
Fluoride release also provides microbial inhibition.
Various additives with the ability to enhance the
antimicrobial effect have been tried. The addition of
zinc sulfate to glass-ionomer has shown significant
inhibition of Streptococcus mutans growth and also
increased fluoride release.
Fluorides  reduce  the  metabolic  activity  of  the
cariogenic microflora in the oral cavity.
It converts calcium hydroxyapatite crystals of the
dental tissues to acid resistant calcium fluorapatite
crystals that resist acid attack from caries producing
micro-organisms.
A few authors have questioned the validity of
fluoride release and its anticariogenic properties. In
one of the surveys, glass-ionomer restorations were
repeatedly replaced due to involvement of secondary
caries.
Fluoride release is considered beneficial. A
continuous supply of low level fluorides can inhibit
dentin demineralization. Fluoride release may also
reduce dentin hypersensitivity. However, release from
glass-ionomer to salivary components has not been
authenticated.
Fluoride is also taken up by glass-ionomer cements,
mainly in early stages (even fluoride-free glass-
ionomers, when exposed to fluorides, exhibited
fluoride-releasing activities), commonly referred to
as ‘fluoride recharge phenomena’.
This is a new development of a fluoride release
material to overcome the shortcomings  faced by
fluoride-releasing material.
The shortcomings are:
• Greater the fluoride release in a material, more
open is the structure, resulting in low strength
material.
• In order to improve the strength of fluoride-
containing materials, they are made more dense;

5. Bioactvity
Glass-ionomer cement is natural bioactive material,
since it releases fluoride, sodium, phosphate (bioactive
ions), etc. into the surrounding medium. In acidic
conditions, these ions are released in higher quantities.
These ions play variety of biological roles in the
environments, maintaining the balance in saliva and
the mineral phase of the tooth (glass-ionomers do take
up ions). Cement may take up calcium and phosphate
ions from saliva and develop a harder surface, as it
matures.
6. Esthetics
Glass-ionomer cements are manufactured by the
addition of pigments (metal oxides) to achieve different
shades. Glass-ionomer lacks the translucency to
simulate natural enamel. It is inferior to composites
and ceramics in this aspect. It is opaque and may
become dull and lifeless in course of time. Esthetically,
compomers are better than light cure glass-ionomer
and traditional glass-ionomers.
MODIFIED GLASS-IONOMER CEMENTS
1. Metal-Modified Glass-Ionomers
i. Miracle Mix (Silver Cermet)
Silver cermet was prepared by incorporation of silver-
tin alloy into the glass-ionomer cement powder. Glass
is generally brittle and addition of silver improves
toughness of the cement. (Silver acts as a stress absorber.)
It also improves the abrasive resistance of the cement;
however, other properties, viz. compressive strength,
flexural strength, solubility and abrasive resistance
remained without improvement. It is esthetically
unacceptable because of its gray/black color (Fig. 20.5).
It did not exhibit promising results, due to metal–
carboxylate interface failure.
ii. Glass Cermet
Glass and metal powder is sintered at high temperature.
The mixture is made to react with polyacrylic acids to
form improved glass-ionomer cement (glass cermet).
The cement exhibit better wear resistance and flexural
strength; maintaining the esthetics. Metals, like
titanium oxide, have also been added in smaller
proportion (approximately 5%) as whitening agent.
These cements are preferred in small Class I cavities
for restoration of deciduous teeth and for core
preparation.
These are widely used in Class V, erosion/abrasion
cavities and also in rampant caries. They are well
tolerated by the pulp and can be finished and polished
immediately after setting. The cement is suitable as
restorative material, as luting cement and also as base
under different restorative materials.

4. Giomers
A new category of hybrid esthetic restorative material,
which differs from both resin-modified GICs and
compomers has been introduced, known as ‘Giomers’.
Giomers are available as one paste form. They are
light polymerizing and require bonding agents for
adhesion to tooth structure. The common preparations
are: Reactmer (Shofu, Japan) and Beautifil (Shofu,
Japan) (Fig. 20.8).
The fluoroaluminosilicate glass in giomers is reacted
with polyalkenoic acid and water. This technology
differs from compomers, in which a variable amount
of dehydrated polyalkenoic acid is incorporated into
the resin matrix and the acid does not react with glass
until water uptake occurs into the restoration. Giomers
do contain glass-ionomer components, but cannot be
classified as compomers as the acid–base reaction has
already occurred. The term ‘prereacted glass-ionomer’
composite is suggested to describe giomers. Like
compomers, giomers are light polymerized and require
bonding system. (Reactmer bond is a single application
bonding agent.) It is glass-ionomer based, all-in-one
filled adhesive based on polymer rigid glass technology
(consisting of fluoroaluminosilicate glass, acetone,
water and initiator). The bonding system is a
hydrophilic solution that is extremely effective in
wetting the tooth surface in one application.
The etching effect relates to acidic monomers/
organic acids, that may interact with the mineral
component of tooth substrate and enhance monomer
penetration. The adhesive etches and penetrates the
etched enamel and hardens on evaporation of the
solvent and exposure to light. Giomer-based adhesives
create a thick hybrid layer between the restorative and
tooth substrate, though their bond strength is lower
than two-step bonding system.

Various clinical agents have been tried to improve

The main agents are:
i. Chlorhexidine: Antimicrobial activity of chlor-
hexidine against gram-positive bacteria has been
accepted. Another feature of chlorhexidine is its
impact on fluoride release. (Higher concentration
of chlorhexidine increases release of fluoride.) A
few studies have observed changes in physical/
mechanical properties of glass-ionomer cements
with use of high concentration of chlorhexidine
(decreases bonding potential and also increases
setting time). To overcome the drawback, nominal
concentration of chlorhexidine was used. A few
authors added chitosan or zeolite nanoparticles

Recently, finely milled particles of chlorhexidine
hexametaphosphate (CHX-HMP) were incorpo-
rated in glass-ionomer cement. Long-term release
of chlorhexidine, without any adverse effects on
mechanical properties, was observed.
ii. Triclosan: Triclosan is a synthetic and anionic
agent with a broad spectrum of antimicrobial
activity. 2.5% triclosan added to glass-ionomer has
shown better results than chlorhexidine (2.0%). It
provides long-term antibacterial activity (gets
immobilized with cement particles, controlling
triclosan release rate).
iii. Quaternary ammonium salt: Quaternary
ammonium salts disrupt the cell membrane by
changing membrane permeability of bacteria.
The long chain (16-carbon substitute) in
quaternary ammonium salts shows higher
antibacterial activity. But the drawback of such
long chain lengths is a resultant decrease in
compressive strength of GIC. For achieving proper
compressive strength, quaternary ammonium
compound with shorter carbon chains (6-carbon)
were incorporated into glass-ionomer cements.
Antimicrobial activity of quaternary ammonium
salts includes:
• Adsorption to negatively charged bacterial cell
surface
• Penetration into cell membrane
• Binding to cell cytoplasmic membrane
• Disturbing the metabolism of cytoplasmic
membrane
Antibacterial activity of quaternary salts
decreases with human saliva because of
electrostatic interactions between quaternary salts
and the salivary proteins.
iv. Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG)/Furanone
derivatives: Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) is
a potent antioxidant and shown antibacterial
activity. However, its antibacterial efficacy is
limited to initial 3–4 hours, when incorporated in
glass-ionomer cement. After 24 hours, biofilm
formation was normal around the cement
restorations.
EGCG enhances human pulp cell proliferation;
hence, used mainly in regenerative endodontics.
Furanone derivatives, though antibacterial, could
not show promising results when added to glass-
ionomer cement.
v. Nanoparticles: Silver, zinc and titanium dioxide
have shown antibacterial activity when
incorporated into glass-ionomer cement. The
addition of nanoparticles is effective in minimizing
the biofilm formation; however, their long-term
effect is under research. Moreover, silver particles
may impart black color to the cement, which is a
major drawback.
vi. Bioactive glass: Bioactive glass has the ability to
raise pH level in an aqueous environment by
leaching of ions, viz. sodium, calcium, strontium,
etc. The antibacterial activity of bioglass is not
effective; prevents bacterial growth for a short-
term, but adversely affects the mechanical
properties of glass-ionomer cement.
vii. Miscellaneous: Antibiotics, plant extracts and
certain herbs have also been tried to achieve
antibacterial efficacy in glass-ionomer cement.
Antibiotics, though potent antibacterial, usually
have shown negative effect on mechanical
properties, especially compressive strength and
the bonding ability. Recently, addition of
ciprofloxacin (antibiotic) and zinc L-carnosine
(mucosal defensive agent) have shown improved
antibacterial effects, without altering the
mechanical properties. Miswak (Salvadora persica)
has shown promising results, without disturbing
the physical/mechanical properties.
Sodium fusidate (fusidic acid and sodium) has
shown effective anti-bacterial properties.
Propolis has also been tried as antibacterial
agent in glass-ionomer cement. Its effect was
observed initially, but not seen in long-term
studies. Even the cytotoxic studies have proved
that propolis is not safe.

I. Isolation
Glass-ionomer cement is very sensitive for water loss
as well as contamination. Saliva, sulcular fluid and
gingival hemorrhage have to be controlled during the
restoration procedure. Rubber dam, retraction cords
and cotton rolls with saliva ejectors are mandatory in
the restoration of glass-ionomer, especially, lesions
close to the gingival margins of the tooth. Details of
isolation devices are described in Chapter 11.
II. Tooth Preparation
Tooth preparation for glass-ionomer restoration may
be divided into:
A.Mechanical preparation
B. Surface treatment (conditioning).
A. Mechanical Preparation
In Class III cavities, the access is gained through the
lingual marginal ridge using small-sized inverted
cone bur, the bur is extended towards incisal and
gingival area depending upon the extent of caries.
The labial enamel is preserved as far as possible
(Fig. 20.9A, B).
In Class V cavities, the lesion can only be in enamel
or both in enamel and cementum. The cavity
preparation follows the access opening through the
lesion and extending towards the occlusal, gingival,
mesial and distal walls. In case the extent of lesion is
too wide, a groove on the occlusal/gingival wall can
be given. Similar grooves on all the four sides can be
given, if lesion so dictates (Fig. 20.10A to D).
The outline form is dictated primarily by caries,
discoloration and esthetics only.
In proximal lesions, no attempt should be made to
break the contact to bring the restoration to a self-
cleansing area. The unaffected tooth material should
be retained to keep as much contact as possible, even
if it is not well supported by the dentin.
The gingival extent of the tooth preparation is
generally dictated by the extent of the lesion. It is often
close to or extending into the sulcus. Care should be
taken  to  manage  the  sulcular  fluid  during  placement
of the restoration.
B. Surface Treatment (Conditioning)
After a thorough prophylaxis, the surface is
conditioned using appropriate chemical agent. 50%
citric acid,  25% tannic acid and 20% polyacrylic acid
are some of the conditioning agents suggested for
better bonding of the cement to enamel and dentin.
Polyacrylic acid is commonly applied for 10 to 20
seconds and then washed away.
Light-activated glass-ionomers have an additional
step of priming the tooth surface. The priming agent
is applied in two or more coats as suggested by the
manufacturers. It is then spread to a thin layer with
gentle blast of air and light activated for 20 seconds.
III. Manipulation of Cement
A. Dispensing Powder/Liquid
When dispensing the powder, shake the bottle lightly
first to fluff up the powder. See that the spoon is quite
full of powder and level off the surface using the lip on
the bottle, which is provided for the purpose.
The powder is dispensed first onto the slab and
divided into two halves with the spatula. The liquid
bottle is then tilted horizontal to allow the liquid to
displace the air through the nozzle and occupy the
nozzle. The bottle is then oriented vertically and
gently squeezed to dispense the required ratio of
liquid without the inclusion of air bubbles (Fig. 20.11A
to C). If the liquid has become more viscous over time,
it can be thinned down by immersing the bottle in
water at 70°C for 15 minutes. Allow it to cool again
before using the liquid.

B. Mixing
The cement is mixed either with hand or using syringe
technique (capsulated cement).  The mixing technique
should be followed according to the manufacturer
instructions.
Mixing can be carried out on a paper pad or a glass
slab (Fig. 20.11D). The advantage of a glass slab is that
it can be cooled a little to prolong the working time.
The primary objective of mixing the cement is to
merely wet the surface of the powder particles with
the liquid and not to aid in dissolving them entirely in
the liquid.
As powder and the liquid are dispensed on the pad,
one-half of the powder is mixed at a time by rolling the
powder into the liquid to wet the surface of the
powder particles. Do not vigorously spatulate. (Plastic
spatula is preferred over stainless steel spatula.) The
first half of the powder should be all wet within 10 to
15 seconds. Roll the remaining powder into the mass.
Do not spread the mix around the pad. The mixing is
completed in about 30 seconds.
C.  Slump Test
This test helps to recognize, if cement is properly
mixed. Lift the top of the pile of the cement with a
spatula. The cement breaks and slumps back. There
will be a point when the cement breaks but does not
slump. Working time is normally 60–90 seconds for
traditional glass-ionomer and about 3 minutes for
resin-modified glass-ionomer.
IV. Restoration
After mixing, both the chemical cure cement and
the light cure cement are carried in one bulk for
placement into the cavity. The use of a matrix is
desirable because it assists in positive placement of
the cement onto the tooth surface and also leads to
reduction of voids and porosities within the
restoration.
A syringe dispenser, like the Centrix syringe,
can be preferred. Syringing out the cement into the
prepared cavity helps to fill the cavity without
entrapment of air bubbles in the cement and gives
absolute control of quantity of cement placed in the
tooth. The tip of the syringe is placed close to floor of
the cavity and the cement ejected into the cavity as the
nozzle is withdrawn out. A light cure restoration
should be subjected to longer exposure than suggested
to avoid deficient light activation. A fully light
activated resin- modified glass-ionomer is resistant to
water uptake after completion of light activation, but
it is still possible to dehydrate the cement.

vi. Class I Cavities
In conservative Class I preparations, glass-ionomer

b. Lining
Glass-ionomer cement is used as lining cement. A
lining is a thin layer placed under a metallic restoration,
which is intended to afford thermal protection to the
pulp-dentin organ. It is also used to fill up undercuts
in cavity design where a line of withdrawal is required
as in an inlay cavity or crown preparation. The type-
III lining cement has limited physical properties and
should not be relied upon as a base for a composite
resin in the lamination technique. (Type-II restorative
or a light-cure cement is preferred.)
c. Luting
Glass-ionomer cement is used for luting crowns
and bridges, as well as orthodontic appliances. The
advantages are its anticariogenic property, low
solubility in oral environment, pulp compatibility,
good thixotropic flow, low film thickness, and also
the adhesive property and tooth color.
The tensile strength of the cement is not sufficient to
enhance retention. To ensure adequate flow properties,

the cement is mixed at a low powder to liquid ratio
(does not possess adequate physical properties).
The luting cements are fast setting and can, therefore,
be exposed to water contamination within 5 minutes
of the start of mix.
The correct mix of the cement for luting is
demonstrated by the string test. When mixed, it should
be able to string out from the pad to about 3.0 cm or
before it breaks and slumps back into the body of the
mix. A small quantity of the cement is painted on the
prepared tooth surface, using a small bristle brush, to
minimize the chances of air inclusions under the
crown. Then coat or fill the restoration with the cement
and seat it in place, applying occlusal forces.
Glass-ionomer does not have an elastic memory and the
restoration will not retreat from the tooth as happens with
zinc phosphate cement.
The excess cement may be removed immediately
upon initial set with a sharp instrument. These luting
cements are radiopaque; any proximal residue can be
detected with a radiograph.
Some patients experience severe sensitivity on
seating of the restoration with this cement. The reason
attributed is the hydraulic displacement of dentinal
fluid. Hence, it is desirable to seal the dentin tubules
by painting the prepared surface with a low viscosity
resin enamel bond or apply a remineralizing solution.
d. Bilayered/Sandwich Restorations
Traditonal type-II and light-cure glass-ionomer
cements are used as base, over which composite
restoration is placed (sandwich restoration). The
comparatively better adhesion of the cement allows it
to bond to the dentin in erosive lesions, leaving out
small area of enamel that can be used for bonding
composites. It is also used for bonding dental amalgam
to posterior teeth where there is compromised retention
form due to loss of walls to support the amalgam.
Glass-ionomer is preferred as a base in Class II lesions,
where cervical extension is subgingival. A glass-
ionomer is easier to place in these locations than the
technique sensitive composite (Fig. 20.22A to D).
