Restorative dentistry is a blend of science and art. The
success of restorative dentistry is determined on the
basis of functional and esthetic results. To achieve
esthetics, four basic determinants are required in
sequence; viz. position, contour, texture and color.
Color makes an object more appealing, attractive and
gives the pleasure of observing. Color is a
psychophysical sensation provoked by eye in the presence
of light and interpreted by the brain. Color combination
not only improves esthetics, but also makes the
restoration appear natural and attractive. The basic
fundamentals of color and light, the radiation
spectrum and the optical characteristics of the object
are to be understood before evaluating and selecting
proper color shade for the restoration.
SOURCE OF LIGHT
Light is mandatory for any color to exist. Light is a
form of electromagnetic energy, which is a small part
of the large and continuous radiation spectrum
(Fig. 24.1). Sunlight or the light of a glowing body is
not single type of wavelength but a combination of
different wavelengths (measured in nanometers, i.e.
millionths of a millimeter). Light in the wavelength
range of 380 to 760 nm is referred to as the visible
light. Wavelengths less than 380 nm are in the
ultraviolet range (cosmic rays, gamma rays and X-
rays). Wavelengths above 760 nm are in the infrared
range (radar waves, TV waves and radio waves).
A beam of light when passed through a glass prism
gets converted into its component parts depending
upon the wavelength. They are viewed as a series of
glowing spectral colors ranging from violet, blue,
green, yellow and red without any clear demarcation
between them. White light is produced when all these
colors are combined together. The wavelength range
of different colors is shown in Table 24.1.
OPTICAL CHARACTERISTICS
When light falls on an object, it is either reflected,
transmitted or absorbed. If the whole light is reflected,
the object appears white; whereas, if the light is
entirely absorbed, the object appears black. Greater
the intensity of the light source, or greater the amount
of light reflected, higher is the brightness of the object
being viewed. The final effect is determined by the
transparent, translucent or opaque characteristics of
the object.
The optical features are as follows:
a. Reflection

the surface of the object being hit, instead of being
transmitted or absorbed. Reflection can be of two
types:
i. Specular reflection: The light is reflected from the
point of incidence in a single direction (Fig. 24.2).

A medium is said to be opaque when it transmits no
light (Fig. 24.8); for example, a brick wall. The incident
light is either refracted or absorbed depending upon
the nature of the medium. The degree of translucency

g. Light Scattering
Whenever light is transmitted through a body, the
intensity of the beam is reduced, because of the fact
that part of the energy is refracted at each point of
change in medium (Fig. 24.9). For example, consider
light striking a window glass. Part of it is reflected on
the outside surface and the rest is refracted towards
the glass pane. As the light leaves the pane to enter
air, another portion is reflected and the remainder is
refracted in the air. When the medium is
heterogeneous, the number of reflections and
refractions occurring inside the medium is
considerably increased. This phenomenon is known
as ‘scattering of light’. The amount of light scattered
depends upon the difference in refractive indices of
the dispersed and the dispersion medium. Scattering
within limits produces translucency in a transparent
medium, while an excessive scattering produces
opacity.
h. Surface Gloss
The amount of specular and diffuse reflection
occurring on a surface is determined by its smooth-
ness. Ceramic surfaces have slight irregularities;
hence, the reflection is a combination of specular and
diffuse types. On a glazed surface, specular reflection
predominates; whereas, on a rough surface, diffuse
reflection predominates (Fig. 24.10A, B).

Color is the property of a surface or substance due to
the absorption of certain light rays and reflection of
others within the range of wavelengths (380 to
760 nm) adequate to excite the retinal receptors.
Briefly, the color is a result of selective absorption and
selective reflection. For example, an object appears
red when it absorbs all constituent lights of the
spectrum except red, which is reflected back and
reaches the eye. Color perception is psychophysical
process—‘a psychological response to a physical

STRUCTURE AND FUNCTION OF EYE
The main features of human eye are shown in
Fig. 24.11. Light enters eye through the cornea.
Immediately behind cornea is the iris. It is a fibrous
membrane with central  circular aperture, which
contracts or expands to control the amount of light
entering the eye. This compensating property of the
eye is known as chromic adaptation. Eye lens is
situated behind the iris and has sufficient refracting
power to focus the image of the object on the retina.
Ciliary muscles control the focal length of the eye lens.
Various layers of the retina are shown in Fig. 24.12.
From inside to outside, these consist of pigment cells,
photosensitive receptors, i.e. rods (rod shaped) and
cones (cone shaped), bipolar cells, ganglion cells and
optic nerve.
PERCEPTION OF COLOR
Irrespective of the nature of light source, color results
only from a reflected or transmitted beam of white
light or a portion of it. There should be enough light
to elicit a color. No object appears colored at low levels
of illumination. This is because at low lights, the more
sensitive rods get functional, which are unable to
differentiate color.
Color perception involves series of events, viz.
(a) light source, (b) surface viewed, (c) individual
observer (eye and brain), and (d) surrounding
environment under which the object is being viewed
(Fig. 24.13). All these features are interrelated and a
variation in any may affect the color perception.
a. Light Source
Light source should be without the dominance of any
wavelength. [If an object is viewed under particular
wavelength (color bands), the specific color becomes
dominant to the observer]. Different types of
lights and their designation by CIE (Commission

b. Fluorescence
Fluorescence is the absorption of radiation of a
particular wavelength and its re-emission as radiation
of longer wavelength.
Natural tooth has fluorescing qualities because of
its organic content [absorbs wavelengths between 300
and 380 nm (ultraviolet range)]. Sunlight, photoflash
lamps, UV lights, etc. are sources of ultraviolet
radiations. The energy absorbed by the tooth is then
released as radiations of longer wavelengths,
generally in the range of 400–500 nm (yellow white
fluorescence). Because of fluorescence, the human
tooth has the brightness and life-like appearance. Any
restoration that is to replace natural tooth structure
should contain fluorescing agents. It is important to
match the shade under two or more lighting
conditions, out of which one should have adequate
UV component (incandescent lights).
c. Opalescence
Opalescence is the phenomenon in which a material
appears to be of one color when light is reflected from
it and of another color when light is transmitted
through it. Opals act like prisms and refract different
wavelengths to varying degrees. The shorter wave-
lengths refract more to escape an optically dense
material than the longer wavelength. The hydroxy-
apatite crystals of enamel also act as prisms. Wave-
lengths of light have different degrees of translucency.

b. Surface Viewed
The surface  of  an  object  being  viewed  affects  color
determination via absorption, reflection and trans-
mission. The surface is analyzed with a spectrophoto-
meter. (The percentage of reflectance compared with
the total amount of light incident on the surface is
recorded.)
Three types of light sources are:
i. Incandescent light: Emits high concentration of
yellow waves (not suitable for shade matching;
low color rendering index).
ii. Fluorescent light: Emits high concentration of blue
waves (not suitable for shade matching; average
color rendering index).
iii. Daylight: Northern daylight closely emits full
spectrum of white light (used in color matching;
best color rendering index).
c. Individual Observer (Eye and Brain)
Eye is most sensitive to brightness in wavelength
between 540 and 570 nm (yellow-green); thereon, the
brightness diminishes in both directions. Interpretation
of color by the brain differs for different observers. The
color perception is also affected by the eye fatigue.
Prolonged viewing of a color for 15–30 seconds strains
the cones and causes depletion of photopigments. The
cones can be made sensitive to the desired color by
gazing at the complementary color.
d. Surrounding Environment
The surrounding environment also influence the
interpretation of the color, viz. background, wet/dry
conditions, angle and intensity of illumination, etc.

BASIC COLOR SCHEMES
The color wheel or color circle (Fig. 24.14) is the basic
tool for combining colors. The first color circle was
designed by Sir Isaac Newton in 1666. The accepted
circle is a wheel of 12 colors; three primary colors (red,
yellow and blue), three secondary colors (green,
orange and violet), created by mixing two primary
colors and six tertiary colors created by mixing
primary and secondary colors (Table 24.3).
• Split complementary color scheme (In addition to
the base color, it uses two colors adjacent to its
complement).
• Square color scheme (Similar to rectangle, but all
four colors spaced evenly around the color circle).
The combining of colors is explained by following
theories.
a. Additive Color Theory
The additive primary colors are red, green and blue
(RGB). Combining one additive primary colors with
equal amounts of other results in the additive
secondary colors of cyan, magenta and yellow
(Fig. 24.15). Combining all three additive primary
colors in equal amounts will produce the color white.
Remember combining additive colors creates lighter
colors; so adding all three primary colors results in a
color so ‘light’, [seen as white (Table 24.4)]. By changing
ratio of the three primary colors by varying degrees,
a wide range of colors can be produced (Table 24.5).
b. Subtractive Color Theory
The subtractive primary colors are cyan, magenta and
yellow (Fig. 24.15). Subtractive color mixing occurs
when light is reflected off a surface or is filtered
through a translucent object. For example, a red
surface only appears red, because it absorbs (subtracts)
all the light that is not red and reflects only the red
light.

COLOR HARMONIES/COLOR CHORDS
The color combinations that are considered pleasing
are called color harmonies or color chords. They
consist of two or more colors having fixed relation in
the color wheel.
These are:
• Complementary color scheme (Colors that are
opposite to each other on the color wheel are
considered to be complementary colors; for

• Analogous color scheme (Colors that are next to
each other on the color wheel).
• Triadic color scheme (Three colors that are evenly
spaced around the color wheel).
• Tetradic or rectangular color scheme (Four colors
arranged into two complementary pairs).

COLOR SYSTEMS
Color, perceived when light reflected from an object
enters the eye, consists of ‘quantity’ and ‘quality’.
‘Quantity’ is the variable lightness (value) and is the
total amount of light reflected from an object
(indicated by the relative nearness of a color to white
or black on an achromatic gray scale, such as black
and white photograph). ‘Quality’ is the chromatic
component of color consisting of two variables, which
can be represented in two dimensions.
The systems describing color of tooth are as follows.
1. Munsell Color System
Munsell color system defines color by hue, saturation
and lightness. The most widely used system arranges
the three dimensions of the color in equal visual steps.
The Munsell color axis simulates a cylinder
(Fig. 24.16). The Munsell cylinder is formed by a series
of wheels stacked one upon the other. The hub of each
wheel represents the ‘value axis’. A colorless or
achromatic axis extends through the center of the
cylinder; pure white at the top (assigned a value of
10) and pure black at the bottom (assigned a value of
0). A series of greys progressing from black to white
in equal steps connects these two extremities. Hues
are arranged sequentially around the rim of the wheel.
There are 100 hues in this scale [10 major hues (5
principal and 5 intermediate hues)]; red (R), yellow (Y),
green (G), blue (B) and purple (P) are the principal
hues; whereas, yellow-red (YR), green-yellow (GY),
blue-green (BG), purple-blue (PB) and red-purple (RP)
are the intermediate hues. Within each hue, the colors
are arranged in scales according to their lightness/
darkness (value) and their purity (saturation). Colors
with lower value numbers are known as ‘dark colors’
and are arranged towards the bottom. Conversely,
colors with higher value numbers are known as ‘light
colors’ and are arranged towards the top.
The spokes of the wheel represent the saturation
(chroma) of a color. The chroma scale starts from zero
and extends to a maximum of fourteen.
The designation of any particular color located in
the Munsell color axis is given by the notation HV/
C, where H stands for hue, V for value and C for
chroma, e.g. 8R3/7 would mean hue is 8 (red), value
is 3 and chroma is 7. The scale of value extends from
0/, 1/, 2/ …. 10/ and chroma from /0, /1, /2 …../14
or farther. Most of the dental shade guides are based
on a section of the Munsell color system that includes
color range of the natural teeth.
Munsell color is defined in three dimensions—hue,
chroma and value. (Value is determined first, followed by
chroma; hue is described last by matching with shade tabs
of value and chroma).
Hue
Hue distinguishes one color family from another, e.g.
red, yellow, blue, etc. (type of color) (Fig. 24.17A). It
is specified as the dominant range of wavelengths in
the visible spectrum that yield the perceived color.
Hue is represented by A, B, C and D on the
commercial vita shade guide.
Chroma
Chroma distinguishes a strong color from a weak one
[saturation/intensity of a hue (Fig. 29.17B)]. This
dimension can be more easily explained by employing
the concept of painting a box. Suppose one side of the
box is painted in pure red. Now before painting the
second side, a little amount of gray is added to the
previous red; the color perceived is weak red.
Similarly, while painting the third side, more amount
of gray is added; the color becomes more weak. Adding
gray reduces the chroma, while hue remains the same.

Value
Value, the amount of light returned from an object,
distinguishes whiteness from blackness. A color of
high value is transmitted as light gray and that of a
low value as dark gray (Fig. 24.17C). If a series of
colors have the same values, all of them would be
composed of the same grays. A younger tooth is
generally whiter and brighter than its older
counterpart. Increase in the thickness of opaque or
core porcelain raises the value; whereas, an increase
in enamel (translucent) porcelain reduces the value.
Lowering value means less light returns from the
illuminated object and the rest is being absorbed or
scattered elsewhere.
The value of a specific color is the value of gray
added to the original color. If the gray of same value
is added each time, no change in value occurs, only
the chroma reduces. On adding a value higher than
the original value of the color, the resulting color has
the same hue, reduced chroma and higher value. On
adding a value lower than the original value, the
resulting color has the same hue, reduced chroma and
reduced value.
2. CIE System
The Commission Internationale de l'Eclairage (CIE)
described color concept based on the theory that eye
possesses receptor for three primary colors (red, green
and blue) and all other colors are seen as mixture of
these three primary colors.
CIE introduced instrumental color measurement
based on the rationale that a color sensation can be
matched by the mixing of three primary wavelengths
(red, green and blue; represented by [R], [G] and [B]).
In CIE system, a series of (human) observers were
employed, using a 2°viewing angle. The observers
determined the amount of the three primaries that
were needed to match all spectral colors within the
visible spectrum. The results obtained for the
observers were collected and the concept of a standard
observer was introduced.
Primaries: The three imaginary primaries are
specified as [X], [Y] and [Z].
Color matching functions: These characterize the
response of the observer, denoted as x–, y– and z–.
Standard observer: Earlier 2° viewing angle was
used; however, a 10° angle is accepted in principle.
Tristimulus values: The amount of the three
primaries (i.e. R, G and B) are known as the tristimulus
values of the color [C]. These are denoted as X, Y and
Z. The color of an object depends on:
• Spectral energy distribution of the light that
illuminates the sample (S)
• Reflectance characteristics of the sample (R)
• Sensitivity of the observer (color matching
functions).
The tristimulus value is the amount of a primary
that will match the color of a given object. The given
color is matched by:
X[X] + Y[Y] + Z [Z]

OPTICS OF NATURAL TEETH
Color of natural tooth is a result of light reflected
directly from the tooth surface combined with light
reflected from the dentin and a minor contribution
from the oral background. Dentin is the primary
source of color and the rays reflected from it are
transmitted via enamel (modified by the thickness and
degree of enamel translucency). The effect of enamel
thickness and translucency on tooth color is evident
in the incisal, middle and cervical thirds of the tooth,
which needs to be duplicated in porcelain restorations
(Fig. 24.19A) using translucent, opaque, enamel and
dentin porcelain (Fig. 24.19B).
a. Incisal third of tooth: This is primarily composed
of translucent enamel; the shade may vary from
light to dark-gray to bluish. The translucency and
the grayness may extend into approximal areas and
sometimes into dentin. The color of incisal third is
referred to as the ‘enamel color’.
b. Middle third of tooth: This area is primarily
composed of dentin. The color of the middle third
region is determined by the dentin hue having been
modified by the overlying translucent blue gray
enamel. It generally falls in the range of orange-
red, blue-yellow or gray-yellow. The color of
middle third is referred to as the ‘body dentin color’.
c. Cervical third of tooth: In this area, the enamel is
thin and the underlying dentin color is quite
marked. The color of the cervical half region is
referred to as the ‘gingival dentin color’ and is in
the range of orange-yellow to brown.

DENTAL SHADE GUIDES
Dental shade guides are a collection of various color
combinations available from the manufacturers;
which aid the operator in selecting the most
acceptable color match with the tooth/teeth in
question. Their use depends on the adequacy of the
shade guide and the familiarity of the operator with
the color matching procedures. The requirements for
any shade guide include: (1) A logical arrangement
of colors in the color space, and (2) an adequate
distribution of colors in color space. An ideal color
space is one in which each color is the center of a
sphere, and the next closest one matches the color
surrounding it.
Most manufacturers provide shade guides for
their material and it is advisable that these should
not be used for shade matching of materials from
other manufacturers. Many shade guide systems use
hue as the basis to define color. Within each hue,
there are various levels of chroma (or  saturation).
The third and most critical component for color
selection (value) has no accountability and is not
addressed in most shade guides. The VITA Classical
Shade Guide is the most commonly used shade
guide; however, it does not address value. Tabs are
grouped into letters as:
A (red-yellow hue) : A1 A2 ….. so on
B (yellow hue) : B1 B2 ….. so on
C (grey hue) : C1 C2 …… so on
D (red-yellow grey) : D1 D2 ……. so on
Chroma is designated with numbers 1, 2, 3, 4.
The VITA 3D Master Shade Guide revolutionized
shade selection, and made the value as the base of
color. With the 3D-MasterGuide, as each color
component is selected, it  is  held constant  while  the
next color component is found. The traditional VITA
Classical Shade Guide has 16 shade tabs and can be
difficult for proper shade selection. The VITA 3D-
Master   allows   for considerably more interpretation.
VITA has also introduced the VITA Linear-guide,
which simplifies the arrangement of the original VITA
3D-Master by separating the value tabs into their own
holders. The chroma and hue tabs are arranged
separately within each value group. This allows quick
determination of the value before moving onto
chroma and hue (Fig. 24.20). The tabs are grouped
into five categories (number in sequence) with
increasing value (1, 2, 3, 4, 5). Hue is designated with
letters L, M and R. Chroma is also designated as 1, 2,
3, 4, 5 (For example, 3M2 correspond to value 3 and
hue M and chroma 2). Chromascope uses different
numbers to distinguish hue [100 (white), 200 (yellow),
300 (orange), 400 (green), 500 (brown)]. Chroma is also
designated in numbers (10, 20 … so on).
Shofu offered the Natural Color Concept (NCC).
Egger conducted a worldwide clinical study in which
the tooth shades of more than 3500 individuals were
recorded and analyzed spectrophotometrically. The
NCC color indicator, derived from these data, served
as basis for the dental color space. The NCC system
consists of 208 color blends based on 38 basic shades.
These blends are arranged in L*a*b* color space.
Shade guides are of two types: Key ring guides and
metal slotted type fixed in plastic holders (mostly
preferred).
Limitations of Shade Guide
• Because of reflectance, refractiveness, opacity,
translucency and transparency, a given shade
button may  not match the tooth color.
• Thickness of the porcelain tab may be different
from that of the prosthesis to be made (show
different color).
• Porcelain shade from the same manufacturer may
vary from batch to batch.
• Variation in surface texture of a tooth and shade
button may affect color matching.
• Most shade buttons have a shape of maxillary
central incisor, introducing another variable in
shade matching.
• The necks of the shade tabs are usually darker in
shade; need to be ground off for proper matching.
• The ‘numbered’ information sent to laboratory may
vary in color of their shade guide.
• Light is reflected and transmitted through shade
guide tabs (look translucent); whereas in
restoration, light is reflected and not transmitted
(look opaque and dense).

SHADE-TAKING DEVICES
Various devices have been designed to aid clinicians
and technicians to specify and manage  tooth color
(Fig. 24.21).
The common devices are as follows.
a. Colorimeters
Colorimeters generally use three or four silicon filters
that simulate the standard observer functions. The
filter colorimeters are considered inferior to scanning
devices, because of their inability to match the
observer function, especially at low light levels.
However, the device is used for quality control.
b. Digital Cameras (Filter Colorimeter)
The digital camera captures images using CCDs,
which contain millions of microscopically small light-
sensitive elements (photosites). There are several ways
of recording the three colors in a digital camera;
mostly use three separate sensors, each with a
different filter. Light is directed to the different filter/
sensor combinations by placing a beam splitter in the
camera. The beam splitter allows each detector to see
the image simultaneously.
c. Scanning (Spectrophotometers and
Spectroradiometers)
There are two types of designs commonly used for
scanning. The traditional scanning instrument
consists of a single photodiode detector that records
the amount of light at each wavelength. The light is
divided into small wavelength intervals by passing
through a monochromator. The present design uses
a diode array with a dedicated element for each
wavelength. This design allows for the simultaneous
integration of all wavelengths. Both designs are
slower than digital cameras; but remain the preferred
tool to develop accurate color-measuring devices.
Advantages of digital shade matching
• Shade matching is objective; can be verified repeatedly.
• Surrounding environment do not influence shade
matching.
• Provide exact information; no subjectivity
• Color replication in the laboratory is accurate.
• Less chairside time; less fatigue.
Limitations of digital shade matching
• Accuracy may get affected by phenomenon of edge
loss
• Position of mouthpiece can change color measurement,
when repeated
• Laboratory should have the same system
• Expensive

GUIDELINES FOR CLINICAL SHADE SELECTION
The features involved in clinical shade selection are
as follows:
1. Lighting of the Operatory
The optimal light source in the northern sunlight in
the middle of the day with a slight overcast sky
(intensity—1500). This light contains almost equal
blend of all wavelengths of light (Morning and
evening exposures are rich in blue wavelength and
reddish yellow wavelength, respectively).
Since, the intensity of daylight varies, the systems
having the  same spectrum  as natural daylight have
been proposed. The capacity of an artificial light to
reproduce natural light is measured by using color-
temperature and color rendering index (CRI).
White light, which is considered the best source
for color comparison, is assigned a color rendering
index (CRI) of 100. Any artificial sources with an index
over 90 are considered adequate.
The color corrected fluorescent lamps, with a CRI
of over 90, are available that provide optimal color
matching. Chair light is not recommended for color-
matching procedures.
Before proceeding for color selection, the patient’s
profession should be ascertained, e.g. whether the
person works indoors under fluorescent lamps?
Whether the patient is an actor and works on stage?
Whether he/she is exposed more to sunlight?
Whether he/she is a night club performer? The lights
should be selected, which closely simulate the
working conditions of the patient, e.g. a night club
performer should be viewed under incandescent and
fluorescent light sources.

Intensity of light is also important. The ratio of
intraoral to extraoral illumination should be 3 to 5:1
with extra oral light intensity of 200–300 foot candles.
This amount of lighting also reduces eye fatigue by
decreasing the difference in brightness levels of the
light needed for execution of intraoral procedures.
The location of light can be from ceiling or surface
mounted installation.
The luminous ceiling is achieved by illuminating
the ceiling by fluorescent lighting.
Open perimeter surface mounted installation
involves using fluorescent fixtures only at the
perimeter of the ceiling and not in the center. Open
perimeter recessed installation restricts lateral and
upward loss of light by providing a proper reflective
surface to project the light downwards.
2. Surrounding Environment
Bright-colored surroundings influence the reflected
light and interfere with color matching. The bright-
colored curtains, wall paints, etc. should be avoided
in the dental operatory. A drape can be used to mask
an undesirable color in the patient’s clothing,
provided the drape itself does not introduce an
undesirable reflecting color. Lipstick should be
removed so that it does not affect the perceived color.
Light gray is an ideal background for color matching.
Surfaces with high gloss produce disturbing glares
and should be avoided.
3. Tooth Condition
The concerned tooth and the adjoining teeth on both
sides should be free of surface stains prior to color
matching. The tooth should be moist while selecting
the shade as dehydration results in a whiter
appearance. Dehydration also results in loss of water
from enamel, increasing the internal scattering of light
and consequently the amount of reflected light
reaching the operator. Dehydration becomes a specific
problem when considerably long time is spent in
arriving at a final color match. Under such situations,
the patient is suggested to close the mouth between
comparisons so that the teeth are moistened by
running the tongue over them.
Shade matching should be carried out before the
application  of  rubber  dam.  It  prevents  any  light
reflected from the dam to interfere with shade
selection and provides a more natural environment.
The isolated tooth may take at least 30 minutes to
regain its original color.

• The tooth surface is cleaned.
• Lipstick is to be removed.
• Bright-colored clothes should be covered with neutral

4. Patient and Operator Position
The patient should be seated upright with the oral
cavity at the observer's eye level. The operator should
stand directly in front of the patient when selecting a
shade.
The shade selection is carried out at 12–16 inches
distance (Distance from which the patient’s teeth are
usually observed).
5. Time of Selection
The color selection process should be performed when
the operator is able to devote sufficient time. The
shade matching should be performed at the beginning
of the day when eye fatigue has not set in.
PROCEDURE FOR SHADE MATCHING IN
PORCELAIN RESTORATIONS
The shade guide is arranged according to four basic
hues: A, B, C and D (Table 24.8). In each hue, there
are divisions like A1, A2, A3, A3.5 and A4, which
represent different chroma, and similar divisions in
B, C and D. Another less common arrangement in
shade guide is according to the value: B1, A1, B2, D2,
C1, C2, D4, A3, D3, A3.5, B4, C3, A4 and C4.
The first step is to select the ‘hue’. Since there is
not much difference amongst the different hues, and
to simplify the procedure, ‘the four hues technique’
is used. In this, the maximum chromas of each hue,
i.e. A4, B4, C4 and D4, are selected and placed at equal
distances. This permits effectively visualize the
differences in hue because chromas of maximum
intensity are placed besides each other. The shade is
selected in no more than five seconds to avoid fatigue
of cones in the retina.
After the basic hue is determined, a color that
closely matches the tooth, i.e. chroma is chosen. For
this, all the different chromas of the selected tab, say
B, i.e. B1, B2, B3, B3.5 and B4, are placed one by one
alongside the tooth to be restored. For simulating
natural conditions more closely, the shade tab is
covered with the patient’s lips or operator’s thumb
to create a shadow effect. A general guideline that
may assist the clinician while selecting a chroma is:
• Maxillary incisors and premolars are similar in
chroma, with the central incisors being the lightest.
• Mandibular incisors are generally one chroma
lighter than the maxillary incisors.
• Maxillary canines are at least two chroma darker
than the maxillary incisors (applies particularly to
the dentin shade).
The second shade guide (arranged according to
value) is used to select the value. Avoid considering
the hue and chroma when choosing for a value.
Squinting aids in selection as it sensitizes the more
sensitive black and white rods. The value is selected
and the results again evaluated by matching against
the corresponding opaque shade.
Surface Texture
A smooth surface reflects most of the incident light
back to the observer; whereas, a roughened surface
scatters light in many directions.
Newly erupted teeth have a rough surface texture.
As the age advances, the surface features are worn
down and the tooth appears smooth with a highly
reflective glossy surface. The surface characteristics
should be simulated for a particular age group so as
to achieve a life-like restoration.
Esthetics Prescription Form
The esthetics prescription form contains detailed
directions about the color. An elaborate diagram
(Fig. 24.22) describes the location of the different
characteristics, like incisal translucency, areas of high
chroma, hypocalcified areas, craze lines and stains. If
additional depth of translucency is required, it is
marked as ‘overlay’ of transparent porcelain. An
increase in the thickness of enamel porcelain may alter
the shade considerably, whereas a thin layer of
overlying porcelain will increase the depth of
translucency with little alteration in the shade. The
translucency of the incisal tip can be accentuated by
placing translucent porcelain behind the incisal edge.
‘Stain colors’ can be marked to represent areas of craze
lines, hypocalcified areas, etc.

Identification Tab
A tab mold using maxillary incisor is fabricated,
coating a thin layer of porcelain on the mold. The
selected opaque, dentin and incisal porcelains are then
layered in prescribed thickness. Characterizations,
such as, deep translucency, mamelons, craze lines and
hypocalcification can be incorporated into the
identification tab.
Characterization Map
An exact location of hypocalcification/translucency
pattern is recorded for duplication in a ceramic
restoration. The hypocalcification or translucency
patterns are drawn on the contralateral tooth of the
working side using Boley gauge. An alternative
procedure is to paste transparent tape on the
contralateral tooth and draw the desired pattern on
the tape.
Staining/Modification of Stain
Ceramic stains used for surface modifications are
usually metallic oxide pigments. These are similar in
composition to the colorants used in opaque and body
porcelains.
Stains can be applied on the glazed or unglazed
porcelain. When applied to an unglazed surface, the
stain penetrates to a much greater depth creating a
more diffused appearance. For subdued changes, the
stain may be applied directly over the glaze. No
matter to what surface the stain is applied, it should
be absolutely clean and devoid of any contaminants.
The modification of shade is carried out by the
application of different stains. The stain powder is
mixed with enough liquid medium to produce a
heavy creamy consistency. A small increment of stain
is picked up with the brush tip and applied over the
surface. Any excess is removed with the brush.
The foremost rule for shade modification is
‘moderation’. Only minor changes should be
attempted. The operator should avoid changing the
basic hue as he/she may lose control over the selected
color. Complementary hue reduces both the chroma
and value with a minimal loss in translucency. An
over addition of complement also change the basic
hue.
