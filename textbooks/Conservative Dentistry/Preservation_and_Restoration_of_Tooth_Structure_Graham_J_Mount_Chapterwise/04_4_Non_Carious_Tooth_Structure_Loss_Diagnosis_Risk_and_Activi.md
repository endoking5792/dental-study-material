## N

on‐carious tooth structure loss includes a number of mechanisms that change the morphology of a tooth from the time of eruption. The mechanisms can be divided into two general groups: (i) physiological mechanisms that have always been present throughout human evolution; and (ii) pathologic mechanisms, many of which relate to the modern lifestyle.
Abrasive loss of the tooth surface resulting from functional use, such as the mastication of food, and attrition that results from tooth grinding, are changes that normally result in physiological adaptations within the masticatory system and that do not therefore compromise health or function. However, the body has a limited ability to adapt to structural loss through corrosion, acid‐accelerated wear, abfraction, enamel flaking, cusp splitting or tooth fracture. Each of these conditions may therefore require active intervention to preserve or restore health and function.
Resorption of tooth structure through the action of clastic cells is not common in permanent teeth, but it can occur. If untreated, it can lead to tooth loss. As complete an understanding as possible of the nature of the various forms of resorption is essential for good management.

### Non‐Carious Loss of Tooth Surface Structure

The principal causes of non‐carious, tooth surface structural loss have been outlined in Chapter 1. They are abrasion, attrition, corrosion, abfraction and various forms of fracture.
More than one process may be acting on teeth simultaneously, sometimes making accurate diagnosis difficult. For example, facet borders may not be distinctively sharp, or may not even exist, due to the simultaneous presence of abrasion and corrosion. Tooth grinding combined with abrasion on an acid‐corroded surface may remove more tooth substance than normal because of the softened enamel surface.
This inter‐play of forces may complicate prevention and care.
However, with a clear understanding of the non‐carious tooth surface mechanisms and their clinical characteristics, and with a thorough medical and dental history, the causes, and therefore appropriate treatment, will become evident.

The ‘MI’ approach to managing non‐carious tooth surface loss
The ‘MI’ philosophy, namely the maximal medical, biochemical and behavioural intervention to prevent and cure disease, combined with the minimally invasive physical actions necessary to restore form and function, applies to many aspects of dental clinical management, not only to the management of carious lesions. The MI approach should be used in the overall management of non‐carious tooth surface loss. Doing this allows a systematic approach that is also specific to the needs of each patient. The general principles are outlined below.

### Identify the mechanism(s) responsible

### for the non‐carious surface loss

If non‐carious tooth surface loss is observed in a patient, the clinician first needs to identify which of the mechanisms are present, and a thorough clinical examination is essential. The causes have been defined and their clinical presentation outlined in Chapter 1.
Second, the clinician needs to determine if the mechanisms observed are currently active or is the loss evidence of past activity. Third, the clinician must determine if the tooth surface loss is physiological and requires no intervention or if it is indeed a pathology that needs to be managed.
Finally, the clinician needs to understand why this is occurring in this individual. By proper patient questioning and identifying the mechanisms involved, the clinician should be able to identify the specific contributing factors responsible.

### Prevention

If the mechanisms involved are pathological, then the clinician needs to arrest them and, once stopped, prevent the problem from continuing or recurring. That is to say, a preventive programme must be ‘tailor‐made’ for each patient. Proper patient communication is of paramount importance. The patient must understand that it is their problem and must accept responsibility for the prevention, following the guidance of the clinician.

### Monitor

There is then a continuing need to objectively monitor the mechanism in the future to ensure that the problem has been arrested and is not allowed to recur.
There are times when restorative management is required as well and this can range from placement of simple restorations to full mouth rehabilitation. Although there are multiple approaches that can be used, with a choice of many different restorative materials, the prime focus is to conserve tooth structure wherever possible and use bio‐mimetic materials that seal margins, chemically bond to the tooth and support an overall remineralizing environment.

### Corrosion (Erosion)

Corrosion is a modern‐day pathology with a prevalence of up to 40–60% in some populations. Except for the very early stages that are nano‐scale, or not clinically obvious, corrosion can readily be identified clinically because of its ‘frosted or glazed appearance’. The dishing out of labial, buccal or lingual enamel surfaces, and the occlusal scooping out of dentine will be apparent (see Chapter 1). In today’s environment, because food is generally processed and relatively soft, the amount of abrasive wear on teeth, in the absence of acid acceleration, is very low.
As a result, most cases of observed dentinal scooping of occlusal surfaces in patients will now result from corrosion. It must also be remembered that the presence of acid, in combination with even minimal abrasion or attrition, can cause excessive tooth tissue loss from acid‐accelerated wear (sometimes called ‘erosive wear’). Furthermore, dietary corrosion is directly related to lifestyle that requires effective patient communication and extensive counselling to bring about change.

Activity of corrosion (erosion)
Once clinical evidence of corrosion is confirmed, the clinician needs to determine if it is currently active or is the result of a past problem. This is important as it will determine the management approach. For example, it would be inadvisable to proceed with complex and expensive treatment in a dentition that is actively dissolving.
Generally, dentine exposed by active corrosion is temperature‐ sensitive because the repeated acid attack keeps the dentinal tubules open to the oral environment. In fact, sensitivity to cold is a good diagnostic feature for active corrosion. Second, in active corrosion, the tooth surfaces often appear to be very clean – free from biofilm and staining, with the ‘glazed surface’ appearance, described above, being clearly evident. Alternatively, there are situations where the corrosion has been mildly active over many years, often acting in cycles, with infrequent or occasional sensitivity and therefore with little, if any, evident
‘glazing’.
The level of corrosive activity can be readily determined using a scratch test. Use a No. 12 scalpel or an explorer to make a faint

Specific contributory factors
The effect of acid on dental tissues is determined by many variables, including the type of acid, the strength of the acid (pH and titratable values), the amount of acid consumed, frequency of exposure, the time the acid is consumed and subsequent oral clearance. Other variables that also affect the final outcome, but can be considered as protective factors, include previous fluoride exposure, the degree of enamel maturity, the presence of biofilm, and the quality and quantity of saliva.
It must be remembered that some acids (e.g. citric), apart from the damaging effect resulting from the H+ ions, have a chelating effect where Ca++ is leached out from apatite, thereby adding to the demineralizing effects. Similarly, although pH is the measure of H+ ions in concentration, and is used as a measure of the potential to cause dissolution, the titratable values of acids are at times more important [1]. Titratable values are an indication of the number of H+ ion available to react. This means that two acids may have the same pH, but the one with the higher titratable value will be potentially more damaging.
Thus, acids from grapefruit and pineapple juices are more damaging than cola drinks even though they have a lower pH.
Careful patient questioning and assessment are required to identify contributing erosive factors that are specific for that patient. Identifying specific drinks consumed regularly, the frequency of consumption, medical conditions (e.g. bulimia,
Sjögren’s syndrome, etc.), medications (e.g. asthma puffers) and poly‐pharmacy (i.e. causing hypo‐salivation), balanced against the protective factors mentioned above, will collectively give an indication of risk associated with that patient. This will, in turn, influence the management of the problem.
The variability created by all these possible confounding factors is so great that it is difficult to determine the possible

multiple levels of risk. Placing individuals in ‘low risk’ or ‘high risk’ categories, albeit still subjective, is an acceptable technique.

### Source of acid from corrosion patterns

Identifying the source of an acid (i.e. intrinsic or extrinsic) will direct the clinician towards identifying the aetiological agents and thus help target the management.
The pattern of corrosion that occurs may help to identify the source of the acid, but considerable variation is possible. The direction of acid movement within the mouth, the variations in saliva clearance, and even the individual pattern of swallowing may have an effect. There are specific patterns that may identify the source of an acid, but over time, the patterns appear to merge, making it difficult to identify the source (Figure 4.1).
Generally, intrinsic acids affect the palatal aspects of the upper molars. Partial regurgitation of stomach contents, or reflux, affects the palatal of the upper third molars more than the second molars, with the first molars affected least. However, vomiting will lead to a broader range of effects, where the palatal surfaces of all the upper teeth can be affected. Over time, remnants of vomitus flowing under gravity to the lower dentition may affect other tooth surfaces, particularly the occusal surfaces of the lower teeth. In cases of rumination, there is often a more severe effect on upper and lower occlusal surfaces due to mastication of the acidic remnants.
Extrinsic acids, in the form of liquids, generally produce a different pattern. Liquids affect occlusal surfaces of the lower teeth more than upper occlusal surfaces due to gravity. This is a common ‘tell‐tale’ pattern that should direct the clinician to questioning the patient about acidic drinks. Acidic drinks may also affect the palatal aspects of the upper teeth, particularly the upper anteriors. This relates to how liquids are distributed around the mouth during swallowing. Unfortunately, this pattern can be misinterpreted, attributing it to intrinsic factors.

Prevention and monitoring

### Interactions between saliva, biofilms and acids

Under normal circumstances, mild non‐bacterial acids in the oral environment will be ‘processed’ no differently from the bacterial acids. Stimulated saliva and the protective nature of the biofilm will buffer and return the resting pH of the biofilm to normal levels. The whole process will occur within the closed system described in Chapter 1.
If the frequency of exposure of the non‐bacterial acids increases slightly, there will be a tendency for the resting pH of the biofilm to fall and, over time (especially if simple sugars are frequently included in the diet), the biodiversity of the biofilm can change towards an imbalance and cause net subsurface demineralization, that is, the initial caries lesion. This will be made worse if the quality and quantity of saliva are also compromised. The preventive approach is similar to that of caries and has been described in Chapter 2.
However, when the frequency of exposure to non‐bacterial acids, or the strength of those acids, is excessive, the biofilm is readily removed, exposing the tooth surface to acid attack. This is relatively common in many contemporary populations and one of the diagnostic features is a pristine mouth with no evidence of staining or plaque build‐up.
A pristine mouth may be misdiagnosed by a clinician simply because it indicates the presence of corrosion. Thus, management of patients may not be successful even though aggressive remineralization regimens may have been implemented. It must be remembered that many popular drinks now have a pH well below the critical pH of fluoroapatite (FA). They also have the ability to easily displace saliva (even stimulated saliva), allowing the acidic liquid to have direct contact with the tooth surface. The conditions at the tooth–acid interface are such that the acidic liquid is highly unsaturated with respect to FA. The result may be rapid surface demineralization where all the raw products from demineralization will be eliminated by swallowing.
That is an ‘open system’ where all the essential ions necessary

for remineralization are permanently lost. Such rapid surface demineralization results in the dished out lesions previously described. Surface loss from corrosion is therefore different from the relatively slower sub‐surface demineralization, the
‘white spot lesion’, associated with the pathogenesis of caries.
In comparison with the sub‐surface dissolution found in active caries, active corrosion affects the immediate tooth surface, causing large amounts of mineral loss from the tooth surface, exposing the damaged ends of the enamel rods at the surface. Unfortunately, irrespective of whether ‘good’ saliva floods the surface, or the surface is remineralized aggressively by various products, the next acid attack, where the pH of the acid may be well below the critical pH of FA, will continue the permanent demineralization. It is important to note that, in this recurring situation, remineralization is relatively ineffective. The best approach is to identify and eliminate the cause completely and this requires careful questioning of the patient to identify the cause of the acidic attack.

### Prevention

Every case needs to be individually assessed and the preventive strategy implemented accordingly. The dental literature lists numerous preventive products and techniques that will prevent erosion and protect the teeth from further attack [2]. These include alkaline mouthwashes, sugar‐free gum, the consumption of dairy products, fluoride mouthrinses, varnishes and gels, and CPP‐ACP (casein phosphopeptides and amorphous calcium phosphate). These approaches may be sound, sensible and effective in low‐risk individuals where the erosion is episodic and activity is very low. These patients may have plaque present and staining evident on some of the eroded surfaces.
From the explanation given in the previous section, it is possible to remineralize the damaged ends of the enamel rods after an acid attack. However, it is essential to prevent further dissolution in the very active erosive cases.
In very active cases, where the patient cannot make the necessary lifestyle changes (i.e. change their dietary habits), the application of an insoluble barrier on the tooth surface may prevent acids from contacting the tooth. For example, metal ion fluorides (e.g. SnF) can have the desired effect through the production of an insoluble precipitate and may offer some protection. Similarly, bulimic episodes will fall within this category because repeated vomiting may cause excessive damage. While the protective barrier philosophy may apply here, there is evidence that immediate rinsing with copious amounts of water, during and after the vomiting episode, has significant benefits. Anecdotally, protective glass ionomer cement barriers that cover the entire tooth surface, and pellicle ‘mimicking’ agents like CPP‐ACP creams, have shown some positive results. However, in the absence of research indicating their long‐term effects, regular applications may be indicated for these and similar agents in patients who are ‘at risk’.
It must be noted there are certain occupations associated with a risk for developing dental erosion, such as professional wine tasters. For these patients it may be important to take advantage

Restorative approach to corrosion (erosion)
Opinions vary as to the management approach to corrosive cases and clinical studies are lacking. As a basic principle, if there is active corrosion that remains uncontrolled, any form of treatment will fail. A simple approach using basic restorative techniques may still be advocated for aesthetic reasons, provided the patient is aware that on‐going reparative work will be required. Complex and expensive work should be avoided, at least until the problem is solved.
Where the preventive management is successful and the corrosion activity stops, the clinician must remember that ‘relapse’ is still a possibility and regular monitoring of the patient, over a reasonable period of time, is essential. Simple restorative work should take precedence, with future complex work, if required, being a longer‐term prospect.

### Abrasion

In comparison to the highly abrasive prehistoric diet, tooth abrasion from food is not a significant problem with current

populations. However, mastication on fewer teeth resulting from previous tooth extraction has the potential to increase the load, and therefore the wear, on the remaining teeth (Figures 4.2 and 4.3).
Asymmetric abrasive patterns may develop from occupational pursuits that promote the use of teeth as tools for grasping objects. Also, specific abrasive patterns may develop from the rigorous action of horizontal toothbrushing leading to wedge‐ shaped cervical lesions. However, it must be remembered that acid plays a role in the development of all these lesions.
Apart from food abrasion that is physiological, other forms of abrasive wear that are modern pathologies can easily be identified. The wedge‐shaped cervical lesion is easily identified and a cause can be associated with its presence. However, other abrasive irregularities, especially on incisor teeth, require questioning of the patient to identify the specific cause of the damage.

Restorative approach to abrasion
Excessive abrasion from food alone in patients with a full complement of teeth is not a common problem within our current populations. Generally there may be a combination of corrosion and attrition accentuating the problem. However, in a reduced dental arch, where a number of teeth have been extracted, the non‐carious tooth loss can be excessive. This often requires complex direct or indirect restorations, together with fixed or removable prosthodontics to restore edentulous areas, and to appropriately distribute the masticatory load across the whole arch. In all these complex cases, the careful selection of restorative materials is essential because of the abrasive incompatibility between some dental materials and tooth structure.
As explained in Chapter 1, toothbrush abrasion is a modern‐ day pathology where the defects can range from a very small cervical notch to a very large wedge‐shaped lesion. The management of these non‐carious cervical lesions will depend on the patients’ circumstances and will be discussed in Chapter 7.

### Attrition

Identification and evidence of active tooth grinding
Wear facets on the working surfaces of teeth offer the most objective evidence for identifying tooth grinding (see Chapter
1). However, a diagnosis of attrition may be difficult to make because of the other mechanisms that may be present concurrently, in particular, erosion patterns. There is large variability as to the extent of wear caused by attrition, however, the evidence is that it is a common behaviour because most people do grind with varying degrees of intensity.
Observation of the following signs and symptoms may also lead to a diagnosis of attrition and in particular give the clinician a feeling for the level of activity:
• Shiny facets – well‐defined and polished facets indicate active tooth grinding, but following corrosion by acid the facet will have a rather dull surface.
• Enamel flaking – active grinding can cause enamel flaking on the incisal edges of teeth but staining associated with these surfaces can imply past activity.
• Myofascial pain dysfunction (MPD) – the presence of MPD syndrome may indicate active tooth grinding with associated pain and tenderness in the temporomandibular joints. Attrition facets are generally in evidence.
• Stiff jaw – an acute episode of stiffness in the muscles of mastication may result from traumatic injury or infection, but chronic stiffness may indicate active tooth grinding, particularly if it is apparent upon waking after a night’s sleep.
• Night‐guards can be used to determine tooth grinding. Polish the occlusal surface to a matte finish only and subsequent tooth grinding activity will show up as shiny facets on the acrylic surface.
• The use of the scratch test, described above, by placing a scratch on a facet and observing over time, can be valuable.
If pathological processes, such as corrosion and the non‐carious cervical lesion, are not evident, and there is evidence of tooth reduction caused by attrition and associated abrasion, the clinician should reflect on whether the observed wear is physiologic or pathologic before any restorative intervention is considered.
Wear in a young individual with a full complement of teeth would be considered as pathologic if it is evident that the dentition will not last into old age. The same amount of wear in an older person with no apparent symptoms could easily be interpreted as physiologic. In such extreme cases the decision for treatment may be apparent, however, the decision will often be subjective and will rely on operator experience.

Although there is no correlation between tooth grinding and occlusion, it must be stressed that the occlusal geometry will have a bearing on whether or not some symptoms develop due to an increased spate of tooth grinding activity. That is to say, the occlusion cannot be entirely discounted and should be observed and understood in the context of treatment planning.
It is not likely that the occlusion can initiate tooth grinding, but it is likely to provide an environment where the direction and intensity of grinding forces may affect teeth, muscles or joints.
Current opinion suggests that there is no association between malocclusions and cranio‐mandibular disorders. The development of malocclusions during growth and development will generally be slow enough for the stomatognathic system to learn through proprioception and develop a functional pattern to avoid occlusal interferences. It can be assumed that the mandibular movements will become part of the ‘functional envelope’ of mandibular motion for the individual. However, there is a potential for problems to develop as grinding levels increase or if a functional pattern undergoes an ‘acute’ change without the body having time to adapt. Such ‘acute’ changes to the occlusion may well be iatrogenic in origin caused by general operative dentistry. Although occlusal equilibrations are not normally advocated, occlusal adjustments to correct specific iatrogenically induced problems do have their place in dentistry.
Long‐standing, active tooth grinding without symptoms may only be a concern if excessive wear occurs. The wear may be generally distributed across the dental arches where group function is established and it may also include enamel flaking. Furthermore, there are situations where heavily restored but weakened teeth may show complete cusp fracture under the load. In all these combinations, the best prophylactic approach to reduce the wear is the construction of a night guard. This does not stop the grinding (although it may be reduced initially) but it does conserve tooth structure at the expense of the acrylic of the night guard.
Finally, current research into reducing attrition has focused on the use of natural and artificial lubricants between the teeth that can be applied at night when the grinding occurs. Although this does not stop the grinding behaviour, it has shown very positive results in reducing wear. Although a number of natural olive oil products show some benefits, tooth mousse has shown the

best results, having the equivalent of the lubricating capabilities of saliva. It also seems to have significant benefits where erosion and attrition occur together causing ‘acid‐accelerated wear’ [3].

Restorative approach to attrition
Wear patterns from tooth grinding can vary depending on the type of occlusion, the most predictable being Angle’s Class 11
Division 2 deep bite configurations. Almost invariably, individuals with this geometry, and with a full complement of teeth, will show severely worn anterior teeth while the posterior teeth remain pristine. Unfortunately these cases are very difficult to restore because of a lack of anterior space. Any attempt to increase the occlusal vertical dimension to build up the anterior teeth requires the inclusion of complex posterior intervention as well, irrespective whether the posterior teeth require treatment or not. In such cases, orthodontic tooth movement, in conjunction with complex restorative intervention, may be required.
These occlusions are easily identified in children and should be corrected orthodontically well before these uneven wear patterns are established.
Often the driving force for treatment is the issue of aesthetics becoming a concern for the patient. Although this is a real issue for patients, it must be remembered that patient communication is important. The patient must be made aware of the balance between attaining an appearance that is ‘glossy magazine’ and ‘Hollywood driven’ and the unnecessary destruction of tooth structure.
A worn dentition with scooped dentine is functional. This differential wear is essential for scissorial function and the perception that this should be restored for the sake of function stems from the preconceived dental idea that the anatomy of the newly emerged tooth is the most ideal functional form.
As explained in Chapter 1, the change from canine protected occlusion to group function is a natural transition where functionality is maintained. The concept of canine protected occlusion as the long‐term ideal is not necessarily valid, but is more probably only one stage within a continuum throughout life.
In severe cases of tooth tissue loss where full‐mouth rehabilitation is required, the use of a prophylactic night guard at the completion of the treatment is warranted if tooth grinding was the predominant factor causing the wear. Overall, the type of decisions made in extreme wear cases would relate to the correct selection of restorative materials, whether direct or indirect, that would withstand functional load.
By recognizing progressive change in tooth form as a physiologically dynamic process, and by understanding the factors outlined above, premature and unnecessary dental interventions can be avoided.

### External Tooth Resorptions

External tooth resorptions that can result in significant tooth structure loss in permanent teeth and that are not of pulpal origin have been classified as follows [4]:
1	 trauma‐induced (non‐infective) resorptions;
2	 infection‐induced resorptions;
3	 hyperplastic invasive resorptions.
Some rare external root resorptions which do not fall into these categories are given the label of ‘idiopathic tooth resorptions’ because they arise from an unknown cause.
An understanding of the aetiology, predisposing factors and tissue responses involved in each category of tooth resorption is essential if a rational approach to management is to be achieved.
The active cell in resorption of tooth or bone can be termed a
‘clastic cell’ rather than being given a specific cell label such as osteoclast, cementoclast, dentinoclast, or ameloclast. These are motile blood‐borne cells, derived from haemopoetic stem cell precursors. They migrate to resorption sites as mononuclear clastic cells where they often fuse with similar cells to form mature multinucleated resorbing cells. Figure 4.5 shows a diagrammatic illustration of a mature clastic cell attached to a tooth surface by a circular organelle‐free ‘clear zone’. This surrounds a central reactive ‘ruffled border’ where the mineral and organic components of tooth or bone are degraded and removed.

Trauma‐induced (non‐infective) resorptions
In this category, using a broader interpretation of the term
‘trauma’, resorptions may have resulted from pressure from unerupted or erupting teeth or some neoplasms; from biomechanical forces involved in orthodontics; from mechanical trauma (luxation and avulsion injuries); and from surgical, thermal or chemical trauma. In all trauma‐induced (non‐infective) tooth resorption, some damage to the cementum/cementoid‐ periodontal membrane complex has occurred which stimulates

clastic activity. Trauma‐induced external tooth resorption may be subdivided into (i) surface resorption; (ii) pressure resorption;
(iii) orthodontic resorption; and (iv) replacement resorption.

### Surface resorption

From a treatment management point of view, the simplest form of trauma‐induced (non‐infective) resorption is surface resorption. This, as the name suggests, is a shallow resorption of cementum often with involvement of a small amount of underlying dentine. Surface resorption may be difficult to observe radiographically as there may be no or only slight changes in the root morphology and the image of the periodontal membrane and associated lamina dura remains intact with no signs of any associated root or boney radiolucency. This type of resorption is self‐limiting and transient and can follow some traumatic injuries or orthodontic treatment. In the absence of superimposed infection, usually from inside the root canal, surface resorption will heal uneventfully with reparative cementum as shown histologically in Figure 4.6.

### Pressure resorption and orthodontic resorption

### Replacement resorption

The most serious form of trauma‐induced (non‐infective) root resorption is replacement resorption that, as the name suggests, involves the progressive replacement of tooth structure

by alveolar bone and ultimately tooth loss. Replacement resorption follows the death of viable periodontal ligament cells due to factors such as compression or drying, as in the case of a delayed replantation of an avulsed tooth. On rare occasions an intact cementum/cementoid layer may act as a biological barrier, so that ankylosis, union with bone, is not accompanied by replacement resorption. However, the usual response is that of ankylosis with replacement resorption due to the development, subsequent to surface resorption, of an interface between bone and dentine with remodelling processes occurring as part of normal skeletal bone turn‐over, but at the expense of dentine.
There is a total loss of mobility due to this union of tooth and bone. The tooth gives a characteristically high percussion sound but otherwise patients with replacement resorption are symptom‐free. Radiographically there will be a loss of the image of the periodontal ligament followed by evidence of the progressive replacement of tooth structure by bone and, in time, the image of the tooth root is lost. An example of a radiograph of an ankylosed tooth that is undergoing replacement resorption is shown in Figure 4.9.
At present, there is no treatment possible for this type of resorption and so the clinical management, from the initiation of replacement resorption to the inevitable demise of the tooth, poses important challenges, particularly in the developing dentition. Treatment options in the developing dentition include surgical repositioning, root submergence or tooth extraction and transplantation of premolar tooth. If the tooth is in a satisfactory position in a mature dentition, there is no urgency for tooth replacement as often the replacement resorption proceeds at a slow rate, in some instances taking many years to reach a stage where carefully planned intervention is necessary. ### External inflammatory root resorption

Classically this type of external root resorption occurs when infection is superimposed on a traumatic injury, usually following replantation of an avulsed tooth or a luxation injury but may also occur in a tooth with endodontic pathosis. Despite the degree of external inflammatory resorption, endodontic treatment can prove successful. A prerequisite for external inflammatory root resorption is damage to the normally protective cementum/ cementoid which then initiates surface resorption exposing the underlying dentine to the passage of bacteria or their metabolites from the root canal to the external root surface. A normal inflammatory response ensues, including the activation of clastic cells resulting in resorption of both tooth and bone. As the inflammatory response is chronic in nature, it is generally asymptomatic unless the infection becomes acute in which case the tooth will become tender to touch and there may be the development of an overlying swelling. Radiographically external inflammatory root resorption can be recognized by bowl‐like radiolucencies in both the tooth root and the adjacent bone and may be evident quite early after injury as shown in Figure 4.10. A more advanced example is shown in Figure 4.11, while the radiographic appearance of inflammatory root resorption resulting from endodontic pathogens is shown in Figure 4.12.

External inflammatory root resorption is progressive and will ultimately result in tooth loss unless endodontic treatment is instituted. As with other forms of infection‐induced resorption, treatment involves the thorough debridement and preparation of the root canal system, followed by the application of anticlastic intra‐canal medication.
Irrigation is a most important component of this debridement process and the sequential use of 17% EDTAC, 1% sodium hypochlorite and a final rinse with EDTAC solution has been shown to be a most effective regimen, resulting in a dentine surface devoid of smear layer. Such dentine preparation facilitates the diffusion through dentine to the external root surface of intra‐canal medications, such as cortico‐steroid/antibiotic preparations which can exert a positive effect on the clastic cells responsible for the resorptive processes [5]. ### Prevention of inflammatory root resorption

A tooth with complete root development which has been subjected to avulsion, intrusion or a severe luxation injury should, after replantation or repositioning, have the pulp removed as soon as possible and the canal dressed with a cortico‐steroid antibiotic paste so that its anticlastic effect can be exerted in the early phases of the healing process. If calcium hydroxide is to be used as the initial dressing material, treatment should be delayed for 2 weeks to avoid cellular necrosis and ankylosis. In a tooth with an immature apex, with a diameter greater than 1 mm, there is a chance of revascularization in all the above injuries, provided in the case of a replanted tooth that the extra‐oral period is relatively short (30 mins) or the tooth has been stored in a medium which maintains the viability of the periodontal ligament. Teeth with wide apices subjected to such trauma should be monitored carefully at monthly intervals for 3 months and at longer intervals thereafter. If radiographic signs of inflammatory root resorption become evident, early endodontic intervention is required.

### Hyperplastic invasive resorptions

Dental resorptions in this hyperplastic group are insidious in nature and generally present complex therapeutic challenges because resorbing tissue invades the hard tissues of the tooth in a destructive, and apparently uncontrolled fashion, akin to the nature of some fibro‐osseous lesions such as fibrous dysplasia. These external hyperplastic invasive resorptions have a periodontal origin in contrast to the rare hyperplastic invasive internal resorptions outlined in Chapter 12 that have a pulpal origin.
Unlike the other types of external resorption where the elimination of the cause of the lesion, for example, micro‐organisms, can arrest progression of the resorptive process, in cases of hyperplastic invasive resorptions, total removal and inactivation of the resorptive tissue are essential if recurrence (or concurrence) is to be avoided. Concurrence indicates the incomplete removal of the resorptive tissue at the time of treatment and recurrence is the re‐establishment of the resorptive process.
The reason for recurrence or concurrence is probably the invasive nature of the resorptive tissue, whereby small infiltrative channels are created within the dentine. These may interconnect with the periodontal ligament in positions more apical to the main resorptive defect. Unless the tissue in these infiltrative channels is inactivated, the resorptive process will continue.
While these resorptions are not considered to be neoplastic, their aggressive characteristics are similar.
At the present time, one proven therapy for inactivation is chemical in nature, but surgical and other technological modalities may also be applied. Critical to the clinical use of therapies aimed at controlling hyperplastic invasive tooth

resorptions is a correct diagnosis of the type and localization of the resorption.
Hyperplastic resorptions may be subdivided into: (i) invasive coronal resorption; (ii) invasive cervical resorption; and (iii) invasive radicular resorption.

### Invasive coronal resorption

This rare condition generally develops in erupting teeth where a localized coronal enamel defect allows the invasion of aggressive hyperplastic resorptive tissue, as illustrated in Figure 4.13.
In this case, a pink resorptive defect can be observed in an area of hypomineralization on the labial surface of the crown of the erupting central incisor tooth. The dental pulp remains protected by an intact layer of dentin until late in the process. Invasive coronal resorption has also been observed in teeth which have been injured by the intrusion of a primary tooth.
Radiographically the image of the resorptive defect is generally irregular in outline and depending on its extent the radiolucency may extend both coronally and into the radicular tooth structure as illustrated in Figure 4.14.

Treatment is directed towards the total removal or inactivation of all resorptive tissue and the restoration of the coronal defect.

### Invasive cervical resorption

Characterized by its cervical location and invasive nature, this insidious resorptive process may occur in any tooth in the permanent dentition. The invading tissue is initially fibrovascular in nature and contains clastic cells responsible for the progressive resorption of dentin, enamel and cementum. The dental pulp remains protected by an intact layer of dentin until late in the process. Ectopic calcifications can be observed in more advanced lesions when the resorbing tissue becomes fibro‐osseous in character and the dentin at the interface shows a semblance to replacement resorption‐ the essential difference being the presence of infiltrative channels which may burrow deeply into radicular dentin and also inter‐connect with the periodontal ligament apical to the original site of resorption. The complexity of this resorptive network makes treatment a challenge as inactivation of all the resorbing tissue is critical to successful management.
Invasive cervical resorption is usually painless unless there is superimposed secondary infection when pulpal or periodontal symptoms may arise. Resorption of coronal dentin and enamel often creates a clinically obvious pinkish colour in the tooth crown as highly vascular resorptive tissue becomes visible through thin residual enamel. Cavitation of the enamel will later occur. Invasive cervical resorption has, and continues to be, misdiagnosed as a form of internal resorption, a misunderstanding that could possibly be attributed to the early descriptions of internal resorption by Gaskill in 1894 and by Mummery in 1920, which included all teeth showing ‘pink spots’. An example of invasive cervical resorption in a maxillary central incisor is shown in Figure 4.15 and the relevant radiograph is shown in Figure 4.16.

In other instances there may be no obvious outward sign of this process and its detection may be by routine radiographs and examples are shown in Figures 4.17 and 4.18.

### Class 3 invasive cervical resorptions

When invasive cervical resorptions are diagnosed at the Class 3 stage of development, treatment poses far greater challenges due to the infiltrative and fibro‐osseous characteristics of the resorptive lesion. Ectopic bone‐like deposits can be observed both within the lesion and at the interface with resorbed dentine. In addition, resorptive tissue creates a series of channels that encircle the root canal and infiltrate into radicular dentine. A further complicating feature is the interconnection of these infiltrating channels with the periodontal ligament at other locations on the root surface.

Recommended Management

In the absence of treatment, invasive cervical resorption leads to progressive and usually destructive replacement of tooth structure. The management of invasive cervical resorption varies with the degree of progression of this complex resorptive process but the basic aim in Classes 1–3 resorptions is the total removal or inactivation of resorptive tissue and the restoration of the resorptive defect.

### Class 1 or Class 2 invasive cervical resorptions

The resorptive tissue in these classes is fibro‐vascular in character and the pulp is walled off by a protective pre‐dentine and dentine barrier that, in proximal lesions, can be identified radiographically by a radio‐opaque line. Traditional methods of treatment consist of curetting the active tissue from the resorption cavity and restoring the defect. An alternative method, which utilizes the topical application of 90% aqueous trichloracetic acid, curettage and restoration, has been clinically assessed and the success rates reported. One advantage of the latter approach is haemorrhage control which can prove a problem with conventional treatment. As the effect of trichloroacetic acid is to cause coagulation necrosis, the resorptive tissue is rendered avascular. In addition, as there is a tendency for recurrence of this type of resorption using earlier methods, the inactivation of adjacent and potentially resorptive cells by trichloroacetic acid is a further reason for its utilization.

### Clinical management

Treatment of the coronal extent of the resorption follows the principles laid down for Class 1 and 2 lesions. However, pulpectomy and root canal widening with Gates Glidden burs are also essential to allow access to the encircling resorptive tissue in the deeper extents of the affected tooth root. Careful inspection of the treated root and adjacent soft tissues with enhanced vision is necessary using an operating microscope or optical loupes of at least 5.5 magnification with a headlight. When satisfied with the preparation of the root canal system, a root canal dressing, either in the form of a corticosteroid‐antibiotic preparation or calcium hydroxide can be placed and temporarily sealed. When re‐accessed several days later, the dressing material is removed by irrigation and sonication and the inactivation of the resorptive defect checked. Recommended Periodontal Surgical Management

Varying degrees of invasive cervical resorption have also been managed by periodontal flap reflection, curettage, restoration of the defect with glass‐ionomer cement or composite resin and repositioning the flap to its original position. Application of trichloracetic acid to the base of the resorption cavity to inactivate any infiltrative tissue has also been used to supplement this periodontal approach.
Class 4 resorptions pose far greater difficulties in treatment using this protocol and the poor success rates that have been reported strongly suggest that alternative prosthodontic replacement is generally preferred, particularly if there coronal discoloration or enamel cavitation. If there is no coronal pink discoloration or enamel cavitation, another option in selected cases of
Class 4 invasive cervical resorption is to leave the affected tooth untreated. Clinical observations suggest the progress of the resorptive process, particularly in older patients, is slow.
