he stage may be reached in the progression of dental caries where preventive therapy and remineralization techniques will no longer be successful.
In the presence of frank cavitation in both enamel and dentine, it will no longer be possible to prevent plaque accumulation. It then becomes essential to surgically debride the lesion and restore the tooth to original anatomy to allow proper cleaning and prevent further plaque accumulation. It is also sometimes necessary to shape and restore teeth damaged by trauma and to remove defective restorations and replace them. Each of these actions requires cutting either the tooth tissues or hard restorative materials.
Until the end of the 1800s, hand instruments were the preferred method of cavity preparation and even after the introduction of rotary instruments, they were essential to the final refinement of a cavity designed in the G.V. Black tradition. Rotating cutting instruments travelling at increasing speeds then evolved and still remain the most effective means of reducing both tooth tissue and old restorative materials. However, recent advances with both air abrasion and laser techniques have added additional instruments to the armamentarium. Furthermore, chemo‐mechanical techniques for the removal of carious dentine are becoming more sophisticated and are contributing to a reduction in patient discomfort during the surgical phase of operative dentistry. All of these techniques deserve further research and investigation particularly in the light of the trend to minimal cavity designs.

### Rotary Cutting Instruments

Classification of burs
There is a vast array of sizes and shapes of rotary cutting instruments available and, in an attempt to rationalize the selection, the International Standards Organization has developed a classification (ISO 6360). The essential dimensions of a bur, including the surface material on the bur head, shank type, overall length, shape and type of finish of the working head and the size of the head, are all numerically ordered (Figure 8.1).

‘Annoyance Factor’
The term ‘annoyance factor’ is used rather loosely to describe the patient’s subjective reaction to cavity preparation and is a combination of the pressure applied to the tooth; the vibrations and noise perceived through the bones of the skull; the heat and smell generated at the interface between the tooth and the bur;
and the time taken to perform a given task (Figure 8.2).

Vibration and noise generation are related closely to the coarseness of the cutting instrument and the speed of rotation.
Within limits, the higher the speed and the smaller the instrument. the lower the vibration, but concentricity is also a factor and requires maintenance of the handpiece, the bur and the equipment in general. The generation of both heat and smell is closely related to the adequacy of lubrication and the efficiency of the instrument in the removal of the shavings being taken from the tooth surface. All of these factors can be combined to give a rough idea of the problems faced by the patient when a tooth needs to be surgically prepared.

Selection of burs
In general, rotary cutting instruments will remove tooth structure either by chipping it away or else by grinding [1, 2]. There are essentially three types of bur designed for cavity preparation although there are many variations for polishing, contouring, etc. This discussion will be confined to those related to preparation of caries lesions only.

### Steel burs

These were originally used when rotary cutting instruments were developed well over one hundred years ago [3]. Initially only foot drills were available with speeds of about 50–500 rpm.
Electric motors followed, allowing speeds up to 5000 rpm and they remain valuable for the removal of caries and the development of retentive elements in dentine. Each bur generally has eight blades and some of them have a positive rake angle to facilitate the cutting of the dentine or the removal of caries (Figure 8.3). This, however, makes them relatively fragile and subject to chipping along the leading edge and they should not be expected to have a long life in normal practice. In fact, single use is recommended.

The use of an air/water spray during cutting with steel burs will increase efficiency but it is not essential. Generally the shanks are designed for latch‐type handpieces but some manufacturers now make friction grip handpiece heads to hold these burs and these help to maintain concentricity and thereby reduce vibration. Any bur rotating at low speeds will deliver a rather high annoyance factor to the patient and a good tactile sense is essential for the operator to avoid over‐cutting. Burs must always be in good condition and visibility must be excellent at all times.

### Tungsten carbide burs

Following the development of higher speed motors and handpieces there was a need to provide stronger burs to withstand the heavier stresses involved and to lengthen their useful life.
Tungsten carbide burs were designed almost exclusively for friction grip handpieces because concentricity is essential and they only cut efficiently at greatly increased speeds (Figure 8.4). In fact they do not begin to reach effective cutting capacity until
100,000 rpm, and are best used at speeds beyond 300,000rpm.
The efficiency of these instruments for removing particles of enamel or dentine depends on the number of blades, the rake angle of the blades and other variations in the design. Blades which twist around the shank of the bur will remove debris more readily and blades which are cross‐cut are more efficient still. Burs with eight blades or fewer are designed for rough or gross cutting and the greater the number of blades, the finer the surface and the smoother the cut. Tungsten carbide burs with 30 blades or more are used for polishing.

The usual bur has six blades and a negative rake angle to provide better support for the cutting edge. For the same reason many have a radial clearance as well. They cut metal and dentine well but are prone to produce microcracks in enamel, thus weakening the cavosurface margin.
It is essential that tungsten carbide burs be used at speeds above 100,000 rpm. Air/water spray is mandatory for the removal of debris and temperature control and they must be mounted in a friction grip cartridge for concentricity. Probably only a new bur will be truly concentric because any loss of a blade, or even a section of a blade, will alter the balance so that only every third or fourth blade will actually contact the tooth. This means that the clinical life is generally quite short. The annoyance factor with a new bur at high linear surface speed is very low but a lack of concentricity will be immediately discernible to the patient.

### Diamond burs or stones

Diamond burs will grind away tooth structure and they are available in a range of particle size from about 150 micron (μ) down to
5μ. Diamonds with a particle size of 150μ are extremely coarse with a high annoyance factor so the regular 80μ particles are the usual selection for basic cavity preparation. Finer diamonds with particle sizes in the 25μ range are recommended for finishing all margins where adhesive restorative materials are to be placed, while polishing procedures can be carried out with particles down to 5μ. In recent times there have been considerable improvements in the methods of embedding the diamond particles in the metal of the bur head so they last longer and there is a far better distribution of particle size.
The particles are attached to the shank of the bur through either a galvanic metal bond or a sintering process and the quality and efficiency of the bur are dependent upon the efficiency of attachment of

the particles to the bur head as well as the clearance of the shavings.

With the advent of increased speeds, these shapes have been retained but modified to a considerable extent. The original subdivision is still valid and burs and their application can still be discussed under those headings, though slightly modified. One complicating factor has been the plethora of rather bizarre bur shapes produced by manufacturers at the whim of individual operators who develop an unusual shape to fulfil one task only in tooth reduction. The result can be a vast array of burs with limited functions which tend to clutter up bur storage and serve a very limited purpose. It is recommended that the bur shapes and sizes be strictly limited and the various shapes be used to their full extent.

Elimination of inverted cone
Simplification of bur selection is achieved by eliminating the inverted cone. The original concept behind this shape was that it should undercut sound enamel and then remove it by being withdrawn vertically through the enamel. This is no longer necessary for cavity preparation and the sharp acute angle which will be produced between the floor and the walls is difficult to restore properly with plastic restorative materials because they do not flow readily into such acute angles. Retentive features can be better developed with the other shapes.

In general, the higher the speed, the smoother the operation, in as much as providing the bur is running concentrically, vibration will be reduced as the speed increases. However, there is a limit because centrifugal forces take precedence at ultra‐high speeds. For example, a bur with a diameter of 2.0 mm should not exceed 300,000 rpm or it may bend and break. Also, it is difficult to maintain concentricity in a rotating bur, but at higher speeds this problem is somewhat overcome. The fact that only every third or fourth blade is actually working is less significant at ultra‐high speeds and vibration is reduced.

Application of load
Because of torque limitations with air turbines, as the speed increases, the load that can be applied without the bur stalling decreases. This means that the tactile sense of the operator also decreases. It is therefore possible to remove too much tooth structure with ultra‐high speed burs if visibility is less than excellent. With electric micro‐motors, torque can remain reasonably high through an electrical feed‐back circuit and tactile sense remains high.
Load before stalling will also be dependent upon the linear surface speed of the bur, the presence or absence of an adequate lubricant and the sharpness of the bur. There is a temptation to continue the life of a bur beyond its useful time, simply by applying greater load, but in so doing the temperature at the work face will be increased, there will be greater wear and tear on the handpiece bearings and the concentricity of the handpiece will degenerate more rapidly.

Cutting efficiency
Tooth structure can be removed at different rates depending upon the intended outcome. If it is necessary to remove a large quantity in a short time, then, within the constraints of the annoyance factor, a large diameter coarse cutting diamond or tungsten carbide bur with good clearance, rotating at ultra‐high speed, with copious water spray, will accomplish this. On the other hand, precise cavity outline form requires greater tactile sense leading to conservation of remaining tooth structure and better adaptation of adhesive restorative materials. This can be achieved with slower speeds and smaller burs, preferably finer diamonds.

### End cutting/side cutting

It is also important to consider whether a bur will cut efficiently along the side or the end or both. Entry through intact enamel requires an end cutting bur, development of cavity outline requires side cutters. It is possible to combine the two functions in one bur. A diamond cylinder with a flat end and a slightly rounded corner will achieve both aims rather efficiently. It will penetrate sound enamel by engaging the surface of the tooth with the bur at a slight angle and then it will extend a trench while held at right angles to the surface. A round bur will also penetrate the surface by using the side only rather than the base.
However, the bur head then disappears into the cavity and is lost to sight. A logical choice of bur should be made according to the task in hand and the number of burs in a kit can then be kept to a minimum.

### Air Abrasion Techniques

Description
While rotary instruments are still used for the majority of restorative procedures, in the past decade there has been a dramatic expansion in the utilization of methods which can augment or indeed replace rotary instruments for caries removal and cavity preparation. Alternative methods of air abrasion, pulsed erbium lasers and chemo‐mechanical caries removal provide the clinician with the ability to work more conservatively and in different quadrants at the same appointment, with a reduced need for local anaesthesia. Overall, this facilitates a minimalist philosophy and reduces stress for both patient and operator [8].

Air abrasion, also called kinetic cavity preparation, particle beam or kinetic abrasion technology, uses the kinetic energy of microfine (20–50 micron diameter) particles of alumina (aluminium oxide, Al2O3) in a high pressure air stream to remove tooth structure by brittle micro‐fracture [9, 10]. The failure of brittle materials during air abrasion occurs by a process of crack creation, extension, and erosion. When the abrasive particles impact such a surface, the depression grows, and radial and lateral cracks are generated in the area. The cracks ultimately join together to isolate and remove a piece of the material. The alumina particles are delivered intraorally using a handpiece with contra‐angle or sickle configuration, fitted with a nozzle through which the particle beam is directed on to the tooth [11].
Alternative powders that can be used for air abrasion include sodium bicarbonate, urea, and dolomite [8].

History
The technique of air abrasion was invented in 1943 by Robert
Black (a cousin of G.V. Black), but was not pursued with vigour as a technology for cavity preparation because of the advent of the air turbine. It re‐emerged in the 1990s, largely due to the work of J. Tim Rainey, a dentist in Texas who was a protégé of
Robert Black. The technique attracted interest because it simplifies the preparation of very conservative cavities and reduces the need for local anaesthesia in many patients [12].
It should be regarded as an adjunct to, but not a universal replacement for, rotary instruments. The main indications include the preparation of minimal access through enamel for occlusal lesions on posterior teeth as well as limited debridement of dentine lesions without local anaesthesia. However, it must be noted that areas of carious infected dentine are not hard and brittle so have a limited ability to undergo brittle fracture.
Therefore, these areas cannot be cut efficiently with air abrasion and alternate techniques are recommended. The traditional

hand excavators are still of value but are not particularly accurate. Slowly rotating round burs are the usual method but now there is a chemo‐mechanical technique which has a number of advantages for removal of grossly softened tooth structure.
Air abrasion units are also useful for micro‐etching of metallic and ceramic crowns prior to cementation or repair, and also for removal of extrinsic stains from the tooth surface using low velocity and softer abrasives to avoid surface damage (Figure 8.14).

Available equipment
There are several suppliers of air abrasion technology and a range of equipment is available. Quite complex units with advanced features, such as supersonic handpieces with increased particle velocity and thus cutting speed, are available. Pulsing of the particle stream, through the cyclical operation of valves, is possible and air desiccation and particle agitation can be incorporated to prevent clumping and blockages of the powder. Micro‐processor control of air pressure and the particle parameters can be varied.
With more complex equipment, a remote control unit, small enough to fit into the palm of a hand, provides control of the basic parameters of the instrument, allowing fingertip control without having to move away from the patient. It is also possible to integrate the foot control with that of the dental unit so one foot control can be universal.

Clinical application
There is a distinct learning curve required for the use of air abrasion, since the cutting action created by the particle beam occurs ahead of the handpiece and there will be no tactile feedback at all. Typically, a rotary cutting instrument is side cutting and there is tactile sense at all times. In the absence of this ‘feel’, there is a tendency for over‐cutting of sound tooth structure which can only be reduced through experience and attention to detail.

The energy delivered by the abrasive particles is directly related to the airflow and the particle size [13]. Doubling the particle size from 27 to 50 microns multiplies the mass, and thus the kinetic energy, by a factor of eight (Table 8.2). While this will accelerate tooth removal, it will lead to more discomfort for the

patient, particularly during the cutting of dentine, although the relationship between energy and discomfort is not linear. In addition, there may be an increase in dehydration which is often interpreted as a cold sensation (Figure 8.15). There is, therefore, an inherent trade‐off between increasing cutting power (energy) and the level of patient comfort, i.e. the annoyance factor.

Factors affecting the clinical efficiency of air abrasion, that is, removal of tooth structure, include:
• nature of the tooth surface – carious enamel cuts preferentially to sound enamel but sound dentine is cut more readily than carious dentine
• moisture level – dry dentine cuts more readily than wet dentine
• nozzle size (fine, ultrafine)
• air pressure (typically 45–80 psi, but can be as high as 120 psi)
• particle flow rate (usually 2 grams/minute but may be up to
8 grams/minute)
• particle size and velocity (e.g. 27, 34, 50 or 100μ range available)
• nozzle‐target distance (typically 0.8–1.0 mm minimum)
• nozzle‐target angle (45, 66, 90 degrees)
• speed of movement of the handpiece over the tooth surface
• pulse frequency (mark‐space interval or dwell) – only for pulsed instruments.
Air abrasion has a variety of applications including entry to a caries lesion, removal of carious enamel and dentine, stains, calculus, organic plugs, composite resin restorations, and fissure sealants [14]. The cutting action comes from firing alumina particles against the target surface allowing an end cutting action only.

Occupational hazards
Alumina, the most common powder material used in air abrasion, is an insoluble white, odourless, non‐flammable inert powder. Grey alumina is composed of flakes of alumina, while white alumina contains globules of alumina mixed with titanium dioxide.
The use of alumina raises three occupational health and safety issues:
• It must not be used in a dental practice where operatory staff suffer from asthma, reactive airways disease, or other respiratory diseases, because of the risk of exacerbation from fine alumina dust, classified as a ‘nuisance dust’.
• In the absence of a rubber dam, the patient must tolerate alumina powder build‐up in the mouth. Wet gauze squares placed on the buccal mucosa and tongue can help contain these residues. However, alumina is not a chemical irritant and is not absorbed through mucous membranes or the skin.
• Dust problems in the treatment area tend to increase as the abrasion dust adds to other environmental sources of dust.
Both conventional intra‐oral high velocity evacuation and additional extra‐oral high performance vacuum systems are required. The extra‐oral system removes the small amount of dust, approximately 10%, which escapes the intra‐oral evacuation system, and also provides filtration of the entire operatory air. In terms of personal protective equipment, a mask is considered adequate protection but both staff and patient must wear eye protection because alumina particles may cause mechanical injury to eyes.

Modus operandi of lasers
At this point the most common laser systems used for cavity preparation are the erbium lasers, which rely on the intense absorption bands within tooth structure in the middle and far infrared regions. Tooth enamel is a mixture of apatites, includ-

ing hydroxyapatite (HAP; Ca10‐(PO4)6‐(OH)2), fluorapatite (Ca10‐(PO4)6‐F2) and carbonated apatite (Ca10‐(PO4)6‐CO3).
The major absorbing components in enamel are hydroxyl (2800 nm), water (2940 nm), carbonate (7000 nm), and phosphate (9000–11000 nm). Regardless of the balance of these components, water is the major absorbing agent from the standpoint of ablation of both sound and carious tooth structure [26].
The major absorption areas for water are within the middle part of the infrared spectrums, specifically in the region near
3000 nm wavelength. A wavelength of 2940 nm will match exactly the maximal absorption of water, and thus provide effective ablation of tooth structure and this corresponds with that of the Erbium:YAG laser. Lasers which operate at more or less than 2940 nm absorb less well in water and thus tend to show less rapid ablation of tooth structure when measured on a weight or volumetric basis. The same is true for ablation of bone.
Tooth structure is naturally 12% by weight of water, and one third of this is bound into the chemical structure of the tooth as a monolayer of molecules surrounding each crystal of apatite mineral, within both enamel and dentine (see Chapter 1).
The remaining two‐thirds are found in pores in the enamel and dentine and this can be removed by dehydration. A laser, which can transfer the water into steam by rapid heating, can therefore explode tooth structure and eject the crystallites. The evaporation of the water creates a cooling effect and this mitigates against the inherently thermal nature of the process. In fact, erbium‐based laser systems can achieve effective ablation at temperatures well below the melting and vapourization temperature of enamel [27, 28].

Sound effects
A variety of photo‐acoustic effects occur during cavity preparation caused by the volume expansion of rapidly heated water and these have clinical relevance. The pressure increase is influenced by several factors including the speed of sound in the tissue, tissue density, and pulse duration. Because of ejection of material from the tooth and ablative recoil, there is little amplitude (loudness) until the ablation threshold is reached. Above the ablation threshold, the loudness increases as active ejection occurs. For this reason, erbium‐ based laser systems produce a characteristic popping sound when the laser is operating on dental hard tissues above the ablation threshold. Both the pitch and resonance of this sound relate to the propagation of an acoustic shock wave within the tooth, and vary according to the presence or absence of caries. These sounds assist the user in determining when caries removal is complete.

Analgesic effect
Generally, patients undergoing cavity preparation with erbium lasers do not need local anaesthesia. Large‐scale clinical trials report that only 2–5% of patients will request local anaesthesia although many patients experience slight, intermittent sensations of cold in their teeth, probably caused by the cooling effects of water evaporation during laser pulses. Some report subtle ‘earthquake’ sensations within the pulp which, while unusual, are not perceived as unpleasant. Many patients have no sensation whatsoever which suggests that the annoyance factor is very low. It also raises the intriguing possibility that there is an analgesic effect created by the laser.

Clinical application
To deliver pulsed erbium laser energy to the tooth, a variety of handpieces have been designed including straight, contra‐angle and curved configurations to suit a variety of clinical situations.
Most are non‐contact handpieces and use a sapphire window to deliver the energy on to the tooth surface. The window also transmits a visible red light beam to allow the clinician to target the area of interest. An alternative is to use a short curved fibre or a straight sapphire tip which can improve access to deep proximal preparations and tunnel preparations. However, the use of longer fibres and tips of this type requires modification in the settings, since they show greater energy loss than the relatively thin sapphire window on a noncontact handpiece.

### Conventional Hand Instruments

Hand instruments preceded rotary cutting instruments by many years and, until the advent of modern high speed handpieces and adequate lubrication of the bur, they were still used for refining the cavity outline. In fact, in the beginning they were essentially the sole means of entering a cavity and G.V. Black and others developed a wide range of chisels, hatchets and hoes for the efficient removal of enamel and dentine and spoon excavators for cleaning out remaining caries (Figure 8.24). Now that rotary cutting instruments are sophisticated, fast and efficient, the need for hand instruments is greatly reduced. Two instrument types still have a place, however:
• gingival margin trimmers for planing the enamel margins in small spaces where access for rotary cutting instruments is limited
• spoon excavators for the final removal of softened, demineralized, infected dentine. Take care not to remove all softened dentine because some of it will be affected only and subject to remineralization.

Gingival margin trimmers
According to the classification of instruments defined by G.V.
Black, gingival margin trimmers are modified hatchets. The two modifications incorporated in these instruments are, first, that the cutting edge is at an angle to the shaft rather than a right angle and the second is that the blade is curved, i.e. it is a double‐plane instrument. It is the curve in the blade that makes it a lateral scraping instrument. They are paired instruments where the blade angle is set for either a left or right function and, in the partner instrument, the blade angle is set for planing the enamel rods in either a mesial or distal proximal box.
The width of the blade varies from 1.0 mm to 1.5 mm and it is the finer of the two which is recommended for continuing use. Typical numbers in the Black classification are 10.80.7.14 and 10.95.7.14 and these are fine enough to trim the cavosurface margins of the average minimal cavity without extending it

Spoon excavators
The spoon excavator is also a double plane instrument that can be regarded as a modified hatchet. However, the cutting blade is curved or rounded. It is also designed for lateral scraping and is generally provided with left and right blades at opposite ends of the same shaft. The typical spoon excavator number is
12.90.8.12 for the right side and 12.10.8.12 for the left, and the blades vary in diameter.
It is essential that the blades are maintained in a sharp condition and that they are used with a very light touch so that the demineralized layer on the floor of the average cavity known as the affected dentine (see Chapters 3 and 16) is maintained rather than removed.
Spoon excavators are designed for the removal of softened dentine at the base of a cavity. However, care must be exercised to avoid producing an unnecessary pulp exposure. The progress of the carious lesion through dentine must be well understood because the deepest layers of softened dentine are merely demineralized and not infected and therefore need not be removed (see Chapters 3 and 11). Certainly there is no need to remove discoloured dentine because this generally represents the translucent zone (see Chapters 1 and 14). It may be difficult on occasions to define the parameters of affected dentine and a sharp excavator may penetrate deeper than is required for control of the lesion.
