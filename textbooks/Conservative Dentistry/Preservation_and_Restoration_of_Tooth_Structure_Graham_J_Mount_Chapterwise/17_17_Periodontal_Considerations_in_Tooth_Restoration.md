### Normal Gingival Tissue

Normal healthy gingival tissue is generally described as firm, coral pink with a stippled surface and a smooth knife‐edged margin around the circumference of each tooth [1] (Figure
17.1). There is expected to be a zone of gingival tissue attached firmly to the underlying alveolar bone all the way around each tooth, varying in width from 1.0 to 5 mm and a crevice about
2 mm deep which can be traced with a fine probe. At the base of the crevice lies the epithelial attachment representing a firm biological attachment of the gingival tissue to the root surface.
The epithelial attachment normally begins immediately at the cemento‐enamel junction of each tooth and is, itself, about 2 mm wide towards the apex (Figure 17.2).

Any divergence from the above description is regarded as a disease state which requires diagnosis and treatment. However, as with all pathology, there is a wide variation of acceptable levels as well as a wide variation in treatment method [2].

Epithelial attachment
It is accepted that, at the time of eruption of a tooth, the epithelial attachment is located at the cemento‐enamel junction and initially the crevice around the tooth above the attachment will be very deep. By the time the tooth is fully erupted, however, the crevice should be only about 2 mm deep. It can remain at that depth for life but the position of the epithelial attachment may migrate down the length of the root of the tooth for a variety of reasons, not all of which are properly understood. It is not essential for it to move and patients of advanced age may show no change at all. It is also accepted that the zone of attached gingival tissue at the buccal and lingual of each tooth can be reduced to 1 mm or less without any indication of pathology and, in a few patients, can be absent entirely without any untoward result.

### Abnormal situations

Reasons for apparent aberrations are not clearly understood and it is suggested that the only indication of pathology or alteration from normal, which is of significance and requiring treatment, is the presence of inflammation [3] (Figures 17.3 and 17.4). In the presence of normal healthy tissue, its position, relative to the cemento‐enamel junction, is of no concern even in the presence of apparently bizarre situations. There will occasionally be situations where migration of the epithelial attachment or loss of attached gingiva is accompanied by severe loss of supporting bone to the extent that the life and stability of a tooth are compromised and surgical intervention is warranted. However, this will be in the presence of a generalized periodontal condition which is beyond the scope of this treatise.

### Problems which Compromise Periodontal

### Tissue

Gingivitis
Gingivitis can be identified as a change in the colour, texture and contour of the gingival tissue around part or all of the circumference of a tooth [4] (Figure 17.5). The earliest signs will be a subtle change in colour and a tendency to bleed from within the gingival crevice following gentle probing. These changes can progress to hypertrophy of the tissue with a major change in colour to a dull red and a tendency to be easily damaged by toothbrushing or even eating. The depth of the gingival crevice may increase through hypertrophy of the gingival tissue in an occlusal direction to a depth of 3–4 mm but the position of the epithelial attachment may remain at the cemento‐enamel junction, particularly in the early stages of gingivitis (Figure 17.6).

### The role of plaque

The cause of gingivitis is bacterial activity [5]. Bacteria attach to the surface of a tooth or restoration and become organized to the extent that they form a soft mass referred to as bacterial plaque. Eventually it will calcify and become calculus which will be firmly attached to the tooth surface and difficult to remove.
Calculus itself will have a layer of active bacterial plaque on the surface. If the plaque is allowed to remain in the vicinity of the gingival tissue, it will inevitably result in an inflammatory reaction in response to the presence of toxins from the bacteria. The pathogenic potential of plaque can vary from one individual to another and from one area of the mouth to another. It is accepted that the inflammation is an immunological defence mechanism and investigations are still being undertaken to determine if the body responds to a greater degree to one or another bacterial group [6].

### Treatment

Treatment of gingivitis is simply the removal of plaque and/or calculus. Achievement of this aim is not always easy because, over time, the plaque matures and takes up additional calcium and phosphate ions and turns into calculus, a hard accretion on the surface of the tooth which is difficult to remove (Figures
17.7–17.10). However, if plaque is to be removed effectively on a daily basis, the teeth must be maintained in a cleansible condition. Any situation in the mouth which compromises the ability of the patient to remove new accumulations of plaque must be avoided or eliminated. This includes maintenance of the correct contour of the crowns and the roots of the teeth, the avoidance of prosthetic replacements which are not cleansible and the elimination of areas of crowding of teeth which compromise cleaning techniques.

### Periodontitis

Periodontitis is the ultimate sequel to untreated gingivitis. It is essentially an extension of the inflammatory process into the connective tissue and the bone surrounding the teeth leading to the progressive destruction of those tissues. It has been suggested that periodontal disease progresses in bursts of activity ranging in length from a few days to a few months.

### Migration of epithelial attachment

The first stage involves the loss of the epithelial attachment at the cemento‐enamel junction. The crevice surrounding the tooth will become deeper and the patient’s ability to remove plaque from the depths of the crevice on a daily basis will be reduced.

### Treatment

Treatment of periodontal disease is based on the same approach as the treatment of gingivitis. Removal of the cause, the plaque and calculus, is of primary importance. Resolution of the damage arising from the disease is often very complex, and at present will be partial at best. A number of surgical procedures can result in new bone formation, new cementum and new attachment, but full restoration of alveolar height and therefore of optimal tooth support is rarely possible.

### Long epithelial attachment

The epithelial attachment will not regenerate in its original form following surgical intervention because the epithelial cells tend to propagate down the inside of a wound very rapidly and prevent the development of a normal tissue attachment on the cementum of the root surface. The result, often, is a ‘long epithelial attachment’ which, whilst not entirely physiological, can remain healthy and free of further inflammation in the presence of careful plaque control routines.

### Effect of Restorative Dentistry on Gingival

### Tissue

In view of the potential for the ultimate loss of the dentition through periodontal disease it is important to consider all the

implications of restorative dentistry on the periodontal tissues
[7, 8]. Restorative materials per se will not have a deleterious effect on the soft tissues (Figure 17.13). There are three different aspects to this problem:
• Prior assessment and treatment. It is important first, that the condition of the periodontal tissues be assessed prior to restoration of the teeth and that all necessary measures be undertaken to restore normal health.
• Minimal trauma during treatment. At the time of carrying out restorative procedures care must be taken to minimize trauma to the gingival tissues [9]. If the tissue is healthy to begin with, it can be expected to heal rapidly and uneventfully. However, it is possible to traumatize the tissues to such a degree during restorative procedures that normal healing cannot take place and some degree of permanent alteration to gingival contour will result.
• Restore original contour for plaque control. It is imperative to maintain the teeth in such a condition and contour that plaque control can be achieved readily on a daily basis [10]. The natural contour of the coronal anatomy of a tooth is generally such that hygiene procedures are relatively effective. However, over‐contour in any dimension will impose problems in plaque removal. Lack of contour is less of a problem providing it does not lead to food impaction between teeth or under prosthetic appliances. It is generally accepted that it is impossible to achieve a perfectly smooth union between any restorative material and the tooth being restored, so, wherever possible, it is desirable to maintain the restoration margin outside the gingival crevice. A further reason for keeping margins supra‐gingival is that some of the common restorative materials, such as composite resin, have a tendency to encourage plaque formation.

Treatment procedures

### Improvement of gingival tissue

Restoration of the gingival tissues to a normal healthy state prior to restorative procedures is essential, particularly if the cervical margin of a restoration has to be placed sub‐gingivally. In many cases it will be adequate to carry out a thorough prophylaxis with removal of plaque and calculus and the topical application of a fluoride gel.
The fluoride will remineralize the enamel and dentine and will also decrease the wettability of the surface of the tooth and therefore reduce future plaque formation. There is rarely any justification for the surgical removal of hypertrophied tissue at this point because elimination of the inflammation will lead to resolution of the soft tissue to the extent that surgery may no longer be required. If surgical modification can be justified, the tissue should be in good health to begin with and healing will be uneventful.

### Improvement of existing direct restorations

Existing restorations which are over‐contoured should be recontoured as far as possible at this time, even if they are

to be removed subsequently [11, 12]. In particular, old, corroded amalgam restorations and over‐contoured composite resins should be contoured and polished so that the patient can use dental floss or interproximal brushes to improve the quality of the gingival tissues [13, 14]. Recontouring can be achieved with very fine tapered diamond stones at intermediate high speed under air/water spray followed by reciprocating diamond blades in a special handpiece to produce a reasonable polish on the surface [15]. Local anaesthesia is not required even though there will often be a copious blood flow because of the gingival inflammation. Removal of the entire restoration at this point is not recommended because it is very difficult to replace in the presence of gingival inflammation and the final anatomy will not be determined until the inflammation is resolved. Temporary intracoronal restorations are not very successful because of the problems of achieving and maintaining proper interproximal contour and most of the temporary restorative materials are relatively fragile (Figures 17.14–17.20).

### Improvement of existing rigid restorations

Recontouring of ceramic crowns and gold restorations is more difficult but should, never the less, be attempted [16, 17]. Diamond burs are indicated on porcelain crowns but multiblade tungsten carbide burs remove gold more efficiently. There is a tendency to gouge into the restoration and it is difficult to achieve a perfect result. Therefore, if the original restoration is to be retained, it must be done with great care. If it is intended to eventually remake the crown, the construction of a high quality temporary restoration may be justified while treatment of the periodontal condition continues. However, the gingival tissue will respond rapidly to the initial recontouring and within a week it may be possible to determine the final contour of the gingival margin and therefore construct a better temporary crown.

Maintenance of occlusion
There are two factors in relation to the occlusion which are of significance to the periodontal tissues and these must be taken into account when restoring posterior teeth.

### Functionally opening contact

Impaction of food debris between two teeth can have a devastating effect on the interproximal periodontal tissues. The cause may be poor contour in relation to the marginal ridge, failure to rebuild a contact area to full contour or an over‐contour at the gingival margin but more commonly it arises because of deflective inclines on the occlusal surface of adjacent or opposing teeth. For example, when the patient closes towards the centric relation position during mastication of a food bolus, a distal‐facing incline on a lower molar may engage a mesial‐ facing incline on the opposing molar. The result may be that the upper molar moves distally and opens the contact between it and the adjacent tooth, allowing ingress of fibrous food debris.
On opening the mouth and relieving the load, the tooth will move mesially again and entrap the food debris. This situation occurs most commonly in relation to the terminal tooth on the arch but it is possible for it to arise between other contacting areas (Figures 17.21 and 17.22).
Therefore, in the presence of chronic food impaction, it is necessary to examine the relationship of the opposing teeth to determine the presence or absence of such contacts particularly as the teeth approach each other in the centric relation position. Occasionally there may be a dominant cusp occluding directly into the contact area of two opposing teeth – a situation which has been described in the past as a plunger cusp. However, in many cases, the problem arises from cuspal inclines not directly related to the contact area at all. Modification to the appropriate cusp inclines will overcome the problem.

### Maintenance of posterior support

As discussed in Chapter 18, a lack of posterior support can lead to undue load on remaining teeth and, if there is already periodontal disease present, it may be severely exacerbated. Maintenance or regeneration of the minimum eight units, in conjunction with treatment of the periodontium, will stabilize the situation.

### Placement of gingival margin relative

### to the gingival tissue

There are a number of factors which will need to be taken into account before deciding the final placement of the gingival margin for any restoration. Because of the potential for plaque accumulation along the interface between restoration and tooth structure, it is desirable to keep the margin out of the gingival crevice [18, 19].
However, the following factors will influence the decision.

### Strength of remaining tooth structure

Following caries debridement, there may be weakened undermined dentine or enamel along the gingival margin. As this will not be subjected to occlusal load, it need not be totally removed but fine acute angles of dentine should be flattened off and rough fragile enamel should be lightly planed with a gingival margin trimmer. It may then be impossible to avoid entering the gingival crevice.

### Retention of the restoration

The strength of retention of a restoration is dependent upon the length of the vertical walls of the preparation. This is of less significance when placing a plastic restorative material in an

intracoronal cavity because grooves and ditches can supplement retention. But it is very important for both inlays and extracoronal restorations. The situation may often arise where it is necessary to enter the gingival crevice in order to develop sufficient length to ensure retention. Very occasionally, gingival surgery with recontouring of the alveolar bone may be indicated but this is undesirable because of the difficulty of redeveloping a normal epithelial attachment.

### Aesthetics

Particularly when designing extracoronal restorations on the upper arch it will be necessary to compromise the position of the gingival margin to develop satisfactory aesthetics [20, 21].
Providing the gingival tissue is healthy, the gingival margin of the preparation can be placed up to 0.5 mm into the gingival crevice without provoking an adverse tissue response. Care must be taken, particularly with tissue retraction techniques for impression taking, to minimize tissue damage at the time of operation, and skilled technical work is required to develop a smooth union between the restoration and the tooth, with the ideal emergence profile, to minimize plaque retention.

Following any damage to the gingivae, there will be inflammation in the periodontal tissue even if it was perfectly healthy to begin with (Figures 17.23 and 17.24). The heat, redness and swelling, which will arise, may alter the gingival contour and subsequent resolution of the inflammation will modify the shape again, making it impossible to predict the ultimate contours.
The following methods can be utilised to maintain gingival health.

### Rubber dam and wedges

The most effective method of soft tissue damage prevention is proper protection. The routine placement of a rubber dam is strongly recommended even though dam, by itself, does not guarantee protection. The dam will displace the soft tissue to some

degree but it is still possible to tear the dam or have an instrument pass through it. When preparing a gingival margin, a further precaution is to place a wooden wedge between the teeth, thus displacing and protecting the dam as well. A wedge will serve multiple purposes in as much as it will also move teeth mesially and distally sufficiently to enhance access and improve the strength of the contact between the teeth following restoration.

### Retraction cord

An alternate method of protecting gingival tissue is to displace the gingival crest laterally by packing a short length of an astringent gingival retraction cord into the gingival crevice. When placed with care, the tissue will be displaced out of the way of any rotary cutting instruments.

### Localized removal of excess gingival tissue

Low grade chronic gingival inflammation, sustained over a long period, may resolve into relatively healthy but over‐contoured fibrous tissue. If it is allowed to remain, it may compromise the anatomy of a restoration and also it is easily damaged during operative procedures and will haemorrhage freely. There are several methods of removing such tissue or, at least, controlling the haemorrhage.
A brief application of trichloracetic acid will arrest the bleeding very effectively and resolve some of the tissue overgrowth (Figures 17.25 and 17.26). Electrosurgery or laser therapy can be used to remove larger areas of excess tissue.

Despite following the recommended procedure of thorough scaling and cleaning at the time of recontouring old restorations prior to embarking on final restoration, there will often be calculus still present on the interproximal surfaces immediately below the gingival margins of old restorations. ### Placement of matrices

Assuming the gingival tissue is in good health, very little damage will be sustained through the placement of a matrix. However, care must be taken to ensure a firm fit with good support for the band using wooden wedges as well as greenstick compound as required. Trim the matrix to contour and polish the roughened edges. Achieve a degree of separation between the teeth with a wooden wedge so that the ultimate contact with adjacent teeth will be firm and positive.

In view of the imperative need to control plaque development and retention in the oral cavity, there must be considerable emphasis placed on maintaining the correct anatomy of each tooth. As indicated previously, over‐contour of any part of the tooth anatomy will enhance plaque retention, particularly in the vicinity of the gingival margin (Figures 17.27 and 17.28).

Modification to gingival architecture

### Surgical modification

The ultimate aesthetic result of restorative dentistry is of importance to both the patient and the operator, particularly in relation to the anterior teeth. A lack of balance and symmetry in the gingival margins may be of significance. Surgical techniques have been developed which include recontouring both the bone and the soft tissue. These must be carried out prior to designing the gingival margins of the ultimate restorations and temporary restorations of very high quality should be constructed to ensure maintenance of gingival health during the healing process.

### Orthodontic modification

There will be occasions where orthodontic movement of a tooth or teeth is desirable prior to restoration. However, the gingival tissue is likely to remain in the same position relative to the cervical margin of the crown of the tooth following movement and surgical intervention may be required to adjust irregularities.
Orthodontic eruption of the remainder of a root following trauma or extensive sub‐gingival caries will always be accompanied by migration of the gingival margin and epithelial attachment as well. The gingival tissue will need to be repositioned and often some degree of alveolar bone recontouring will be involved. The restoration of the crown is further complicated in this technique by the fact that the dimensions of the root face will be notably smaller than the desirable aesthetic shape of the crown and a compromise is generally necessary, particularly in relation to the emergence profile.
In view of the difficulty of re‐establishing a normal epithelial attachment, it is suggested that the advantages of such surgery must be weighed carefully against the likely modification to gingival anatomy and surgery should be undertaken only when essential. The techniques come into the realm of periodontal surgery and will not be discussed further in this treatise.

### Correct occlusal anatomy

The occlusal anatomy is of equal importance particularly in the area of the marginal ridge (Figure 17.29):
• the marginal ridge should rise above the contact area and be smoothly rounded to allow ease of access of dental floss;
• there are generally shallow grooves running out to the buccal and lingual of the marginal ridge to guide the food bolus away from the contact area and off the height of the cuspal inclines;
• in youth, the cuspal inclines tend to be steep but, as the patient ages, the occlusal anatomy assumes a flatter profile;
• deep intercuspation between opposing arches is undesirable because of the lateral stress which can be imposed on cusp inclines, particularly nonworking cusps;
• it is important to adjust both opposing occlusal surfaces as required during restorative procedures to minimize the intercuspation because deep intercuspation may lead to the development of both balancing and working side interferences during lateral excursions.
