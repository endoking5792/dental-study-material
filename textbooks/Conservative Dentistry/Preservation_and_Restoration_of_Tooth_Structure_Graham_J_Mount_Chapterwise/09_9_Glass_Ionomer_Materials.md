## G

lass‐ionomer was developed in England and first reported by Wilson and Kent in 1972. At that time it was recommended for the restoration of cervical abrasion lesions but the early versions lacked aesthetics and translucency. Subsequent development and research by various manufacturers led to the evolution of a number of very useful materials which can be used for a variety of functions in both preventive, therapeutic coating, and restorative dentistry.
Glass‐ionomers are now used for luting rigid restorative materials, lining and basing cavities prior to placement of other restorative materials as well as an aesthetic, translucent restoration in their own right.
Since their development, glass‐ionomers have increasingly become an essential part of the dentist’s armamentarium for providing treatment that conserves tooth structure and assists in its remineralization while, at the same time, maintaining aesthetics.
Of the numerous attractive characteristics of the glass‐ionomers, the most significant are the ability to bond chemically to dentine and enamel through an ion exchange mechanism, as well as an ability to exchange ions with remaining tooth structure and the oral environment. This leads to a long‐term release of fluoride and other ions without increasing solubility and the ability to re‐absorb these ions. It therefore acts as an ion reservoir and leads to a degree of healing of a caries lesion.
The principal negative feature, at this point, is susceptibility to brittle fracture – the material is unable to withstand repeated occlusal or incisal loading.

### General Description

Glass‐ionomer is a water‐based cement formed from the acid/ base reaction between a polyalkenoic acid as the liquid and an ion‐leachable, fluoride containing, calcium or strontium aluminosilicate glass as the base [1]. The official ISO terminology for glass‐ionomer cements, ‘glass polyalkenoate cements’, indicates the principal components. The terminology used is consistent with current understanding of the chemistry and the properties of the materials, and has received general endorsement [2].
Glass‐ionomer cement (GIC) is a versatile material and is presented as restorative materials, liners, bases, luting cements and fissure sealants. Recently, it was suggested that GIC could also be useful in the preventive arena as therapeutic coatings.
This new terminology is used to describe a material that can be painted on a susceptible surface and form a long‐lasting coat to protect, both mechanically and chemically, against accumulation of plaque where a patient is unable to render effective oral hygiene. Some glass‐ionomers have been modified by the inclusion of various resins, such as HEMA, in an attempt to improve physical properties and increase resistance to early water contamination. However, there will come a point where the ion exchange properties will be overwhelmed in the presence of a resin or an inclusion, thus negating one of their principal advantages. In general, the auto‐cure materials, free of resin, remain the preferred versions under most circumstances [3].

The basic glass powder

The mixture is fused, quenched, ground and sieved to obtain a particle size ranging between 4–50 μm depending upon the proposed clinical application for each material. Generally, finer particles are used for luting cements and lining cements and coarser particles are used for restorative materials. The better tranlucency of the newer materials was achieved by matching the reflective index of the glass to that of the liquid. The range and particle size distribution will have a bearing on the working properties, the setting speed and the final physical properties of each material [4].

The glass powder may be modified in several ways. The reactivity of the glass is controlled by the fusion temperature and the heat history during manufacture. The glass mix will be taken to a given temperature, allowed to cool for a specified period, and then quenched in water. Materials formed with higher temperature glasses set more quickly and reactivity can be varied by modifying the annealing process. These modifications will be used to vary the setting time as well as the physical properties.
Various ions can be added to enhance radiopacity, such as strontium and lanthanum. It is interesting to note that strontium is close to calcium on the periodic table and can substitute entirely for calcium due to their similar atomic diameters and valency states. This has been incorporated into most of the modern materials and, as a side issue, has made it easier to study the remineralization of a demineralized tooth structure adjacent to a glass‐ionomer restoration. This arises because strontium ions will be taken up into the demineralized dentine in exchange for calcium ions in the apatite structure and can be differentiated under an SEM.

### Fluoride content of the glass

Fluoride is an essential component of a glass‐ionomer cement because of its effect on the temperature of fusion of the glass and its action as an oxide scavenger during the melt. It can affect the working characteristics and the ultimate physical properties of the finished restoration although it is not a physical part of the matrix. Even so, its presence can modify the translucency so some products use NaF instead. The fluoride ion is very small and highly reactive, almost as small as the hydroxyl ion, so it can move reasonably freely both out of and back into the set glass‐ionomer cement, without bringing about any dissolution

or breakdown because it is not an integral part of the matrix. Its presence is beneficial because it will enhance remineralization and inhibit demineralization in the surrounding and adjacent tooth structure.
The fluoride content of the glass can be varied and a moderate reduction will enhance translucency without unduly reducing the caries control potential. It may also have some inhibitory effect on acid production in plaque in the vicinity of a restoration.

The liquid
The scientific terminology of ‘glass‐polyalkenoate’ arises from the acid which is one of the polyalkenoic acid family, only a few of which have so far been tested in this context. A typical liquid contains a 40–55% solution of 2:1 acrylic acid‐itaconic acid copolymer in water or, alternatively, a copolymer of maleic acid/ acrylic acid. The use of copolymers improves storage compared with the aqueous solution of polyacrylic acid used in the original glass‐ionomers because they tend to become quite viscous relatively quickly. It is also possible to incorporate variations in the liquid. There has been limited experimentation on variations from the original polyalkenoic acid with trials being conducted with polyphosphonic acids but only the polyalkenoic acid has so far been clinically successful.

### Tartaric acid

To control the setting reaction, small quantities (5–15%) of the optically active isomers of tartaric acid are added to the liquid.
This assists the extraction of ions from the glass powder, retains the working time and sharpens the setting time. It also allows the use of lower fluoride‐containing glasses which are more translucent, thereby improving the aesthetic characteristics of the set cement.

### Anhydrous forms

The term ‘anhydrous’ has been suggested for some varieties, inferring that water is not part of the liquid. However, it is a misnomer in as much as the glass‐ionomers are a water‐based cement and water is an essential component of all types. In this particular group the polyacrylic acid, normally used as the liquid, has been vacuum‐dried and premixed with the glass powder so the liquid which is then incorporated during mixing is either water or a dilute aqueous solution of tartaric acid. On mixing, these cements have a relatively low viscosity and are particularly suitable for luting or lining purposes. Another advantage is that a polyacrylic acid of higher molecular weight can be used in a dehydrated form and this can lead to moderately improved physical properties.

### Resin‐modified glass‐ionomer

These materials consist of the components of a glass‐ionomer, as described above, modified by the inclusion of a small quantity of additional resin – mostly HEMA – as well as photo initiatorss and chemical initiators and traces of other resins and reactive chemicals [8]. They set partly by an acid/base reaction and partly by a photo and chemical polymerization. In some materials, the polymerization of the resin component also contains a chemical initiator mechanism. These are known as ‘tri’ cure materials.
The liquid contains 15–25% resin component in the form of
HEMA and other methacrylates, including dimethacrylates to cross‐link, and acidic methacrylates for compatibility with the acid/base cement. Some manufacturers include polymerizable groups on the acrylic acid copolymer, Depending upon the powder/liquid ratio used in the mix, there will be a residual HEMA content in the set cement ranging from 4.5%, where there is a high powder content, to possibly 15% in a thinly mixed lining or luting cement. As HEMA is hydrophilic, there is a potential for water uptake and subsequent degradation with release of
HEMA into the surrounding dentine.
In the first stages of the setting chemistry, the acid/base reaction between the glass and the polyalkenoic acid will still take place as described below but the moment light activation is undertaken. the resin component will polymerize and the risk of water contamination will be eliminated. The polyacrylate chain formation will continue in the same fashion as the regular auto‐cure glass‐ionomer but at a slower rate under a protective ‘umbrella’ of set resins. In the long run, any resin not set by light activation will set by auto‐cure, because of the presence of an incorporated reduction/oxidation (redox) reaction, and will achieve the same physical properties. This means that, even in the absence of light activation, these materials will set completely over time. It has been shown that their physical properties are not down‐graded if allowed to auto‐ cure only [9].
In some of the resin‐modified materials, a small quantity of the light activator catalyst is incorporated into the powder, thus making the powder susceptible to ambient light. Always follow the manufacturer’s instructions and do not dispense either the powder or liquid on to the mixing slab until immediately prior to mixing. This is not a problem if the materials are encapsulated.

### Resin‐modified auto‐cure material

Variations on the resin‐modified materials include ones which are either fully auto‐cure or else subject to a combination of light and heat plus auto‐cure [10]. For these materials, the powder

component will generally be very finely ground although the particle size will vary according to the proposed function. There will also be a small quantity of a catalyst incorporated that is designed to stimulate the auto‐cure reaction of the resin component. The liquid will contain the usual polyalkenoic acid, tartaric acid, water and HEMA, as well as a small amount of a cross‐ linkable monomer. Some products also contain a polycarboxylic acid modified with pendant methacrylate groups.
Following mixing of the powder and the liquid, the usual acid/base setting reaction will be initiated. At the same time the catalyst in the powder will initiate polymerization of the
HEMA and the cross‐linkable monomers. Ultimately there will be cross‐linking between the two systems and the entire mass will set hard, with uniform physical properties, even if there has been no light‐activation at all. This property is important in minimally invasive restorations where light might have difficulty reaching right through the restoration.

### Heat‐assisted auto‐cure material

Another variation incorporates a method for speeding up the setting reaction of the auto‐cure materials. This involves enhancing the speed of the acid/base setting reaction by using a simple physical principle. If a dye is incorporated in a glass‐ ionomer and the colour is specified such that the L* value, when expressed by an L*a*b* colorimetric system in a standard light
D65 is 60 or less, the curing reaction will be accelerated by the dye absorbing heat from the blue spectrum of the light source to hasten the acid/base reaction. Thus, if a glass‐ionomer is coloured red, then mixed, placed and irradiated by the standard blue dental halogen activator light, the acid/base setting reaction, which is already under way, will take place more rapidly.
The material can now be regarded as light‐initiated but there is a complete absence of any added resin. Setting time will be dramatically reduced but there will be no significant generation of heat and the physical properties of the set material will not be downgraded in any way.
This factor has recently been incorporated into a low powder/liquid ratio glass‐ionomer to which has been added a small quantity of a red pigment. As an additional benefit it has a high fluoride release. Because of its rapid command set, it can be recommended for a number of applications as a therapeutic coating, i.e. fissure protection, root surface protection and as a lining material. The distinct colour can be used by the dentist as a marker for specific procedures such as indirect pulp capping with partial caries removal.

### Polyacid‐modified composite resin: ‘compomers’

There is a further group of materials that contain either or both of the essential components of a glass‐ionomer cement but at levels insufficient to promote the acid/base setting reaction in the dark, i.e. the immediate acid/base reaction is not available because it is overwhelmed by the amount of resin present [11]. These materials will not set without light activation. They do not belong in the glass‐ionomer category and

Commonly called ‘compomers’, they can be quite useful materials as long as their limitations are recognized [12]. It is not possible for a compomer to bond to tooth structure through an ion exchange mechanism because the acid/base reaction does not occur for some time after placement. Compomers are largely no longer available on most markets and they are mentioned here for historical interest.

Clinical test to distinguish ‘resin‐modified glass‐ionomer’ from ‘polyacid‐modified composite resin’

For the clinician to differentiate between the resin‐modified glass‐ ionomers (‘dual’ or ‘tri’ cured) and a polyacid‐modified composite resin. there is a simple test that can be carried out in the clinic. Mix a sample of the appropriate material and place a small quantity on a glass slab for testing. Immediately after mixing, place it under a

lightproof cover and test it at brief intervals to check the degree of set. The true glass‐ionomer material will be reasonably hard to touch within 7 minutes. The other materials will not set at all although a few may achieve a rubbery consistency within 15 minutes. But they will not set beyond that without being subject to light activation.

### Giomers

A recent addition to the range of materials has been classified as a ‘giomer’. This contains pre‐polymerized glass‐ionomer particles, in a range of sizes, supported by a light‐activated resin vehicle. As the matrix is resin, the ion exchange potential will not be available to the surrounding tooth structure and the material clearly falls into the same group as the resin‐based composites.

### Potential modifications

Research continues and the potential for further modifications is broad [13, 14]. It must be noted that, to date, there has been little variation in the polyalkenoic acids used in these materials and also no great variation in the formula for the glasses. Both sides of the basic formula are subject to considerable variation in as much as there are many different kinds of polyalkenoic acid available and in the formula for the glass melt, there are a number of metals that could be tried in different combinations and concentrations.
Considerable effort has been expended in the search for inclusions that will enhance fracture resistance but so far without success. Glass or resin fibres have been tried but, as with silicate cements, collateral problems appeared, such as an inability to develop and retain a smooth surface on the restoration.
Recently, experiments have been carried out to enhance the therapeutic value by the inclusion of antibacterial agents [15], or remineralizing agents such as CPP‐ACP [16] (see Chapter 2).
Results are so far equivocal and the real value of such inclusions is questionable in the light of the already valuable ion exchange properties that should not be inhibited or affected. There have also been attempts at incorporating antibacterial agents such as chlorhexidine and chitosan.
The primary object in further research should be to increase fracture resistance because, even in the best of these materials currently available, this property is less than ideal. However, it is essential that the original ion exchange potential not be interfered with or reduced. The ion exchange with tooth structure is of immense value because it is the only material that will ensure a union which is proof against microleakage between the restoration and the tooth structure. It is therefore possible to seal a lesion and isolate it from further ingress of bacteria and/or their nutrients. In addition, the ion release has now been shown to enhance the remineralization of tooth structure that has been ravaged by caries, thus allowing the use of minimally invasive cavity preparations and preservation of natural tooth structure, even though demineralized (see Chapter 2).

Methods of dispensing
The first materials marketed were provided as a powder and a liquid and required mixing on a glass slab or paper pad. A small spoon was used for measuring the powder and the dropper bottle was intended to dispense a standard‐sized drop of liquid.
However, it was apparent from the beginning that dispensing,

with the high degree of accuracy required, was not possible, particularly when the person responsible was under the normal pressures of clinical practice. It was not long before the manufacturers developed methods of capsulation for accurate standardized dispensing and machine mixing.
One of the main disadvantages of encapsulation is the incorporation of porosities into the final mix. The latest development in the delivery of resin modified GIC is automixing which removes any potential for incorporating porosities into the final mix, while still allowing injection directly into a cavity or a fixed prosthesis. This mode of delivery would also allow the operator to mix only the required amount.

### Powder/liquid

Physical properties are heavily dependent upon the powder content and, for optimum results, full and correct measure is imperative. A small spoon is provided by the manufacturer to dispense the powder. Instructions specify that the spoon be filled to capacity and the excess be wiped off the top using a lip at the top of the bottle. Laboratory testing of this method shows that, no matter how carefully the operator tries, there will be a variation as much as 10% either way in the weight dispensed.
This is clinically unacceptable. If hand mixing is to be reliable, it is necessary to weigh the powder on a set of scales accurate to two decimal points.
The bottle of liquid is provided with a dropper mechanism designed to accurately dispense one drop at a time. It must be noted that the first drop out of a bottle is always deficient and should be discarded. Subsequently, if the liquid to be dispensed is a rather viscous polyalkenoic acid, it is essential to turn the bottle on its side first while the liquid flows into the dispensing tip to allow any air bubbles to escape. Hold briefly, turn vertical and only then dispense the drop. Any drop that contains an air bubble should be discarded. Again, the best way is to weigh the drop on the scales and then dispense the amount of powder that will give the right P/L ratio with that drop.
With some materials, the liquid is water or a dilute tartaric acid. This is easier to dispense with a dropper bottle but must still be done with care. Simply because it has a low viscosity, it is easy to dispense more drops than required.

### Capsules

There are now several designs of capsule available and all offer accurate proportioning of the powder and the liquid (Figure 9.3).
Laboratory experiments show a high level of reliability in both the powder and liquid proportions. Most of the capsules are designed to be both a mixing capsule as well as a dispensing syringe and placement with these allows for considerable accuracy. Each manufacturer will issue their own instructions for storage, mixing and dispensing. However, in general, it is desirable to gently disturb the powder in the capsule by tapping on the bench top before placing the capsule in the mixing machine. ### Paste‐Paste

There is a further variation in dispensing now available where the material is presented as a paste‐paste system, packaged in a twin syringe apparatus (Figure 9.4). The main advantage is that there will always be the correct proportions of both components dispensed on to the slab where it will then be hand‐ mixed. It is now possible for the operator to prescribe variations in quantity to be mixed without any problems in maintaining

the quality of the final mix. At present these systems are used for luting and lining cements, as well as for endodontic and orthodontic applications.
Recently, a nano‐filled restorative cement was made available in a capsule with self‐mixing capability. As a general rule, these paste‐paste materials require a higher resin content but it must be noted that both fluoride release and physical properties are compromised. They generally have a finer particle size and therefore an ultimate film thickness in the range of 5μ and a setting time within 3 minutes. In most of these materials there is a small quantity of resin included, as well as traces of a catalyst that is required to bring the setting reaction of both the resin and the glass‐ionomer to completion but the ion exchange mechanism with tooth structure remains unaffected.

Mixing

### Handmixing

Handmixing is possible for all types of cements, although great care is required when measuring out to avoid under‐ or over‐ dispensing. It is not possible to vary the size of a drop of liquid reliably and it is difficult to accurately dispense a spoonful of powder by hand and it is certainly not possible to dispense a partial mix in an attempt to conserve material.
The principal object during handmixing is to wet the surface of each glass particle without completely breaking down and dissolving the powder particles in the liquid. The strength of the set cement lies in the remaining glass particles rather than in the matrix. Therefore the mix should be undertaken quickly on a cool dry glass slab, or a paper pad, without spreading the mix around or spatulating heavily. As with all handmixed materials, reduction of the temperature of the glass slab to just above the dew point will allow for an extended working time but care must be exercised to avoid incorporating additional water into the mix. If the slab is stored in the refrigerator, then it should be wiped dry before placing on the bench and then checked again for further deposition of dew before dispensing the cement.
Dispense the liquid immediately prior to mixing in the centre of the slab and spread it out over a small area (Figures 9.5–9.9).
Do not allow it to stand exposed to air for longer than necessary because it will either lose or take up water depending upon ambient conditions. Measure the powder carefully onto the slab then divide the pile in half so it can be mixed in two parts. The first part should be incorporated by gently but rapidly rolling the powder into the liquid within 10 seconds. Then include the second part entirely leaving no residue and finish the mixing within a further 15 seconds. The finished mixed material should be ‘glossy wet’ on the surface and the working time should now be between 60–90 seconds. For convenience it should be transferred to a disposable syringe for accurate and positive placement into the cavity.

### Improved wetability

One of the original problems with handmixing was the relative reluctance of the liquid to actually wet the powder particles. For example, if a drop of liquid is placed on the top of a pile of powder (see Figure 9.10 and Figure 9.11), it will show no sign of absorption, so some degree of persuasion is required and mixing by hand becomes tedious. It has now been shown by one manufacturer that agglomeration of the particles and elimination of the dust from the powder will improve the wetability of the powder and reduce this problem considerably. As a result of the internal porosity of the granulated particles, the liquid is absorbed immediately and, while the granulate is strong enough for transport, it will break down rapidly with mixing. There appears to be some minor improvements in physical properties and it is possible to achieve a suitable mix in less than half the normal time. It is suggested that there would be some advan-

tages to be gained from the use of a granulated powder in areas where machine mixing is not available. A major weakness in the granulated powder is its shortened shelf life once the seal on the powder bottle is broken, specially in high humidity in a tropical climate.

### Machine mixing

Quality control in the manufacture of the capsules is generally very high but care must be taken in activating a capsule to ensure the full release of the liquid into the chamber containing the powder. Pay careful attention to the manufacturer’s directions for machine mixing. The time of mixing will vary from one manufacturer to another and there may well be variations in the consistency of the energy provided by different mixing machines. The energy of a mixing machine is likely to decline with age and once in a while the efficiency should be tested as discussed below. Mixing time can be varied to a small degree, either up or down, but it is unwise to rely upon changing the time of mixing. Rather, it is far better to maintain the efficiency of the machine.

### Paste‐paste

A recent innovation has the glass‐ionomer cement capsulated as two pastes in the one capsule with an auto‐mixing delivery system designed to ensure a reduction in the inclusion of porosities within the freshly mixed material. One half of the capsule contents is resin‐based and the other is water‐based and the two parts are fully mixed when dispensed direct into the cavity.
The main advantages are that the freshly mixed cement can be injected directly into the cavity with essentially no prior handling. Also, there is a notable reduction in porosity, leading to a moderate increase in physical properties in the set cement. The disadvantage is that these materials have a higher resin content so the ion exchange adhesion with the tooth structure will be compromised to some degree.

Setting reaction of auto‐cure materials
An understanding of the chemistry of the setting reaction of a glass‐ionomer is very important if full advantage is to be gained from the bio‐activity which is inherent with this group of materials [18, 19]. The true auto‐cure glass‐ionomer will undergo a simple acid/base reaction which leads to a diffusion‐based adhesion between the glass particles and the matrix. This process must be complete within a clinically acceptable time period.
Calcium or strontium and aluminium ions are released by acid attack on the surface of the glass particles and these ultimately cross‐link the polyacid chains into a network. The leaching of ions from the glass particles creates an ion‐depleted zone.
The fluoride‐rich phase can also be seen in this glass particle.

Three stages have been described in this setting reaction which is regarded as a long‐term chemical exchange. The calcium or strontium cross‐linking forms first, producing a clinically acceptable early set, but these are relatively fragile links that could be disrupted by early water contamination. The aluminium chains cross‐link thereafter and are strong and insoluble and provide the major physical properties of the set restoration.
With the early versions of the glass‐ionomers, the matrix formed in the initial stages of the setting reaction was predominantly

based upon calcium and was therefore highly susceptible to both water loss or water uptake. The speed of setting has been further modified by removal of calcium ions from the surface of the glass particles and the modern auto‐cure glass‐ionomers are based mainly upon aluminium and are therefore far more tolerant of early exposure to water. However, it is still recommended to protect all restorations with a waterproof sealant immediately after placement.
The setting mechanism involves the dissolution of the surface of the glass particles with the release of ions and can be divided into three clearly defined stages (Figures 9.12 and 9.13).

Loss of gloss/slump test

Learn to recognize a properly mixed capsulated specimen by carrying out a ‘loss of gloss/slump test’. Make a trial mix and express the cement on to a glass slab. Lift the top of the pile of cement with a small instrument until the cement breaks and slumps back on to the slab. There will come a point where the cement breaks but does not

slump. Measure that time and subtract 30 seconds to determine the working time [17].
Normally this should be between 60 and 90 seconds but for the resin‐modified glass‐ionomers it will be nearer to three or three and a half minutes in the absence of light activation.

Three stages of the setting reaction
Stage 1 Dissolution. The surface layer of the glass particles is attacked by the polyacid to produce a diffusion‐based union between the two. Approximately 20–30% of the glass is decomposed and ions, including calcium/strontium, aluminium and fluoride, are released, leading to formation of a cement sol.
Stage 2 Precipitation of salts – gelation and hardening. During this stage, the calcium/strontium and aluminium ions bind to polyanions at the carboxylate groups. The initial clinical set is achieved by cross‐linking of the more readily available calcium/strontium ions. This reaction is relatively rapid, usually forming a clinically ‘hard’ surface within 4–10 minutes from the start of mixing. Maturation occurs over the next 24 hours

as the less mobile aluminium ions become bound within the cement matrix, leading to more rigid cross‐linking between the poly(alkenoic acid) chains. Fluoride and phosphate ions form insoluble salts and complexes. Sodium ions contribute to the formation of an orthosilicic acid on the surface of the particles and, as the pH rises, this converts to a silica gel which helps bind the powder to the matrix.
Stage 3 Hydration of salts. Associated with the maturation phase is a progressive hydration of the matrix salts, leading to a sharp improvement in the physical properties.

Rate of setting
The rate of setting depends on a number of manufacturer‐ controlled variables, such as glass composition, temperature at which glass fusion occurs, temperature at annealing stage, powder particle size and size distribution, tartaric acid concentration, the composition of the liquid, and the inclusion of a resin component and photoinitiators. The rate is also dependent on several operator‐controlled factors, such as:
• the mixing temperature. Storage of the mixing slab and the bottle of powder in a refrigerator increases the working time by up to 25%. If the liquid is a polyalkenoic acid, never store it in the refrigerator because it is likely to gel at lower temperatures. Also beware of dew settling on the glass slab in areas of high humidity.
• the powder:liquid ratio. Although a higher powder content will yield improved physical properties, sufficient liquid must always be present to wet the surface of all the powder particles. Inadequate liquid results in a decline in translucency as well as a decline in physical properties. Variations in the ratio of powder to liquid will alter the clinical handling characteristics. A luting cement, for example, will be mixed with a lower powder content because it needs to be able to flow sufficiently to allow a fine film thickness between the restoration and the tooth. However, physical properties will be lower.

### Structure of the set glass‐ionomer

The set material contains particles of unreacted glass, surrounded and supported by a siliceous hydrogel, embedded in a polysalt matrix of cross‐linked polyalkenoic acid molecules rich in calcium (strontium) and aluminium ions [21]. The set cement contains water, both bound in and free, so it is regarded as a water‐based cement. Ion migration can only occur in the presence of water so this material is ideal to allow for the transit of ions both inwards and outwards within the oral environment
[22]. In particular, ions with small dimensions such as hydroxyl and fluoride ions will move through the material and will be available for remineralization of damaged tooth structure.

Water balance
Once the material is set, the water content is arbitrarily classified as either ‘loosely bound water’ or ‘tightly bound water’. The loosely bound water can be lost quite readily in the early stages after placement if the cement is left exposed to air but the tightly bound water cannot be removed at all. The latter is associated with the hydration shell of the cation‐polyacrylate bond, particularly that of aluminium and, to a degree, some of the silica gel water as well.
Successful management of the water balance of the glass‐ ionomer material is probably the most significant factor in ensuring long‐term success with the auto‐cure cements [23].
Approximately 24% of the set glass‐ionomer is water and susceptibility to water uptake and loss are shown in Figures 9.16 and 9.17.
The cement is most susceptible to moisture contamination during Stage 2 of the setting process before the calcium/strontium ions have been locked into the resistant gel matrix. It also remains susceptible to water loss for some time after placement.
The period of vulnerability to water loss and water uptake will vary with the material and considerable research has been undertaken to minimize the problem. Most of the present materials and particularly the resin‐modified glass‐ionomers will be resistant to water uptake within minutes of placement but they all remain susceptible to water loss for anything up to two weeks [24].
The best method of protecting the cement from either water loss or water uptake is to paint a liberal coating of a single component, light‐activated, resin enamel bond over the restoration immediately it is set [25, 26]. Most manufacturers supply the appropriate material. Remove the matrix and immediately apply the seal. This will maintain isolation for about 24 hours and thereafter the water balance will be stable. Note that not all the glass‐ionomers are equally unstable but routine sealing of all variations of these cements is strongly recommended.
Except for the slow set aesthetic materials, all types of auto‐ cure cements have been modified during manufacture by removing some of the excess calcium/strontium ions, thereby limiting the formation of calcium polyacrylate chains. There is at least one material that is so fast‐setting that it is best exposed to water immediately after it has achieved its preliminary set because it appears to require additional water to achieve full strength [27].

Adhesion to tooth structure

### Bond to mineralized tissue

One of the most important characteristics of glass‐ionomer materials is their ability to adhere through an ion exchange to mineralized tissues (Figures 9.19–9.22).
The probable mechanism is based upon both diffusion and adsorption phenomena [29] and is initiated by the polyalkenoic acid when freshly mixed material, that still contains available unreacted acid, contacts the tooth surface. Phosphate ions will be displaced from the apatite by carboxyl groups, each phosphate ion taking a calcium ion with it to retain electrical neutrality. The setting of the glass‐ionomer, combined with the release of mineral ions from the surface of the enamel or dentine, leads to buffering of the polyacid, a rise in the pH and re‐precipitation of minerals at the cement/tooth interface. It appears that chemical bonding is therefore achieved through the combined calcium/strontium phosphate/polyalkenoate crystalline structure acting as an interface between both enamel or dentine and the set material. This can be described as a ‘diffusion‐based adhesion’ [30–32].

### Clinical Properties of Glass‐Ionomers

Biocompatibility

### Biological potential of glass‐ionomers

Prior to the introduction of glass‐ionomer cements, all of the restorative materials used in dentistry were biologically inert relative to the tooth structure they replaced. As long as the profession concentrated on trying to eliminate caries through a surgical approach, there was not a great deal of interest in the potential for bioactivity, apart possibly, from the release of fluoride ions. It was recognized in the 1950s that silicate cement released fluoride and this was regarded as a potentially useful novelty.
When the glass‐ionomers were released in 1976, it was noted that they also released fluoride ions in the same manner and for that and other reasons, most notably their ability to adhere to enamel and dentine, they rapidly replaced the silicates.
Recent investigations have looked more deeply into the exchange of ions both out of the tooth structure and back into it from a variety of sources. The saliva, and therefore the biofilm, are of course the most important elements from this point of view because, in a healthy patient, the aqueous phase adjacent to the tooth surface is a super‐saturated solution in relation to both calcium and phosphate ions. However, it is now known that glass‐ionomer cement is also a rich source of ions and it is possible to trace the exchange from the cement both into and out of tooth structure as well as other skeletal structures [38].
There is already considerable clinical evidence of the value of these techniques and clearly it opens the way to far more conservative methods of dealing with the ravages of caries.

The fluoride ion is not a structural part of the matrix and is approximately the same size and mobility as the hydroxyl ion. This means that a continuing exchange of fluoride ions can occur, depending on the gradient of fluoride available at any given time in the oral environment generally. Fluoride release normally occurs from the matrix into the adjacent environment but, in the presence of a high fluoride concentration in the mouth, such as during a professional application of fluoride as a preventive measure, fluoride ions can be taken up into the cement again (Figures 9.40 and 9.41). Glass‐ionomer materials can, therefore, be regarded as a fluoride reservoir.

It is suggested that, for the fluoride concentration to be effective in initiating re‐ mineralization, the concentration in saliva should be at least 10 parts/million. It is this level which is available from glass‐ionomer materials and any concentration below this level will be of little or no clinical significance.

Solubility and disintegration
In a clinically relevant organic acid solution, such as lactic acid, solubility is low compared with zinc phosphate and zinc polycarboxylate cements [45]. In water, solubility is less than that of silicate cements but slightly greater than that of several other cements including resin materials.
However, the surface of glass‐ionomer can be damaged in the presence of a low pH such as during the application of some topical fluoride solutions. Acid phosphate fluoride solution has a pH 3.0 while neutral sodium fluoride is about pH 6.5. Therefore, it is possible for roughening of a restoration surface to occur if a phosphate fluoride solution is applied regularly. Caution should be exercised when using these solutions near glass‐ ionomers, particularly in relatively hostile environments such as those found in patients with xerostomia. In conditions of severe xerostomia, such as found in Sjögren’s Syndrome, auto‐cure glass‐ionomers will often only last for a year or so before disintegrating unless they are laminated with composite resin.

### Dimensional change

A free‐standing specimen of a glass‐ionomer cement, correctly manipulated and protected from early exposure to hydration or dehydration, will show a volumetric setting contraction of approximately 3% which develops slowly through the prolonged setting process. In the presence of adhesion, through the ion exchange with tooth structure, the shrinkage will be controlled and, in view of the time taken for the setting reaction, there will be a degree of stress relaxation leading to a greatly reduced marginal discrepancy.
The resin‐modified glass‐ionomer restorative materials contain less than 5% additional resin and show a very small initial shrinkage of the resin component at the time of light activation.
Subsequent shrinkage from the continuing acid/base reaction will develop very slowly and will be controlled to a degree by the adhesion. Over time, the set material will take up water and expand to some degree and this will more than negate any setting shrinkage. In contrast, light‐activated composite resins

### Resistance to fracture

One of the few limitations of glass‐ionomers is their susceptibility to brittle fracture. Compared with hybrid composite resins and dental amalgams, glass‐ionomer materials are weak and lack rigidity [46]. Clinical use should avoid situations that subject the restoration to heavy occlusal load or bending.
There is a difference in strength among various glass‐ionomer cements, with a substantial difference between the original auto‐cure glass‐ionomers and composite resins or amalgams.
The resin‐modified glass‐ionomers are stronger, with the best of them showing more than double the fracture resistance so that they are nearly comparable with the microfil composite resins (Table 9.2). Recent research has led to improvements in the physical properties of the auto‐cure materials and there are a number now on the market with a fracture resistance superior to the resin‐modified materials and very close to microfilled composite resins. Further improvements are expected.
It should be noted that the presence of silver does not impart additional fracture toughness to silver cermets although it does improve abrasion resistance. Cermets are not indicated where occlusal loading precludes the use of other glass‐ionomer materials.
The modulus of elasticity, which measures rigidity, ranges from 7 GPa to 13 GPa and the silver cermets tend to have a relatively low elastic modulus.

### Abrasion resistance

Immediately after placement, glass‐ionomers are less resistant to abrasion than composite resins but their resistance improves considerably as they mature. Providing the material is well supported and protected by remaining tooth structure, abrasion resistance is satisfactory. Because abrasion leads to loss of matrix, there will be an increase in surface roughness over time with exposure of the glass particles and internal porosities (Figures 9.42 and 9.43).

### Radiopacity

Radiopacity can be achieved by the incorporation of barium (Ba), strontium (Sr) or lanthanum (La), by fusing metal to the glass particles, as in cermet powders, or by mixing in dental amalgam alloy or zinc oxide. The silver cermet has a radiopacity approaching that of dental amalgam. Radiopacity can be important in many applications of minimal intervention dentistry but is unnecessary and undesirable for materials used where aesthetics is of prime importance.
Most of the glass‐ionomers are more radiopaque than dentine and several exceed the radiopacity of enamel. Some of the auto‐cure Type II.1 restorative aesthetic cements are entirely radiolucent because the incorporation of a radiopacifier may alter their translucency.

### Clinical Considerations

Taking into account the foregoing factors it is suggested that, during clinical placement of each of the types, consideration should be given to the following factors.

Type I – Luting

### Reasons for use

The following are the main reasons for regarding glass‐ionomer cement as useful for luting indirect restorations [48, 49]:
• fine ultimate film thickness, easily obtained because of good flow properties;
• low solubility in the oral environment;
• tensile strength and abrasion resistance equivalent to zinc phosphate;
• presence of a continuing fluoride release;
• high tissue tolerance from both pulp and gingival tissue.

### Strength of retention

Strength of retention with an indirectly fabricated restoration, is directly proportional to the area of the vertical walls of the preparation and the accuracy of fit. It is unwise to expect to enhance the retention with any luting agent because the tensile strength of the cements is in the order of only 2‐4 MPa. Therefore, glass‐ ionomer cement should not be used for its retention properties and conditioning of the tooth structure, prior to cementation to enhance adhesion, is undesirable.

### Ultimate film thickness

The powder in the luting cement should contain particle sizes of
10 μm or less to allow for optimum film thickness [50, 51]. The freshly mixed cement shows properties of thixotropic flow so it is unnecessary to maintain pressure on the newly placed restoration once it is fully seated. For preparations with long parallel sides, it is wise to vent the crown to encourage full seating.

### Correct powder/liquid ratio

The prescribed powder/liquid ratio is normally 1.5:1 and it is important to achieve this during mixing [52]. Capsulation is the

preferred method of dispensing and mixing. Too little powder will leave an excess of liquid with the risk of post‐insertion sensitivity and increased solubility. An excess of powder will reduce the ability to achieve complete seating of the restoration thus altering the fit and the occlusion.

Type II.1 – Restorative aesthetic

### Reasons for use

The following are the main reasons for using these materials as restoratives in their own right [53, 54]:
• adequate aesthetics and translucency are available in both the auto‐cure and the resin‐modified materials;
• physical properties are sufficient as long as the restoration is fully supported by surrounding tooth structure and it is not subject to undue occlusal load;
• adhesion can be achieved with underlying tooth structure through the ion exchange mechanism, thus completely eliminating microleakage;
• the material acts as a fluoride reservoir. The continuing release inhibits plaque formation on the restoration, thereby enhancing tissue tolerance, as well as providing a source of fluoride for remineralization of any adjacent demineralized tooth structure [18].
• it is a bioactive material so there is a continuing exchange of fluoride and other ions available from the restoration into both the tooth structure as well as the surrounding oral environment.

### Dispensing and placement

The prescribed powder/liquid ratio is 3.5:1 or greater and, for optimum physical properties, the powder content should not be reduced. Capsulation is the preferred technique for dispensing and mixing because it is always reliable.
The use of a matrix is desirable to improve adaptation of the cement to the cavity floor and to help restore the original crown form. It will also minimize the subsequent need to contour and polish the restoration.

### Water balance

Maintenance of the water balance of the auto‐cure cements in this category is essential. Some manufacturers claim to provide a cement which is resistant to water uptake within 15 minutes of mixing but all these materials will develop superior translucency if they are protected with a waterproof sealant for at least the first hour.

### Light activation

Manufacturers recommend 20–40 seconds of light activation for the resin‐modified materials. However, as it is not possible to light‐activate excessively, it is recommended that the light be applied for at least 20 seconds in each of several directions to ensure the material is fully cured.

### Contour and polish

Initial contouring is best carried out using a matrix. Any excess can then be modified using fine diamonds at intermediate high speed under air/water spray and progressing finally to graded aluminium oxide discs, also under air/water spray. Take care not to dehydrate the cement in the first six months after placement.

Type II.2 – Restorative reinforced

### Reasons for use

In situations where a fast‐setting material is desirable with increased physical properties, but the colour match and translucency of the restoration with the tooth, are not important.

### Physical properties

The physical properties of this group, particularly the tensile strength, ultimate compressive strength and the fracture resistance are not yet comparable with amalgam and will never be as good as gold. However, considerable progress has been made in improving these properties so their uses will expand. There are now materials with strength values, assessed by a shear punch test, close to microfilled composite resins.

### Core build‐up

The term ‘core build‐up’, although widely used in relation to these materials, is really a misnomer. While it can be used to a limited degree for rebuilding a badly broken down tooth in preparation for the placement of a full crown, its physical properties are such that it cannot be relied upon to accept any undue occlusal or lateral load. It is essential that there is still adequate remaining natural tooth structure strong enough to withstand the stresses to which the crown is likely to be subjected. There should be at least one half of the original crown bulk present with a collar of at least 3 mm of natural dentine around the gingival margin, incorporated in the preparation. Reinforcement of glass‐ionomer with pins and posts will not be sufficient, on their own, in the absence of sound dentine.

Type III – Lining and base cements

### Reasons for use

There are two different applications for this group based on the powder/liquid ratio employed:
• low powder content (ratio 1.5:1) should be used only as a lining under another restoration to prevent thermal change irritating the pulp;
• high powder content (ratio 3.5:1 or greater) should be used as a base or dentine substitute with another material laminated over.

### Lining cements

Lining cements have relatively low physical properties and are designed to be entirely covered by another restorative material.
They should be used in thin section to fill voids in cavity design and act as a thermal insulator to prevent pulpal insult. Because of

their physical properties they should not be expected to act as a bonding agent for the overlying restorative material. Under no circumstances should they be left exposed to the oral environment.

### Dentine substitute

When the cement is mixed with a high powder content, it can be regarded as a dentine substitute and can act as a significant part of a total restoration. Always use the strongest material available with the highest possible powder content and completely restore the cavity with glass‐ionomer. It can then be cut back to make room for a stronger material, such as amalgam or composite resin, to be laminated over it.

### The Lamination or ‘Sandwich’ Technique

The concept of using two different materials to form one final restoration is new to dentistry and leads to some confusion
[55–57]. The rationale behind the technique is to make the most of the biological, physical and/or aesthetic properties of each material and, in the presence of adhesion, to achieve as close as possible to a single monolithic reconstruction of a tooth. It has been shown that a tooth can be restored to its original physical strength by this method with no compromise in aesthetics (Figures 9.46–9.54).
As long as there remains doubt about the efficiency and longevity of techniques for bonding composite resin to dentine, the lamination technique must be the preferred system for the placement of these restorations in any cavity where at least one margin is located on dentine [58]. Also, where a cavity extends deeply interproximally beyond the cemento‐enamel junction, and the ultimate restoration is to be amalgam, then it is relatively simple to place a glass‐ionomer base in the gingival half of the proximal box and laminate over that with amalgam.

Principles of lamination
To obtain the maximum benefits from the technique the following principles must be observed:
• Use the strongest glass‐ionomer available, place it in quantity and regard it as a dentine substitute – note that resin‐modified is not necessarily the strongest.
• Avoid the placement of a sub‐base, such as calcium hydroxide, because it will reduce the area for adhesion of the glass‐ ionomer cement.
• Condition the cavity as usual with polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds to develop full ion exchange adhesion with the underlying tooth structure
• Place the glass‐ionomer cement and allow it to set fully, either chemically or by light initiation, before proceeding.
• Once set, cut the glass‐ionomer back to expose all the enamel margins because the composite resin union to sound enamel is the strongest available.
• If the gingival margin of the proximal box in a Site 2, Size 3 restoration is sub‐gingival and there is no enamel at that margin, leave the glass‐ionomer exposed.
• Allow sufficient space for a reasonable thickness of composite resin, taking into account its relative flexibility under load.
• Build the contact area with the adjacent tooth in composite resin rather than glass‐ionomer cement.
• Develop a union between the glass‐ionomer cement and the composite resin by lightly etching the glass‐ionomer while etching the enamel so as to obtain optimum advantage from the ‘monolithic reconstruction’ concept.
• Always use a radiopaque glass‐ionomer.

### Lamination of auto‐cure glass‐ionomer

It is possible to develop a mechanical interlock between an auto‐cure glass‐ionomer and composite resin similar to the union between composite resin and enamel. As soon as the glass‐ionomer is set, apply a standard acid etchant to both the glass‐ionomer and enamel margins and leave for 15 seconds only. Wash thoroughly and dry lightly but do not dehydrate the glass‐ionomer. Apply a thin layer of a low viscosity enamel bonding resin and light cure. There is no need to use a complex dentine bonding routine because there is no dentine still exposed to which to bond. Finally, place the composite resin using a standard incremental build‐up technique.

### Lamination of resin‐modified glass‐ionomer

It is unnecessary to etch a resin‐modified glass‐ionomer cement when laminating it with composite resin, because there appears to be sufficient bonding capacity in the HEMA, incorporated in the cement, to ensure an adequate chemical bond between the two materials. If acid etchant does flow over the cement, however, it will do no harm. Follow the same routine as described above but etch the enamel only.

### Lamination of amalgam

This combination is likely to be used in restoration of a molar tooth where the gingival margin of the proximal box is well sub‐ gingival. Use the strongest glass‐ionomer available as the base and develop the full ion exchange adhesion to tooth structure by conditioning the cavity. Ensure the material is fully set and then cut it back reasonably generously to allow sufficient space to compensate for the relatively brittle nature of the amalgam.
Expose all the enamel margins and develop a mechanical interlock design in both the glass‐ionomer and the remaining tooth structure so that the amalgam is firmly locked into position in the usual style.
It is possible to develop a degree of chemical union between the glass‐ionomer and the amalgam by wiping a small quantity of 45% polyacrylic acid (standard glass‐ionomer liquid) over the set cement just prior to packing the amalgam. Wipe off any excess but leave a fine layer of acid on the cement only. The freshly packed amalgam will now unite with the glass‐ionomer to some degree, although the value of such union is open to question.
