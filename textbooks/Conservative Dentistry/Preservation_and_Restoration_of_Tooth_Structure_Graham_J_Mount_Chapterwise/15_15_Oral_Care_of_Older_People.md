## T

he aging patient is the one most likely to be suffering chronic health problems and to this can be added chronic dental health problems. The challenge for the dental profession is to avoid adding to these, and at the same time ensure that the patient’s latter years are comfortable and free of dental complications. At the same time it is essential to acknowledge that age is physiological rather than chronological, and that some patients will reach their physical limits much earlier than others.
Maintenance of the dentition becomes the dominant consideration rather than restoration to physiological perfection. This means that all the disciplines devoted to prevention of further disease become paramount and it may be unwise to embark on extensive restoration. The patient must be counselled on the risks inherent in excess refined carbohydrate and the central importance of correct oral hygiene. The loss of manual dexterity may well complicate this situation and the availability of routine hygiene services with topical fluoride application becomes more important. Education in the use of an electric toothbrush using a high fluoride toothpaste, periodic professional application of fluoride and in some cases daily use of a chlorhexidine mouth rinse will assist in maintaining the remaining dentition without restorative heroics. Retention of the minimum of eight units of posterior support is generally sufficient for most patients, providing the teeth are in good condition and the supporting periodontium is healthy. With routine preventive care even better outcomes can be achieved for most patients.

### Introduction

Who are old people?
Few of us like to be labelled as ‘old’ and even traditional means of defining populations – such as by sex or income level – attract criticism. However, within the medical and dental fields it is often necessary to define a population, or sub-population, in order to best describe their health needs and/or a particular vulnerability. These populations may require unique attention through the delivery of particular services specific to that subpopulation. Health needs assessments are prerequisites for practitioners specifically defined by those policy-makers and public health officials who define, purchase or commission services.
Preventive health services in dentistry have been concentrated, in the main, on young children. There is a vast amount of evidence to support interventions in youth and they are certainly a group at high risk of caries. Defining young children is easy through the developing dentition in its primary, mixed and early permanent stages as well as to define groups of interest such as pre-school, primary school or secondary education. Such definitions are operationally useful not only for population interventions but also for individual care programmes in the private dental practice. Epidemiological surveys report on the burden of caries in children, and national and international global comparisons are possible. The definition of ‘young’ within dentistry draws little criticism and it seems to be allied with a considerable range of other health initiatives that are similarly targeted at this group.
However, beyond late adolescence and the beginning of the adulthood era, definitions are more problematic. Broad age brackets, economic status, employment status or other characteristics can be used to describe adult groups. However, with the advancing years, the definitions often become more problematic, none more so that the concept of ageing, which, in the current medical vernacular leads to the term ‘Frail and Elderly’.
Viewed by many as pejorative, the term is, none the less, an attempt to move away from a purely chronological concept of ageing. For example, chronologically over the age of 65 years may be regarded by some as being old, but, in fact, the biological age and state of health would be the best measure. It must be acknowledged that the older group of individuals represents a heterogeneous mix with a diversity of experiences throughout life that shape the health care requirements in an individual way.
The use of chronological categories, or even typical life cycle models, tends to describe stereotypical individuals who reflect a perceived norm at a given age. Such categories have often been used in the characterization of health loss with increased age but they ignore the vast diversity generally seen in this population.

Defining biological ageing in a meaningful way
There is a range of indices that assist in placing the older population into categories using the medical terminology of ‘frail and elderly’ [1, 2]. These vary in their approach and there has been a mixed reception to their usefulness in predicting negative health outcomes [3]. A recently reported study examined three different models of frailty using the terms, the ‘frailty index’, the ‘frailty

phenotype’ and the ‘FRAIL scale’. It seems that all three can significantly predict future mortality [4]. Despite the contested nature of such indices, there is a need to ensure that health care providers can define populations and population groups. This means there is a requirement to put populations into categories to assist in assessing health care needs and so allow proper planning.
The use of such indices in dentistry is limited and research has tended to focus on the residential setting of older people rather than a measure of their physiological health [5, 6]. While this is an important consideration in the planning and provision of oral health care services, it does not necessarily infer a level of need or disease burden. In a recent conference and workshop on dental care for older people, the following definitions were developed to help describe patient types that might be considered useful in defining dental care needs:
1	 No Dependency. Fit, robust individuals who exercise regularly and are in the best condition for their age [1].
2	 Pre-Dependency. The patient has a chronic systemic condition with potential impact on oral health but it is not currently relevant. The symptoms are well controlled [1].
3	 Low Dependency. Patient with an identified chronic systemic condition that is currently impacting on oral health but who currently receives or requires no care-giver support in managing access to dental services or maintaining oral health.
These patients are not frankly dependent but their chronic disease symptoms are affecting them [1].
4	 Medium Dependency. Patient with an identified chronic systemic condition that is currently impacting on oral health and who receives or requires care-giver support in managing access to dental services or maintaining oral health. This category would include patients who demand to be seen at home or where logistical services to move patients are not available.
5	 High Dependency. Patients whose complex medical management prevents them from being moved elsewhere to receive dental care. This category differs in that the patient may need to receive care at ‘home’.
These categories are clearly independent of age group and, with the exception of the final category, are not related to residential setting. Considering these definitions is helpful in defining the care required to maintain oral health and the approach to prevention and treatment. However, such definitions can remain abstract and therefore four case scenarios are presented at the end of this chapter to place these definitions into context and to reinforce points that will be made below.

Epidemiology of older people
While it is suggested that a definition of older people should be based on need, the epidemiology of populations remains based on chronological age. Nonetheless, data lead to an understanding of demographic shifts that are occurring globally, and explain why there is a need to ensure that the population is considered when developing plans for oral health delivery on either an individual, local, national or international basis.
It is widely accepted that there is a demographic transition underway [7] and that this transition is a combination of a falling

While the life expectancy may not be as long as in slower growth countries, the numbers of older people still represent a significant challenge for health care provision.
The significant factor in all these figures is that there is a growing population of older people with an expectation for living longer still.

### Oral Health Epidemiology of Older People

The epidemiology of childhood oral care conditions, mainly caries, is reported widely and in depth. However, when it comes to reporting and analyzing adults, and older adults in particular, the data are more heterogeneous. This is due, in part, to the varied methods by which periodontal diseases are measured and the complexities of scoring secondary and root caries. However, the data available are useful indicators of need.

Tooth loss
Either through dental caries or periodontal diseases, tooth loss may lead to edentulism. The precise reasons for rendering an individual edentulous vary and it is unlikely that simple incremental tooth loss accounts for many cases. It is far more likely that this is a decision made by health care professionals in combination with their patients and will be influenced by a range of social determinants. The prevalence of edentulism in developed countries has been steadily declining. For example, in the UK, approximately 6% of adults have no teeth and 35 million Americans are edentulous. These remain significant numbers.
Individuals who are edentulous have been shown to have poorer diets and their oral status is likely to have additional negative impacts on their general quality of life in relation to speaking, eating, chewing and socializing. With the current atti-

tude to maintenance of the dentition, it is likely that dentists will need to manage these patients, and those who continue to experience incremental tooth loss, for many years to come.

Dental caries
It must be noted that older people can become again as cariesactive as they may have been in their youth and that their carious lesions are not restricted to coronal surfaces. Older patients are likely to experience a mean caries increment of about one surface per year and the majority of these will be root caries. Add to this there will be secondary caries, that is to say, lesions occurring in relation to existing restorations. Residential setting appears to be a strong predictor of increased caries activity, with some reports suggesting that older people in residential care show double the caries increment of their community-dwelling peers. If residential care patients also suffer from dementia, then the increment is doubled again. These data support the notion that caries risk persists throughout life and that effective caries prevention, whether delivered professionally or via self-care, is essential.

Periodontal problems
Older people will often have some experience of periodontal diseases beginning with loss of attachment of the gingival tissues at the coronal margin. Some of this will be simple migration of the attachment rather than the more complex periodontal diseases that lead to the development of deep pockets around the gingival margin with loss of alveolar bone levels.
However, failure to understand and maintain a high level of oral hygiene, with routine removal of accumulated bacterial plaque, will lead inevitably to the development of a low grade inflammation in the gingival tissues. This in turn will lead to loss of alveolar bone support followed by loss of teeth. It is only in relatively recent years that the dental profession has learned the complexities of these problems and developed techniques for controlling and eliminating them. The introduction of an electric toothbrush may be of assistance to the patient with declining dexterity but they will generally require personal instruction in its application.
Treatment methods for these problems are discussed elsewhere.

Oral cancer
While this is a relatively uncommon problem in developed countries, when it occurs, it is devastating to the afflicted individual and their family. Cancer in the areas of the head and neck generally has a poor outcome and is mostly associated with the use of tobacco. It may also be related to a high alcohol intake and recently the Human
Papilloma Virus has been implicated, mostly in younger patients.
Again, these problems are discussed in depth elsewhere.

Dry mouth
Xerostomia is the term used to identify the situation where there is a lack of salivary gland function leading to reduced saliva flow.
It is not always identified and reported by the patient but there is strong evidence that it contributes to poor oral health. The patient suffering from xerostomia will frequently undertake behaviours to relieve the dry mouth, such as sucking on sweets, and this may lead to an increase in the caries rate. The low saliva

Often this is simply the result of inherited characteristics but for others there is always the possibility of ill health or lack of self-care in youth, along with any number of chronic disabilities, some of which may have no obvious cause. Biological age is far more important than chronological age from the dental point of view and has to be taken into account when diagnosing and treatment planning for a new patient.
It is relatively simple to examine a new patient and just place them into a category. However, thanks to modern medicine, patients can survive for long periods in the presence of low grade chronic problems that once were considered terminal. It is not the place for the dental professional to make decisions on possible longevity but at the same time, if the profession is to maintain responsibility for the remaining dentition, it will inherit the problems inherent in any systemic disease.
The hypothetical cases on the following pages are presented as possible examples to be considered, but it must be accepted that these are mere samples and are far from comprehensive.
There will be many more examples possible and it is up to the true professional to assess each case on its merits and treat it with the understanding that the dentition is important to the patient and they do not need further complications in their life as it is approaching its end.

Summary

Older patients suffer from the same oral health challenges as any other adult group. They remain susceptible to caries and periodontal diseases and vigilance over pre-malignant lesions is important. Caries remains the dominant cause of tooth loss in this group and the need for poly-pharmacy, along with moving to residential care, are both important risk factors. While the number of patients with no natural teeth remaining continues to fall, there are still many patients requiring complete dentures.
Incremental tooth loss is an issue that requires careful treatment planning and intensive use of prevention to ensure a functional natural dentition can be maintained.

### Evidence-Based Prevention in the Elderly

While Ettinger [8] and others have rightly called for pragmatic treatment planning for older patients, there is no requirement at all to limit the scope and nature of preventive treatments and therapies whether they are provided professionally or by the patient as part of a self-care plan. While the research base for effective preventive dental care is still dominated by interventions for children, there are sensible and relatively simple approaches that can be adopted for the older patient.
Fluoride remains the mainstay of preventive intervention for older patients and this can be delivered through a range of different methods. Incorporation of the fluoride ion in the community water supply remains the simplest and probably the most effective. Its incorporation into toothpaste is also very effective and patients of all ages must be encouraged to incorporate it into their daily routines. Regular topical applications by a dentist or hygienist become very important indeed as the patient ages, especially if there is a problem with alternate methods as described above.
What is more, professional application can be part of the routine removal of accumulated plaque and calculus that is likely to lead to periodontal problems as the patient’s dexterity declines.

### Pre-dependency / Less Independence

As the patient ages and becomes less competent, it becomes more important to consider additional tests such as a salivary flow rate assessment. This is often a hidden problem and the patient may well be oblivious to it and it is up to the dentist to check carefully for the problems this may cause. If the patient is wearing a partial denture or has undergone extensive reconstruction, there is greatly increased risk of caries and periodontal disease so an ­anti-microbial mouthrinse may be prescribed as well as a toothpaste with a higher fluoride content. Routine professional oral hygiene sessions become more important and this is often a suitable time for the application of a topical fluoride. The patient needs to be monitored effectively to ensure that the preventive programme is working.

### Medium Dependency

Regardless of chronological age, chronic health conditions may impact on oral health, exposing the patient to the risk of a significant decrease in oral health and the quality of life. It is important to maintain access to dental services under these circumstances and a support network may become necessary. Working with family and home-care workers will assist. A toothpaste with a high fluoride content is essential and attention needs to be given to the toothbrush selection to ensure that it can be handled properly. An electric toothbrush can be a valuable adjunct but the patient will require proper instruction in its application. An anti-microbial mouthrinse should be considered as well as periodic use of a topical fluoride. Regular professional oral hygiene care will help to maintain proper gingival health. Further treatment planning should focus on teeth of strategic importance, repairing rather than replacing failing restorations and, where possible, using a glass-ionomer cement because of its anti-caries merits. Keep in mind the importance of posterior support if the patient has lost teeth in the past. There is a need to maintain at least eight units of posterior support where a molar tooth can be counted as two units and a bicuspid as one unit. As long as the teeth are apposed to each other in the two arches, there will be sufficient support to maintain the occlusion without unduly overloading any pair of teeth and leading to loss of vertical dimension.

### High Dependency

Treatment of the patient with a high dependency problem poses difficulties for the general practitioner. While visiting the patient may be straightforward, the provision of any definitive treatment without the usual equipment may be very difficult.
However, a general dental practitioner should be able to provide relief of pain under most circumstances but, at the same time, it is essential to be aware of the medical complications the patient presents. Treatment may have to be delivered in the home setting and that will obviously limit what can be achieved. If the patient has attended the practice regularly for ongoing care, the new problems will hopefully be rather minor. The patient’s carers should be shown how to undertake normal oral hygiene procedures and it may be sensible to introduce an electric toothbrush. It may be necessary to use a chlorhexidine mouthwash product if optimal toothbrushing is not possible. Regular contact should be maintained with patients under these circumstances in an attempt to predict and anticipate problems and therefore keep them as a minor irritant at worst.

Discussion
These four hypothetical cases provide examples of how the transition through dependency needs to be matched with increasing intensity of prevention. The use of high fluoride toothpastes has been shown to be effective in the management of root caries as well as a clear dose response in relation to coronal caries.
The mix of professional and self-care needs to be considered in light of the patient’s ability to perform oral hygiene procedures.
Considering the levels of dependency, it will be crucial to implement effective prevention early in the ageing process to prevent issues developing. Effective communication with older patients is therefore essential. It must be acknowledged that they are an important group of patients with continuing needs for general dental services and they fall within the scope of practice of all competent dental care professionals.

Formal care pathways
While the case scenarios here are a useful means of considering the prevention and treatment options open to older patients, there is a need to formulate formal approaches to the care of dependent adults. Care pathways offer the opportunity to recognize individual patient needs, implement effective care programmes and monitor the outcomes in a predictable manner.
The development of pathways should be based on evidence from robust scientific studies. In the case of older patients, such studies are often complex and the literature is sparse, especially in relation to randomized clinical trials. However, the aetiology of the oral diseases is common across the age groups, as are the social determinants, and therefore a pragmatic approach is called for to ensure that effective pathway development is undertaken.

### Summary

The older patient is going to become an increasing proportion of any dental services’ case load. There are clear requirements for a comprehensive examination and assessment of such

patients and the results of these examinations should directly lead to the provision of prevention programmes involving selfcare as well as professional care. Treatment plans for interventions should consider the ageing process and be related to the ability of the patient to maintain themselves both now, and in the future. The focus should be on physiological rather than chronological age.
