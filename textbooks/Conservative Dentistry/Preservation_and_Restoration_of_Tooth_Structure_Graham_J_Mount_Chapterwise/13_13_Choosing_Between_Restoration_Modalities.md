## Choosing Between Restoration Modalities

G.J. Mount, M.S. Wolff & H.C. Ngo

## I

t is necessary to take a number of factors into account when selecting the restorative material to be placed for the restoration of any tooth. There are advantages and disadvantages associated with every material currently available. There is no one perfect material and there is not a single material that is suitable in every clinical circumstance. While there is considerable pressure at this time to use aesthetic restorative materials wherever possible, it must be acknowledged that a combination of factors may be applied in selecting a material. Longevity, cost effectiveness, clinical research evidence and the patient’s desires should all be guiding factors, and aesthetics alone should not be regarded as paramount. Patients and practitioners must make informed decisions utilizing the best evidence available at the time while making decisions that avoid creating permanent harm to the tooth.
Maintenance of original tooth structure should be the primary aim in treatment of the carious lesion, but as the lesion progresses and the cavity becomes larger, there comes a stage where sacrifice of further tooth structure is required to provide protection from occlusal load to ensure the integrity of the restored tooth. In view of the fact that all restorative

materials have a potential life span, ideally measured in decades rather than years, a considerable amount of a practitioner’s restorative work represents replacement of failing old restorations.
Generally, replacement becomes necessary because of either caries around restorations (CARs), failure of the previous restorative material, or fracture of remaining tooth structure. This is the reason why selection of the most conservative restorative technique possible, and the most effective preventive interventions should be applied when first restoring a tooth. The replacement of restorations inevitably results in the further loss and weakening of the remaining tooth structure. These factors will dictate the choice of the replacement material which must always also offer the greatest longevity possible.

### Introduction

There are at least six materials or combinations of materials that can be used to restore acquired defects in tooth structure. None of the materials should be regarded as ideal or universal. Each material will have advantages, disadvantages and limitations
[1]. Any of them can perform well for the whole of the patient’s remaining life, without the need for replacement. Any of them can perform badly, failing in a few years or less. Whether a particular material will serve for a patient’s life or for much less time will depend on two groups of variables.
The first group relates to whether or not the disease that caused the defect has been controlled. If it has been controlled, then the material chosen has the potential to last for many decades.
The dentist can and should contribute to success in controlling disease. The patient, or those who help care for the patient on a daily basis, must also help.
The second group of variables relate to how well the material is chosen and how well it is used clinically. The patient or those who help care for the patient may contribute to the choice of the material. The dentist can, and should, provide the best advice possible about the options available. How well each material is used clinically is entirely under the dentist’s control.
This chapter will focus primarily on three materials: glassionomer cement, silver amalgam and resin-based composite. These three are best suited to the restoration of relatively small defects in patients in whom the disease or diseases that have caused defects have been controlled. Each is used by direct placement into cavities, either alone or in combination.
Each can create excellent restorations that endure, if they are used well.
Three other restorative materials, or combinations of materials, will be described much more briefly. These are cast gold alloy alone; heat-fused or milled porcelain alone; or porcelain fused to a metal alloy by heat. Each of these is best suited to the restoration of large defects. They are fabricated outside of the mouth, and then cemented into place with either glassionomer cement or composite resin, both of which can adhere to the tooth and to the restoration if used well. These material combinations can also create excellent and enduring restorations, in the absence of ongoing disease. All can fail, rapidly, if the disease continues.
It is likely that the best outcomes for the long-term health and well-being of patients will be achieved when the disease that caused the acquitted defect is controlled either before or at the same time as any restoration is placed, and continues to be controlled thereafter; and when the placement of smaller restorations rather than larger ones is possible.
There is a degree of pressure from the dental industry, and sometimes from individual patients, to place tooth-coloured restorations as well as pressure to eliminate the use of dental

amalgam because it contains mercury. The selection of a material must consider the expected longevity of the restoration as a guiding factor as well as the conservation of tooth structure.
There is an ongoing need for continued scientific evaluation of the clinical performance of materials to test the bounds of longevity.

### Glass-Ionomer Restorative Materials

Advantages
Glass-ionomers are water-based cements that are stable in the oral environment. They are bioactive, so they are capable of exchanging calcium, strontium, fluoride and phosphate ions with tooth structure to develop and maintain an intimate physico-chemical union. They are of low solubility when used properly.
The materials contain fluoride which is released into the surrounding tooth structure after placement. There is a strong fluoride release initially that reduces over the first two months. Release has been shown still to be present over at least seven years [2]. The fluoride content can be continually recharged from topical applications from many sources including toothpaste and professionally applied fluids or gels.
As a result, the material appears to have a strong resistance to the development of recurrent caries at the interface with the tooth structure. The intimacy of the bond to enamel and dentine promotes remineralization of both, and there is an inhibitory effect from the fluoride release on bacteria in the biofilm.
The acid content of the glass-ionomer cement allows surface etching and adhesion to both dentine and enamel. The bond is weaker than the bond of modern composite resins to enamel, but the material performs well clinically, in terms of adhesion.
The intimate and strong bond to dentine seals well, preventing fluid flow through the dentine tubules, thereby reducing or eliminating post-operative sensitivity.
Glass-ionomer restorative cements have an acceptable degree of translucency and colour matching. Also, because of their adhesive properties, there is no need to modify the cavity design to develop mechanical interlocks for retention, so they are very conservative of natural tooth structure.
The restorative cements are available in both a light-activated form, also known as resin-modified or dual cure, as well as the original chemically activated or auto-cure system [3]. The dualcure mechanism was developed through the addition of resins, including HEMA and photoinitiators. The main advantage with these is improved resistance to water contamination immediately after light initiation. The original acid/base reaction, which allows the development of adhesion to the tooth, and is therefore the key to the glass-ionomer system, is still present but is protected by the umbrella-like presence of the dual-cure

resins. Dual-cure resins have been shown to decrease, but not negate, the fluoride release of the glass-ionomer system. They have an elastic modulus similar to dentine and as such are ideal for the restoration of both carious and non-carious cervical lesions.

Disadvantages
The main limitation with a glass-ionomer cement is a relatively low fracture and abrasion resistance. It cannot be used alone to withstand heavy occlusal load but will last well if supported by surrounding tooth structure. Glass-ionomer restoratives are therefore not suitable to rebuild marginal ridges or incisal corners [4].
Solubility is low and improves over time due to the long-term setting mechanism, but early exposure to water contamination will result in loss of physical properties. Effective surface sealing with a resin-based coating immediately after placement and the initial set will overcome this risk. On occasions, translucency in some chemically activated or autocure materials may not be sufficient for colour matching, and lamination with composite resin may be required for a satisfactory aesthetic result.
The creation of enduring glass-ionomer cement restorations requires a careful clinical technique, particularly relative to moisture control both during and after placement. Good moisture control using a rubber dam increases the chances of clinical success; at the time of placement, dentine should be moist, but should not be frankly wet or contaminated with saliva.
Effective surface sealing with a bonding resin or similar material immediately after initial set has proven to be very beneficial for the development of good aesthetics and long-term surface integrity.
A further important caution is that, as a water-based material, glass-ionomer cements remain at risk of dehydration if aggressively dried, particularly in the early hours after placement. They may also dehydrate and suffer surface disintegration over time in patients with very low saliva flow. For those with
Sjögren’s Syndrome, or similar salivary incompetence, it may be necessary to confine the use of glass-ionomers to replacement of lost dentine and to laminate over it with a resin-based material.

### Resin-Based Composite

Advantages
Resin-based composite restorations can have excellent aesthetics and can be built in multiple layers and shades. When the resin composite is placed incrementally, and properly lightactivated at each stage, variation in both colour and translucency can be incorporated, and anatomical form reproduced reasonably accurately. Significant development has been carried out in recent years on the filler particles incorporated within the resin and there is considerable variation between the products on the market [5]. Physical properties and translucency will be affected by the filler particle size and filler particle loading. This affects the material’s ability to be shaped and manipulated as well as the material’s ability to retain a smooth polished surface [6].
Properly placed, the physical properties of resin composite are sufficient to withstand moderate occlusal load throughout the mouth and the wear factor of the more heavily filled composites is sufficient for these restorations [7]. Relative to ceramic restorations, resin composite is inexpensive, but it has a potentially shorter potential lifespan [8, 9]. It is relatively simple to develop a micro-mechanical union between enamel and composite resins through applying an acidic etchant for a brief period. This is the strongest adhesion available in the oral cavity. Bonding to dentine is more difficult due both to dentinal fluid and a high collagen content, so different strategies must be used to achieve adhesion to that tissue. Modern dentine bonding agents require that the dentine be maintained moist, avoiding desication and therefore reducing the risk of post-operative sensitivity that can be severe.
Dentine bonding agents utilize strategies that promote adhesion to the collagen in dentine through the addition of hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA) in a hydrophilic carrier, such as ethanol or acetone, to facilitate its movement into the moist tubule.

Disadvantages
Placement techniques are extremely technique-sensitive and require a high degree of clinical skill. Alhough dentine bonding requires moist dentine, the bonding agent must not be applied in a frankly wet or saliva-soaked environment.
Resin composites, like all polymerizing plastics, contract during setting. The process of shrinkage has the net effect of producing stress at the adhesive interface and, if improperly placed, can pull the composite resin away from the cavity margins. This will lead to microleakage with the risk of pulpal inflammation and death. Careful incremental placement of the material will control the direction of shrinkage and minimize the total amount to within range of 1–2%. However, this may still be sufficient to place considerable stress on the bond of the restoration to both the tooth structure and a cement base [10]. Improper placement of resin composites can allow fluid movement through the margins leading to post-operative sensitivity.

### Amalgam

Advantages
Over many years, particularly in small restorations, amalgam has been shown to have a very satisfactory history of longevity. It is relatively easy to handle through standardized methods and placement techniques have become very routine. Amalgam is tolerant of less than ideal placement techniques, although over-contour, particularly in relation to the gingival tissues, can pose a challenge through plaque retention. Properly placed, amalgam has physical properties which are sufficient to withstand normal occlusal stresses. It is a very economical material and therefore cost-effective. Following placement, it will corrode quite rapidly in the oral environment and the corrosion products will seal the margins against microleakage within the first few days after placement [12].
This, therefore, makes it highly resistant to recurrent caries [13].

Disadvantages
The main disadvantage of amalgam is that it is aesthetically undesirable. The material itself is initially silver then declining to a dull grey in colour. As it corrodes, it darkens in itself and may also release metallic ions into the surrounding dentine, leading to a blue or grey discoloration in the remaining tooth structure.
Corrosion is therefore both an advantage and a disadvantage. In modern formulations with a high copper content, the corrosion potential has been reduced sufficiently to minimize the colour

### Gold

Advantages
There are three types of gold alloy of varying hardness and ductility that can be used for tooth restoration using indirect techniques. With the range of alloys available, it is possible to select a material suitable for restoration of almost any large defect. The wear rate of gold alloys can be very similar to that of natural tooth structure.
Careful laboratory casting techniques allow for very high accuracy in fit and the inherent strength and ductility of gold mean the material can be used in relatively thin sections to protect remaining tooth structure. Gold is a noble metal and does not corrode. With a highly polished surface, gold is relatively resistant to plaque formation and therefore shows a very high tissue tolerance and caries resistance.
One of the major advantages of cast gold restorations is that, when a restoration is produced by an indirect technique in the laboratory, it is possible to recreate both the occlusal and proximal surfaces to the full anatomic contours of the original tooth with a high degree of accuracy. This is more difficult to achieve with directly-placed restorative materials.

Disadvantages
The high cost of gold itself, and the additional costs in time and materials of indirect fabrication, make a cast gold restoration relatively expensive. Attempts to reduce the cost of alloys by replacing some gold with less expensive, non-precious metals involve compromises with increased hardness, less ductility, greater corrosion and greater difficulty in casting.
Cast gold alloy restorations constructed by indirect techniques involve complex laboratory procedures as well as rather lengthy chairside operations. They are, therefore, relatively expensive in the first instance although their longevity can justify the initial outlay. In addition, the multi-stage production routines allow the introduction of errors, requiring very high levels of skill both in the clinic and the laboratory. The error, or accumulation of errors, will often only be detected at the final insertion appointment and may require repetition of the entire procedure.
Cast gold restorations must be cemented or luted to place in order to create a seal against bacterial microleakage. They are therefore dependent on another material, a luting cement, for their clinical success and longevity. Glass-ionomer cements can serve well for decades in this role, but the potential for very long-term dissolution must be considered as a possible disadvantage.
In the current era of aesthetic dentistry, the appearance of a gold restoration becomes debatable. Many patients request more aesthetic materials.
Direct gold restorations can be placed in the form of gold foil, which is pure 24 carat gold leaf. The process involves taking small pellets of uncontaminated gold foil, placing the pellet in a very conservative retentive cavity and utilizing pressure or

malleting to cold weld the gold to a uniform mass. Historically, cohesive gold restorations were considered to be of very high quality. However, placement techniques are very demanding and time-consuming. They are also highly prone to microleakage in the absence of a sealing base material.

### Ceramics

Advantages
Ceramic restorations are produced by utilizing basically two technologies: a laboratory multi-step process creating fired or heat-fused porcelain and a computer-assisted design and manufacture process creating a restoration by milling from a block of pre-fired material [15].
The art and science of dental ceramics have reached a very high level and it is the material of choice for large aesthetic restorations. With careful attention to detail, ceramic crowns and veneers can defy detection by the naked eye. The well-finished glazed ceramic surface will not readily accumulate plaque and the anatomy of both the occlusal and the proximal surfaces can be reproduced on the laboratory bench with great accuracy.
Computer-aided design and fabrication of ceramic restorations by milling from a pre-fired block of high fracture-resistant ceramic (CADCAM) is reaching a level of quality where the fit, shape, aesthetic and durability of the cemented restoration can be excellent.
The initial cost to the patient of ceramic restorations is high but the outlay can be justified because of both superior aesthetics and potential longevity.

Disadvantages
The many stages involved in the production of fired ceramic restorations introduces a high potential for the introduction of errors and leads to the high cost. All stages of production in both the clinic and the laboratory must be carried out to the highest possible standard if success is to be assured. The initial preparation must be precise with clearly delineated margins wide enough to allow for a level of bulk in the finished

### Porcelain Fused to Metal

Advantages
Over 30 years ago techniques for fusing porcelein onto metals were adapted to dentistry. Various gold alloys, as well as other combinations using non-precious metals, were subject to investigation to the extent that the combination of porcelain fused to metal overcame the limitations of using porcelain alone.
Careful laboratory casting techniques for the metal core evolved and these allowed for very high accuracy in fit and improved physiological contours. The limitations of allporcelain bridgework were overcome but the brittle nature of the porcelain remained the major limitation. The skilled technician was able to build extensive reconstructions of the dentition with both strength and a high level of aesthetics.

### Factors Governing the Selection

### of a Restorative Material

Taking into account the advantages and disadvantages of the available restorative materials, as listed above, the following rationale can be applied in any given situation with the materials discussed in order of preference.

Restoration and maintenance of physical properties of a tooth
As discussed in the Introduction, there is no one ideal material for all circumstances.
Glass-ionomer cement is the material of choice for restoration of small defects because of its ion exchange adhesion and fluoride release. The cement will enhance remineralization of demineralized tooth structure. However, glass-ionomer is relatively brittle and requires a reasonable level of support.
The available adhesion will restore some of the lost physical properties but the cement itself has a relatively low tensile strength and cannot therefore offer a significant reinforcement to remaining tooth structure. The material also has relatively low wear resistance when placed in areas of high abrasion.
Glass-ionomer cement is the material of choice for an corrosion lesion or for any lesion where the occlusal surface is not involved. If the load is expected to be too great, it can be laminated with composite resin, and this combination will restore reasonable strength and aesthetics to the remaining tooth structure. When used in this way, glass-ionomer cement may compensate to some degree for the risk of microleakage under composite resins.
Both amalgam and composite resin restorations can serve well as intracoronal restorations. Because of shrinkage, composite resin serves best in smaller restorations, and success becomes less certain as the restoration size increases. Neither material has any immediate resistance to further plaque accumulation or caries, so it is critical to maintain good oral hygiene. The more extensive lesions may need to be extended

Adhesion with resins is only as strong as the tensile strength of the component parts of the system. Neither composite resin, resin bonding agents nor enamel are consistently strong in tension. Cavity modifications and an acid etch union between enamel and resin can provide a degree of protection to weakened cusps, but not substantial reinforcement. With careful attention to detail, it is possible to restore aesthetics with composite resin. Conservative cavity preparations can be developed but the integrity of the margin depends entirely on the availability of sound, well-supported enamel to be etched and bonded [17].
Cast gold may be a good choice in some circumstances because it can be placed in thin section to protect and reinforce remaining tooth structure. It is most frequently constructed using indirect techniques, so it is possible to reconstruct the ideal contour and anatomy of a tooth crown and rebuild occlusion with a high degree of accuracy [18, 19]. Its use is limited by expense, demand for precision, time to construct and the aesthetic result.
Ceramic restorations also are built by indirect techniques and it is therefore possible to re-create anatomy and occlusion with a high degree of accuracy. They can now be designed and constructed using a prefabricated block of porcelain in a computer-assisted system. Ceramic materials are generally too brittle to be used with confidence in thin section and therefore more sound tooth structure must be removed to allow sufficient room for the restorative material, especially under occlusal load.
Ceramic is the material of choice for restoration of some large lesions because it is possible to simulate the original tooth in colour, translucency and character with a high degree of accuracy. However, in many cases, it is necessary to be relatively destructive of remaining tooth structure to provide adequate thickness for the porcelain material.
With the development of reliable adhesion to sound enamel using resin bonding techniques, it has become possible to produce ceramic veneers to restore the labial surfaces of anterior teeth with relatively minor removal of enamel. As with all restorations, care must be taken to avoid over-contouring as this may compromise gingival health.

Choice of restorative material according to site and size of lesion
Natural tooth structure is the best defence against further caries. It can be remineralized by saliva and strengthened against further acid attack using fluoride and the patient can be educated in dietary and hygiene procedures. Remineralized tooth structure is as hard or harder than the original tooth and is more resistant to further caries attack. Therefore, even though the surface may be mildly disfigured and stained, the most

conservative approach to cavity design should be adopted. The following recommendations are offered as a rational approach to the choice of cavity design and the subsequent selection of the restorative material [20, 21].

### Site 1 lesion: pits and fissures on smooth surface

• Newly erupted immature tooth with deep fissures: place a protective fissure seal using an unfilled resin or a glass-ionomer cement. No instrumentation will be required.
• Mature tooth with small carious dentine involvement or moderate size cavity, with no undue occlusal load. Seal the pit and remaining fissure to remove the lesion from access to nutrients and bacteria. If surgical intervention is necessary, excavation of the caries should be performed as conservatively as possible. Fissures should be opened only when there is evidence of further caries. Restore with glass-ionomer cement and laminate with composite resin as required. The remaining fissures should be sealed at the same time to reduce the possibility of microleakage.
• Moderate to large lesions in posterior teeth with extensive occlusal involvement. Open the lesion conservatively and excavate the caries around the walls with care. Fissures should be opened and extended only when there is evidence of further caries. Restore with glass-ionomer on the floor and laminate with composite resin. Any remaining fissures should be sealed at the same time to reduce the possibility of microleakage. If, due to occlusal load the survival of a composite resin restoration is in question, restore with amalgam over a glass-ionomer base using a protective cavity design where required to prevent further breakdown.

### Site 2 lesion: contact area, posterior

• New lesion just involving dentine. Lesions not penetrating the dentine can be managed non-surgically. This will include dietary management, oral hygiene instruction, use of a fluoride toothpaste, office- and home-applied supplemental fluoride. When the lesion has penetrated into the dentine and beyond the dentino-enamel junction, surgical intervention will be required using a conservative technique. There may be no need to break facial or lingual contact with the adjacent tooth. Caries should be removed and fissures preserved and sealed during restoration. A tunnel preparation preserving the marginal ridge while removing the carious lesion and restoring with glass-ionomer cement will be a most conservative approach. Minimal slot preparations, restored with composite resin, is a useful alternative.
If the lesion extends subgingivally and the margins cannot be adequately maintained, a glass-ionomer base laminated by composite resin or amalgam may be a useful alternative.
Tight contact and an appropriate contour of the embrasure space are critical.

• Larger lesion with marginal ridge involved. Use a conservative modified cavity design. A glass-ionomer lining cement may be placed in the deepest aspects of these lesions. An appropriate composite resin can then be placed over the lining cement using a dentine bonding agent. Amalgam and composite resin restorations are a challenge to contour when used as direct restorations. Composite resin and ceramic restorations can be bonded to enamel with minimal additional mechanical design. Direct restoration with amalgam is usually the primary choice. Gold or ceramic extra-coronal, indirectly fabricated restorations, must be considered to achieve correct contours.

### Site 2 lesion: contact area, anterior

• Initial lesion. Lesions not penetrating the dentine may be managed non-surgically. This will include dietary management, oral hygiene instruction with a fluoride toothpaste, and office- and home-applied supplemental fluoride as part of the initial therapy. Once the lesion has penetration into the dentine and beyond, the dentino-enamel junction surgical intervention is required. On some occasions, the lesion may be entered from the facial or lingual surface to minimize the loss of tooth and composite resin with a bonding agent will be the preferred choice for aesthetic reasons.
• Larger lesions or replacement restorations. Further preparations should remove as little tooth structure as possible. Glass-ionomer lining cements may be placed in the deepest aspects, and composite resin restorations, with dentine bonding agents, can be placed over the lining cement. Tight contacts and appropriate contours of the embrasure spaces are critical.

### Site 2 lesion: involving incisal corner

• Small initial lesion. A relatively small lesion will generally be the result of trauma in this classification. Injuries to the tooth, sufficient to cause an enamel fracture, can cause damage to the pulp that may be irreversible. The patients should be cautioned to watch for the tooth becoming sensitive to hot, cold or percussion and seek professional dental care if those signs appear. If the enamel only is involved, it may be sufficient to bevel the enamel and restore with a bonded composite resin.
If there is any dentine involvement, the dentine may be protected with a glass-ionomer cement first, before lamination with composite resin, to provide pulp protection and adhesion to the dentine.
• Larger lesions or replacement restorations. Restoration of teeth with extensive damage along the incisal edge is difficult in two respects. The amount of surface area for bonding is likely to be limited and the occlusal forces due to eating, chewing and habits could easily exceed restoration bond strengths. In addition, the health of the pulp may already be compromised. The dentine immediately above the pulp, or the exposed pulp itself, must be covered and protected as soon as possible. Direct covering of an exposed pulp with calcium hydroxide has been demonstrated to give the pulp the best chance of survival, but if there is no exposure, the dentine should be protected with a glass-ionomer lining cement. The remaining enamel margin can then be subjected to a 2 mm bevel, etched, and laminated with composite resin.

### Site 3 lesion: cervical margin

• Erosion/abrasion lesion. The care of the erosion lesion should begin by addressing the risk and causative factors initially.
This may include the reduction of acid from food intake, or medical management of problems associated with gastric acids, such as bulimia and gastric esophageal reflux disease.
This consultation should include both timing and application of toothbrushing technique. Low pH food intake, followed immediately by a scrubbing action with a tooth brush can be the original cause in many cases and these habits need to be eliminated. The generation of these lesions may be stabilized non-surgically with fluoride and other remineralizing therapies (see Chapter 5). When restoration of these lesions becomes necessary for aesthetics, or loss of strength in the tooth crown, a Type II.1 restorative aesthetic glass-ionomer cement or dentin bonded hybrid composite may be utilized. No instrumentation is required and the lesion should be cleaned with pumice and water only to remove the pellicle before being conditioned with an acid for 10 seconds, washed carefully and restored. The resin-modified glassionomers are generally aesthetically satisfactory. These lesions may also be successfully restored with a dentine bonded micro-hybrid composite resin material.

• Carious lesion or replacement restoration. The cavity should be instrumented only as far as necessary to remove active caries. Mechanical retention is unnecessary and demineralized enamel may remain unless badly undermined. Glass-ionomer cement or bonded micro-hybrid composite material may be utilized to restore these lesions. These materials have similar modulous of elasticity to dentine and clinical trials have suggested a similar longevity to the glass-ionomers.

Extracoronal restorations
• Porcelain or composite resin laminate veneers. A laminate veneer can be regarded as a relatively conservative method of modifying the shape or aesthetics of an anterior tooth.
As at least half the thickness of the labial enamel must be removed to avoid over-contour, such a restoration should not be regarded as reversible. It must be noted that, as it is almost impossible to know the thickness of remaining enamel, the labial surface should not be cut back to an arbitrary depth for fear of inadvertently removing all enamel and leaving a bond to dentine only. This applies particularly to the gingival margin where it is most difficult to achieve a good bond to dentine.
Composite resin may be the preferred material for minor modifications to the shape of one or two teeth only but indirectly built porcelain veneers are recommended for extensive modification of multiple teeth for the one patient.
• Partial coverage crown or onlay.This is the most conservative of the extracoronal restorations and is valuable for reinforcing posterior teeth in particular. The selection of the best material is determined by occlusal stresses and aesthetics. Where aesthetics is not an issue, gold may be the best material to use because it can be cast with a high degree of accuracy and used in very thin section. Other castable alloys may be a suitable alternative. Skilful cavity preparation will minimize the gold display and in many cases the aesthetic result is entirely acceptable. Indirect and CAD-CAM fabricated porcelain and composite onlays may be of value to produce suitable aesthetic restorations but require a greater sacrifice of natural tooth structure. Milled porcelain restorations now provide greater fracture resistance than fired porcelain, allowing less bulk and therefore greater conservation of structure. If cost is a problem, and the practitioner skills allow, a cusp-replacing amalgam restoration will provide the most cost-effective restoration.
• Full crown. A full coverage restoration may be required where either the remaining tooth structure is so badly broken down that no other method will adequately restore the tooth or it is necessary to upgrade aesthetics or occlusion [22]. If aesthetics is of no concern, cast gold is the material of choice because the remaining tooth structure can be conserved to a greater degree and the occlusal wear factor will be similar to the opposing tooth.

If aesthetics requires a tooth-coloured outcome, the best alternatives will be porcelain fused to metal or an all-ceramic crown. Construction of either will probably require removal of a greater amount of tooth structure than has already been lost. In the case of porcelain fused to metal, retentive features must be developed. With the all-ceramic approach, bonding between the ceramic and tooth structure with a resin cement can reduce the need to develop retentive features, but the requirement for bulk in the crown itself to provide both fracture resistance and aesthetics may necessitate the removal of additional tooth structure.
Existing restorations should be fully upgraded first. Glassionomer cement and bonded composite resin may be sufficient to restore small to moderate deficiencies in the tooth structure prior to tooth preparation.
