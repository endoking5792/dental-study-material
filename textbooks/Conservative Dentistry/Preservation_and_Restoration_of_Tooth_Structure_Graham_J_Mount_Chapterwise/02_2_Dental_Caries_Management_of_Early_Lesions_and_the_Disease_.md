## S

everal distinctly different diseases can cause the loss of tooth structure.
Among these, dental caries has been the most common in many of the world’s societies in recent centuries. In describing the relationships between the management of diseases of the crown of the tooth, the management of early lesions and the approach to restoration of more advanced defects in teeth, it is therefore reasonable to begin with a focus on caries. The principles that apply to caries apply also in general terms to the other diseases, as will be evident in the chapters that follow.
There are some significant differences in approach between the three major sites at which carious lesions occur. They will therefore be described separately. This chapter describes how to manage early carious lesions at various sites and how to manage the disease that causes such lesions.

### Development of Carious Lesions

Dental caries is the progressive demineralization of the mineralized tissues of teeth, initiated by unique biochemical circumstances within bacterial biofilm on the tooth surface. Carious lesions begin in locations where certain physical and biochemical circumstances coincide:
• The physical circumstances obtain where biofilm, also called bacterial plaque, accumulates, remains relatively undisturbed for several days or more and in which the bacterial content is changed and sustained by sucrose.
• The biochemical circumstances relate to acid production, caused by bacteria within the thickening biofilm that mature and metabolize sucrose and other sugars in food and drink.
This will effectively lower the pH and, when acid retention in the biofilm is sufficient in amount and frequency, it will cause demineralization of the subjacent enamel and dentine at a rate that outweighs the normal capacity for remineralization. A carious lesion then develops.
Carious lesions follow a spectrum of severity that begins with evidence of minimal mineral loss initially, followed by loss of surface integrity, and, finally, cavitation in enamel and subsequently dentine. These physical and biochemical circumstances are generally created at characteristic sites on tooth surfaces due to a combination of:
• the physical retention of thick biofilm at such sites for several days or more;
• limited local salivary access due to local tooth shape in fissures and pits;
• limited local salivary access due to the presence of an adjacent tooth;
• limited local salivary access due to adjacent soft tissue contour;
• pathological limitation of saliva generation and flow.
There are three sites where carious lesions most commonly begin, as shown in Figure 2.1. They are listed below in order of frequency of incidence in whole populations, the first being the most common and the third the least. For reasons that will become evident, in some individuals, the second or third sites may be more common sites for caries initiation than the first.
1	 In fissures, pits or other surface defects on the tooth crown where, even with good oral hygiene, thick biofilm accumulates relatively undisturbed and where salivary access is limited.
2	 Approximating tooth surfaces beneath the contact areas between two teeth. That is, areas where thick biofilm can accumulate relatively undisturbed, and where salivary access is limited.
3	 In the cervical regions of teeth adjacent to the gingival tissue, on facial, lingual, mesial or distal surfaces, on either enamel or the exposed root surface following gingival recession.
Thick biofilm may accumulate in these areas and remain relatively undisturbed, and salivary access is limited.

There are fundamental differences between the physical circumstances of each of these three general sites. It is therefore appropriate to describe the approach to the management of early lesions for each site in turn.

### Site 1: Fissures, Pits and Other Defects

### on Tooth Surfaces

The reason why fissures, pits and other defects are the commonest sites for caries initiation is that thick biofilm accumulates within them and is not removed either by chewing food or by any routine oral hygiene procedures. Where the inclines of two cusps come close together within a fissure, there will be an area where biofilm on one cusp meets that on another. In that area, the biofilm is twice as thick as it would be on a simple, smooth surface. This double biofilm thickness, combined with the limited access for salivary movement within fissures, is what places fissure enamel at greater risk for development of carious lesions.
The internal geometry of an enamel pit is even more conducive to risk, for similar reasons.
In modern populations who eat food of low abrasivity, wear on the coronal surface is limited and fissure systems can remain throughout life. If the biochemical circumstances are unfavourable, demineralization will occur in the enamel, followed by the dentine subjacent to the fissure walls.

Anatomy of the fissure system
Fissures form during calcification of the crown of the tooth. Within the tooth bud, calcification commences at the tip of the cusps and, as the cusps grow, they fuse together to some degree at the completion of the occlusal surface of the crown. Fusion will not always be complete and in a high percentage of cases there will be defects within the area of fusion, ranging from single deep pits to extended

Early stages of the Site 1 carious lesion:
intact surface
In the earliest stage of a carious lesion, there is no cavitation, that is, no breakdown on the outer tooth surface. The enamel surface is essentially intact macroscopically but there will be sub‐surface demineralization. This sub‐surface demineralization may extend through much or all of the thickness of enamel before the surface breaks down.

Be Aware

It is important, for all sites, to distinguish between an initial carious defect (i.e. sub‐surface demineralization, with an intact surface; non‐cavitated) and a carious cavity (i.e. a frank loss of tooth structure; surface breakdown and cavitation). These two lesions may be managed in very different ways. This circumstance is clearly evident in histological sections of teeth.

Detection of early lesions
For the early lesion to be detected clinically by visual examination, the tooth must be cleaned and dried. With good illumination and, ideally, with magnification such as a clinical microscope, the enamel surface will appear to be intact but there may be a change in appearance, most commonly an increase in whiteness, or opacity (see Figures 2.2 and 2.3).

While sensory input from touch, using an appropriate instrument, can be helpful in understanding the nature and status of a pit or a fissure system, compressive force with a sharp instrument must be avoided. Direct compressive force applied through a sharp instrument, such as an ‘explorer’, may result in collapse and irreparable damage to the partially demineralized enamel surface. A light scratching motion over the enamel surface with a less‐than‐sharp explorer or fine periodontal probe may reveal a
‘roughened’ surface by touch, indicating partial demineralization.
For much of the past hundred years it was common practice for dentists, during the examination of teeth, to force a sharp, pointed instrument into pits or fissures to feel whether the explorer tip would ‘catch’ or ‘stick’, that is, become difficult to remove. This was taken to be evidence of the presence of an initial carious lesion. It most cases this was probably so, and occurred because the enamel surface, weakened by sub‐surface demineralization, collapsed or fractured as a result of the force applied. The sharp instrument then jammed into the resultant defect. By this action, further damage was caused, ensuring the need for placement of a restoration. This technique cannot now be justified as part of an examination.
In addition to vision, using incident light and low‐pressure touch, several new techniques are available to increase the accuracy of detection of Site 1 carious lesions:
• Intense transillumination with closely focused, whole spectrum, high intensity light, delivered through a fibre‐optic system, allows visualization of changes in enamel density through light scattering and changes in colour. Early carious lesions appear more opaque than intact enamel because of decreased mineral, and therefore, increased water content. More advanced lesions appear darker because of bacterial ingress. Careful observation over a period of several seconds, while moving both the light source and mirror, allows development of a three‐dimensional appreciation of the fissure system.
• Laser fluorescence uses a laser light source of fixed wavelength.
Such light passes through intact enamel essentially unchanged.

Pit and fissure sealing
Sealing pits and fissures is a very effective and economical way of reducing the risk of caries in these systems. It is also a safe and effective component of treatment for early carious lesions in those sites, in combination with ongoing measures to limit demineralization and promote remineralization.
It is very difficult to determine with certainty whether or not early carious lesions exist within the enamel walls of pits and fissures so the procedure of fissure sealing is justifiable with any patient at risk at any age (see Figures 2.4–2.7).

Fissure sealing halts the progress of the disease at that site because it changes the local biochemistry, preventing access of simple sugar substrate to any bacteria that may remain in the biofilm within the sealed fissure. It is a logical, ethical and effective preventive treatment and has been used for many years with a long history of success. Pit or fissure sealing is appropriate in any circumstance where there is the perception of risk of development of caries, or where the presence of early carious lesions is suspected or known.
Fissure sealing is desirable in any patient with a high risk of caries development. Caries risk assessment is described in detail in Chapter 3. In modern societies, all young children should be considered at risk of developing caries, principally because immature enamel is relatively soluble. It takes several years in the oral environment, and significant contact with fluoride, for enamel to mature. Fissure sealing is relatively simple to perform and the cost is low, so there is no reason for failing to provide this service to all children, and to any other patient at risk.
Many materials and techniques, ranging from silver nitrate precipitation to melting of enamel with lasers, have been used to seal fissures. The most popular sealant over recent decades has been resin in various forms [1], and now glass‐ionomer cements are also proving to be of value.

### Resin sealants

Resin, either unfilled or lightly filled, was originally used over
30 years ago and was demonstrated to be safe and effective in well‐conducted clinical trials. It now has a long history of success. There are resin materials now available in both a light‐ cured or an auto‐cured form and many are tinted for easier identification. The aim is to flow the resin into the depths of the fissure to obtund it completely so preventing the future admission of more plaque and bacteria.
Prior to placement, the enamel should be cleaned, then acid‐ treated or etched to remove residual biofilm, reduce the surface energy of the enamel and dissolve the outer layer of the enamel rods. This induced porosity will allow penetration of unfilled resin into the spaces leading to micro‐mechanical attachment of the resin. Unfortunately, the walls of a fissure are covered with an amorphous layer of enamel rods, as described in Chapter 1, and they may not accept a good etch pattern. This means that, in some patients, attachment of the cured resin may be tenuous.
The presence of biofilm can interfere with the infiltration of resin into the depths of a fissure. It has been suggested that the occlusal surface should be well cleaned prior to placement but it is apparent that most methods of cleaning will only add to the debris trapped in the opening to the fissure. Also, it is apparently not possible to flow the resin into the fissure beyond the point where the fissure is less than 200 μm wide, so complete penetration is unlikely.
Loss of resin sealant may occur through partial lifting or fracture, with consequent exposure of one segment or another of the fissure. It must be noted that a resin sealant has no antibacterial properties. In spite of the possible limitations, the

clinical outcomes of resin fissure sealing have been shown to be very good.

### Glass‐ionomer cement sealants

Glass‐ionomer cement is also a very effective protection material for open fissures although there has not been a great deal of long‐term research published to date [2, 3]. Its value lies in the fact that it is possible to develop adhesion to the enamel, both normal and amorphous, through the ion exchange mechanism. In addition, it has a continuing ion exchange with the environment [4] and the subjacent tooth structure. The same limitations apply, as described above for a resin seal, in as much as it will not flow into a fissure beyond the point where the fissure is 200 μm wide and neither is it possible to completely clean a fissure without the use of cutting instruments (see Figures 2.8–2.13).

Prior to placement of a glass‐ionomer sealant, condition the tooth surface with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds, wash thoroughly with water only and dry lightly. This will remove most biofilm and reduce the surface energy of the enamel to allow the cement to adapt readily and develop a good adhesion.
A fast‐setting, high‐strength, Type II.2 auto‐cure restorative cement should be mixed with a high powder/liquid ratio, and flowed into the fissure. The tip of a gloved finger, very lightly lubricated, makes an excellent matrix to apply pressure to the cement to ensure maximum penetration into the fissure.
Pressure should be maintained for 3 minutes until the cement has set.
The use of a low powder content, physically weak, glass‐ionomer material is contraindicated because its limited compressive strength and wear resistance do not allow such cements to last for long. Despite this, it has been shown that, even though the cement is lost, the caries activity is reduced because of the fluoride uptake into the enamel. Also there may be a glass‐ionomer cement residue left in the entrance to the fissure.
Ongoing caries control is required to stop the further progress of the lesion, and to reverse it where possible, by behavioural and biochemical means.

Recommended Management

Recommended management for early fissure lesions is therefore by elimination of future plaque accumulation by fissure sealing, combined with ongoing caries control strategies.

Be Aware

Change the local circumstance by doing the following:
•  	Effective, daily biofilm removal in sites where that is possible, noting that such removal is not possible in pits and fissures.
•  	Changing the whole‐mouth demin‐remin balance to favour remineralization.

The change in the demin–remin balance can be achieved by
•  	reducing sucrose intake, which limits both the thickness and the gel nature of biofilm;
•  	reducing eating and drinking frequency, which reduces the proportion of time during which demineralization occurs relative to remineralization;

•  	additional remineralization aids, with fluoride as the first choice.
Restoration of a lesion is not caries treatment: it is defect repair.

Slightly more advanced pit and fissure lesions:
initial breakdown of the surface
The presence of more advanced lesions in pits or fissures requires visual examination of the dried tooth surface using magnification, possibly with a clinical microscope, to reveal minor surface defects in some part of the fissure system. At this point there may also be sub‐surface demineralization extending into dentine, as evidenced by a change in colour or an increase in radiolucency.
The area of loss of dentinal structure will be small enough that there is very little or no risk that the integrity of remaining tooth structure will be compromised under occlusal load.

Recommended Management

•  	Localized debridement of the area involved, with hand or rotary instruments
•  	Restoration of the resultant small defect with either a composite resin or glass‐ionomer cement seal
•  	Elimination of future plaque accumulation by sealing the remaining fissure system with either a resin or glass‐ionomer cement sealant
These procedures are described in detail and illustrated in Chapter 7.

Because the presence of a lesion demonstrates that the patient is caries active, these actions should be combined with ongoing caries control strategies. There are strong data showing that this approach, which is very conservative of tooth structure, provides excellent care outcomes.

Larger lesions: moderate involvement of dentine, as evidenced by change of colour and/or radiolucency
As lesions extending into dentine progressively increase in size, they will leave areas of unsupported enamel that may be at risk of fracture or collapse from occlusal forces.
The form of the cavity will need to be modified to ensure that the strength of the restored tooth is not compromised, taking into account occlusal factors. The principles behind such modification of cavity form are described in detail in Chapter 7.

Recommended Management

•  	Ongoing caries control strategies
•  	Conservative debridement of softened dentine (see Chapter 12)
•  	Restore with an appropriate restorative material over a base of glass‐ionomer cement (see Chapter 7)

### Site 2: Tooth Surfaces below the Contact

### Area between Teeth (Approximal Surfaces)

The area beneath the contact between two teeth is the second most common site for caries initiation. Biofilm accumulates there and is difficult to remove, even using a toothbrush. Daily flossing or the use of interdental brushes or wooden picks will disturb the biofilm, and their use should be more common.
If the biofilm remains undisturbed over time, and if there is low salivary access and frequent contact with simple carbohydrates, demineralization will occur in the enamel immediately adjacent to the thickest parts of the biofilm.

Early stages of the carious lesion: intact surface
Initially, the enamel surface will appear intact, but there may be sub‐surface demineralization (loss of calcium and phosphate content) involving the enamel only, or enamel and dentine.
In young patients the lesion will usually be about 1 millimetre below the contact area where the gingival crest is high because that is where plaque is thicker than it would be on a simple, smooth surface. Also salivary access for buffering and dilution of bacterial acid and dilution of substrate is limited in these areas. Thus, conditions favouring demineralization develop readily at that site (see Figures 2.14–2.17).
In older individuals where gingival recession has occurred, lesions tend to begin just beneath the contact because that is where plaque is thickest. The risk is greatest when salivary flow is compromised by either drug use or pathology.
The presence of an initial subsurface lesion may be determined from radiographs because of increased radiolucency in the subsurface tissue. This may be confirmed by trans‐illumination in some cases.

Initial surface breakdown: next stage of development of the approximal carious lesion
It is important to distinguish between an initial carious defect (sub‐surface demineralization with an intact surface) and a carious cavity (a frank loss of surface tooth structure). A carious cavity forms when there is small break in the enamel surface.
This may not be evident radiographically, but the passage of dental floss or tape may show the abrasive action of the sharp edges of the lesion. Alternatively use the impression method described above.
The presence of a surface defect substantially increases the risk for caries progression because biofilm within the defect is further removed from salivary access and buffering.
As is the case with early lesions in pit and fissure systems, the progress of the disease can be halted by surface sealing, combined with ongoing caries control. ### Site 3: Carious Lesions in Enamel or Exposed

### Root Surface in Areas of the Tooth Crown

### Adjacent to the Gingiva

This section describes the management of active caries in the cervical regions of the tooth crown, whether beginning in enamel or on exposed root surfaces. The management of lesions in these and other areas from causes other than caries is described in Chapter 4.
There are many similarities in the restoration of advanced defects in cervical regions of teeth from any cause, whether carious or non‐carious. The restorative phase for lesions of all causes in these areas is described in Chapter 7.
Site 3 lesions are the least common because routine oral hygiene procedures using toothbrush and toothpaste remove the causative biofilm, and salivary access is good relative to circumstances occurring at Site 1 and Site 2. Nevertheless, such lesions do occur, particularly in patients who fail to apply proper oral hygiene practices.

Early stages of the carious lesion in enamel
The enamel surface may appear visually intact but there may be sub‐surface demineralization involving enamel only, leading to an increase in enamel opacity. This is called a ‘white spot lesion’.
Because of good visual access, on facial surfaces in particular, both the presence of the initial lesion and its reversal can easily be observed. On facial surfaces and elsewhere, changes in surface texture can also be felt using light touch and a fine‐tipped metal or plastic instrument.

Recommended Management

•  	Disease control and ongoing remineralization only, reversing the ‘white spot’ either partially or completely

Activity level can be determined by serial observation over weeks or months noting changes in opacity and surface texture from rough to smooth.
Surgical treatment is neither necessary nor ethical. Outcomes of this approach are excellent because, as is the case with remineralized Site 2 sub‐surface lesions, the remineralized enamel surface is of very low solubility.

Initial enamel surface breakdown
Lesions beginning with a small break in the enamel surface, as well as sub‐surface demineralization involving enamel and dentine.

Recommended Management

•  	Ongoing remineralization and disease control
•  	Surface sealing or very conservative restoration with an adhesive material to re‐create a smooth surface and prevent further plaque accumulation
Clinical procedures for the repair of more advanced defects, where enamel breakdown has occurred, are described in Chapter 7.

Early stages of the carious lesion on an exposed root surface
Site 3 lesions can also begin on root surfaces that have become exposed to the oral environment through gingival recession.
The dynamics of mineral loss beneath acid‐producing plaque on exposed root surfaces are very different from that beneath plaque on enamel. On the cementum–dentine surface there is no equivalent of the ‘white spot’ lesion, which is the optical effect of subsurface demineralization of enamel. Instead, the collagen matrix of the root structure loses mineral, becoming increasingly soft as demineralization progresses, but there is not always a colour change.
The lesion may be detected through loss of hardness on the exposed root surface and this is best assessed with a blunt instrument such as a small ball burnisher. Alternatively, there may be modification of colour, usually darkening. Colour change follows diffusion into softened dentine of extrinsic stains from bacteria or other sources.
Because the early lesion can be reversed by remineralization (see below), sharp instruments should not be used to test the surface, because inserting a probe into demineralized dentine will irreversibly damage it.
Early demineralization of the root surface can be reversed by purely biochemical means, provided that the collagen matrix remains intact. Maintain serial observation of the surface hardness over the following weeks or months. As with enamel, it is not essential to remineralize the full depth of the lesion but this will halt progression.

Recommended Management

•  	Ongoing disease control and remineralization only, to re‐form a fully mineralized tooth surface

If a colour change has occurred, it may be necessary to consider some form of aesthetic improvement, although this is often not necessary. A remineralized hard, but discoloured, root surface can be monitored for continuing hardness over time. It will be a useful indicator of continued, overall, oral hard tissue health, and the opportunity for such monitoring is lost if lesions, of even moderate size, are unnecessarily restored.

There is a significant body of evidence that caries in the outer one‐third of dentine can be sealed and remain stable for many years, with effective non‐surgical disease management.
The presence of demineralization in the outer third of dentine should only be treated surgically if the tooth surface cannot be directly visualized and monitored. Aesthetics may be a rationale for restoration placement in some instances, but plaque retention is probably not. Once remineralized, such lesions rarely transform into softened caries and are most commonly found in association with exceptional gingival health

Substantially larger carious lesions of the root surface
When the structural integrity of demineralized dentine has been lost, or when the shape of the tooth surface has been sufficiently altered that removal of plaque with normal oral hygiene measures becomes difficult or impossible, restorative intervention becomes necessary. Restorative procedures for such lesions are described in Chapter 7.

Recommended Management

•  	Ongoing elimination of cause and remineralization
•  	Conservative debridement and placement of an adhesive restorative material
