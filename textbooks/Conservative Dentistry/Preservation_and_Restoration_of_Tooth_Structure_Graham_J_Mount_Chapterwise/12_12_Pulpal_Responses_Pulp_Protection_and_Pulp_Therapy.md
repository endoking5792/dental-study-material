### Dentine and Pulp

Dentine is a partially mineralized collagen and mucopolysaccaride matrix, fully traversed by permeable tubules that contain odontoblastic cellular processes and tissue fluid. Odontoblasts form the outer layer of a unique connective tissue, the dental pulp, which is rich in blood vessels and sensory nerves. In the mature, intact tooth, pulpal tissue is almost entirely enclosed in dentine.
The presence of dentinal tubules and their connection with the pulp make dentine and pulp a single biological unit, reasonably described as the dentine‐pulp complex. Because of this intimate connection, physical, chemical and bacterial insults to dentine are also insults to the pulp. Because of this close association, any physical or chemical therapy applied to the dentine is also applied to the pulp.
Insults such as the caries process and tooth restoration are unlike those found elsewhere in the body. It is not surprising, therefore, that some aspects of the pulpal response to insult are unique. Some therapies used to treat the dental pulp are also unique.

### Causes of Damage to Dentine and Pulp

The pulp can be damaged in one of three ways – caries, bacterial microleakage, or mechanical, thermal or chemical trauma.

Dentinal caries
Most commonly, the dental pulp can become inflamed and may die as a consequence of the advance of the caries process through dentine [1]. The causative agents are probably bacterial metabolic or cell breakdown products. Since the micro‐flora of dentinal caries is highly variable in composition and in bacterial numbers or load, it is not surprising that the pulpal inflammatory response is also highly variable. The process can be halted by effective caries treatment or through the application of preventive measures, either alone or in combination with tooth restoration.

Microleakage in restored teeth
The restoration of a caries defect in tooth structure is usually in the best interests of the patient’s health and well‐being. However, the action of restoration may not, in itself, be sufficient to prevent the ultimate death of the pulp, over time, even when carried out in parallel with effective caries preventive measures. The principal reason for pulpal inflammation following restoration is microleakage, the existence of a gap between the restorative material and the dentine, in which bacteria can propagate. As with dentinal carious lesions, the bacterial population beneath a leaking restoration is likely to be highly variable, and so, therefore, is the pulpal response.

Mechanical, thermal or chemical trauma
Direct mechanical trauma to a tooth can interrupt the blood supply by tearing the fine blood vessels at the root apex, leading

to avascular necrosis of the tissue within the tooth. If this occurs, the dead tissue should be removed and replaced with an inert material using endodontic therapy, to prevent the risk of bacterial infection of the dead tissue. Those at risk of injury (e.g.
contact sports players) should be encouraged to wear protective mouth guards to reduce the risk.
Some pulpal cells, in particular the odontoblasts, can be damaged or killed by:
• direct trauma to the cells themselves, including to the odontoblastic processes in dentine, during tooth cutting;
• heat generated during tooth cutting;
• chemicals applied to dentine, particularly when freshly cut;
• gross exposure of pulp tissue during cavity preparation;
• direct physical trauma to the tooth;
• bacterial invasion following exposure to the oral environment.
It is rare for the entire pulp to be killed through chemical or thermal trauma. Although death of part of the tissue will cause inflammation and pain, the pulp will usually heal in the weeks after the damage occurs, unless bacteria or their products also reach the damaged tissue.

### Responses of Dentine and Pulp to Insult

In very general terms, the dentine‐pulp complex responds to damage in ways similar to other connective tissues, i.e. it can undergo various levels of inflammation, it can heal, or it can die.

Defence within dentine
Dentine has some capacity for its own defence. Dentinal tubules are a potential pathway for the diffusion of chemicals from the external environment to the pulp and for the inward movement of micro‐organisms. However, the tubules can be reduced in diameter or be totally closed by one or more of several processes.
• dentinal sclerosis;
• calcium phosphate deposition;
• salivary precipitation;
• the formation of reparative dentine.

Mild acute injury
If the injury to dentine is mild, such as attrition or the preparation of a shallow cavity, most odontoblasts beneath the damaged area survive. Regular reparative dentine, which contains relatively normal tubules, will be laid down at the pulp‐dentine interface in relation to the area of damage as a continuation of the main body of dentine.

Moderate to severe acute injury
If more odontoblasts die because the injury is more severe in either degree or duration, the pulpal cells can produce reparative hard tissue of widely varying types. The form will depend on the nature and the stage of differentiation of the cells that affect the primary calcific repair process. Secondary dentinogenic

### Pulpal Inflammation in Response to Bacteria

Chronic inflammation
Whatever the microbial mix and its pathogenic vectors, it is likely that the first response to either caries or microleakage within pulpal tissue will be a low grade, chronic inflammation.
This will be characterized by the presence of greatly increased numbers of T‐lymphocytes in the extra‐vascular space. Mild chronic inflammation is usually symptomless, since the lymphocytes do not cause the release of factors that change the sensitivity of the pulp’s sensory nerves.
It is likely that the level of response will depend, to a large degree, on the particular bacteria involved. In one individual there may be a small zone of dentinal caries, well removed from the pulp, which evokes a vigorous response. Either the lesion contains particularly pathogenic micro‐organisms or the pulpal response includes a specific immune component related to prior challenge. On the other hand, the same microbial mix may be particularly benign in another patient and there may be little or no response until the lesion is large and close to the odontoblast layer. In some young patients with rapidly advancing carious lesions, the pulp may be completely exposed to the oral cavity and yet survive. This is probably because, in those cases, the dominant bacteria in the lesion do not evoke a destructive immune response. Similarly, a relatively small number of bacteria beneath a leaking restoration may evoke a chronic inflammatory response in one patient but not in another.

Acute inflammation
Foci of acute inflammation can develop within the chronically inflamed tissue if microbially‐derived toxins damage pulpal cells and this may bring about the local synthesis of histamine, bradykinin or prostaglandins. Alternatively, humorally‐mediated hypersensitivity reactions to microbial components or products themselves may cause tissue damage. If such acute foci develop,

the pulpal nerves may become sensitized to normal stimuli.
As a result the patient may report sensitivity of short duration to hot or cold food or drink, to cold air, or to osmotic change while eating. The pulp will not necessarily die under these circumstances and the institution of appropriate therapy may well allow healing.

### Reversible pulpitis

If, at the stage of simple chronic inflammation or chronic inflammation with small, acute foci, the aetiologic factors are removed by debridement, or denied substrate by the creation of an effective seal, the inflammation may well resolve. That is to say, the inflammation is reversible. However, it is likely that the pulpal tissue, after such a cycle of inflammation and repair, will be less vascular, less cellular and more fibrous than before, and may therefore be less able to withstand subsequent insults.

### Irreversible pulpitis

If the injury is more severe or if there is a major immune‐ mediated response to the microbial challenge, the tissue changes may become irreversible. The pulp may then die painlessly over time, or alternatively total necrosis may take place quite rapidly and cause considerable discomfort. If the level of stimulus remains relatively constant, such as under a leaking but otherwise stable restoration, the ability of the tissue to resist bacterial toxins will decline over time and a reversible inflammatory process may become irreversible. Some form of surgical ablation of the pulp tissue, such as pulpotomy or pulpectomy, will then be required if the tooth is to be retained.
The boundary between reversible and irreversible pulpitis is impossible to define. Remove the cause and a pulp in a healthy young patient may heal from a state of chronic, suppurative inflammation such as a pulp micro‐abscess. On the other hand, in older individuals, or in teeth that have been subjected to previous episodes of inflammation, a pulp micro‐abscess is more likely to spread because the tissue is less able to elaborate repair. In such circumstances the toxic products of cell lysis may kill adjacent cells.

### Internal tooth resorption

The dental pulp can be involved in several types of internal tooth resorption, usually following physical trauma. It is of clinical importance to identify those resorptions which are transient, thus not requiring treatment and those which are progressive where clearly some form of endodontic intervention is desirable.
The apical internal resorption is considered a healing response to the injury, allowing ingress of vasculature to aid in the recovery of the traumatized pulp. It can also prove beneficial in resolving a concurrent coronal discoloration due to pulpal haemorrhage. The longer‐term healing response involves the laying down of reparative hard tissue onto the resorbed tooth surface and the restitution of the apical root morphology.
Generally there will also be pulpal changes which result in progressive root canal calcification, evidenced radiographically by the narrowing of the root canal.

### Thermal and Chemical Insults

Reversible acute inflammation
Non‐bacterial, traumatic stimuli of short duration may kill or damage a few odontoblasts only. The action of cutting dentine or over‐heating it during cavity preparation, or the placement directly onto dentine of restorative materials that release toxic chemicals, such as unfilled or composite resin, may cause a simple, acute inflammatory response. The effect may be a direct sensitization of sensory nerve endings, short‐term vaso‐dilatation, and a reversible increase in blood vessel wall permeability. This situation may then be followed by increased local tissue fluid pressure and, possibly, increased lymphatic flow.

Acute inflammation leading to pulp death
More severe, non‐bacterial insults causing a greater degree of cell damage or death may bring about more marked vaso‐dilatation and the movement of substantial amounts of blood fluid and protein into the injured tissues. In the young individual, healing may occur through the sequence of events described above, despite the severity of the injury and the initial response. However, in an older patient or where a pulp has been compromised by previous episodes of inflammation and repair, such damage may lead to the death of the entire pulp. Cell death and disintegration may release lysosomal enzymes into the extracellular environment causing the death of more cells. The process of cell death almost always ends just short of the root apex. The periapical tissues are more able to resist damage than those of the aged or compromised pulp because they have a rich collateral circulation.
Direct pressure measurements in various areas of normal and inflamed pulps have shown that the tissue behaves as a gel, not a fluid, and that local pressure increases will not spread. The assumption that a pulp is destined to die if it suffers an episode of acute inflammation is therefore not warranted, particularly if bacteria are not involved.

Treatment of the dead pulp
Once blood supply and vitality have been lost, the only predictable therapy that will allow tooth retention, in the long term, is the removal of necrotic tissue debris from the pulp space and its replacement with an inert filling material. Unfortunately, if this is not done, the tissue debris is likely to become infected at some later time, resulting in periapical infection, inflammation and pain. The anticipated mode of infection is by anachoresis, the lodgement of bacteria from adjacent tissues, or the blood supply, into the pulp chamber where they can survive and multiply.

Periapical inflammation
There is continuity between the pulp and the periodontal ligament through either the apical foramina or the lateral accessory canals. Therefore, inflammatory processes within the pulp tend to affect the periapical or periradicular regions and induce what are generally termed periapical lesions. Most com-

monly, chronic apical periodontitis develops as a consequence of, and concomitantly with, chronic pulpitis of bacterial origin.
Depending on the nature and number of the micro‐organisms invading the dentine and pulp, this apical response can become well established while all or most of the pulp tissue is still alive.

### Pulpal Pain and Sensation

The pulp is richly supplied with sensory nerves, many of which end close to the odontoblastic cell bodies, and will respond to stimuli such as change of temperature. Application of stimuli may also cause pain through movement of the odontoblast cells through the fluid in the dentinal tubules. Finally, cell damage and inflammation within the body of the pulp may fire the sensory nerves and also cause pain.
Complete loss of sensibility of the dentine and pulp usually indicates that the entire pulpal tissue is dead. However, there is not an absolute link between sensibility and vitality, since patients may report apparently normal sensory responses in teeth that, on histological examination, show no evidence of a vital pulp. On the other hand, some patients may have no sensibility in teeth that are otherwise normal.
The periodontal ligament at the root apex is also well innervated.
Sensory nerves within the ligament normally provide information to the brainstem nuclei on pressure or mechanical load and tooth displacement. Such sensory information subconsciously contributes to masticatory control and may also be noted consciously as touch, pressure and pain. Inflammation in the periapical tissues decreases the critical firing threshold of the sensory nerves of the region and allows the initiation of pain by relatively minor tooth movement. Palpation through gentle movement of the tooth with finger pressure or alternatively percussion by gently tapping with a solid instrument may well elicit pain under these circumstances.

‘Cervical sensitivity’
Since the relatively recent advent of strong acid components in the human diet, a new form of tooth sensibility has emerged that is termed ‘cervical sensitivity’.
As described in Chapter 1, lesions develop in cervical regions of teeth due to the abrasive effects of toothpaste on acid‐softened tooth structure. When these lesions reach dentine, the dentinal tubules become open to the salivary environment, without any protection from either smear layer or biofilm. The same situation occurs in occlusal abrasion or tooth fracture. There is often a combination of daily exposure to acid, usually in the form of fruit juice, wine or cola drinks, followed, within hours, by tooth brushing with a dentifrice that has abrasive particles harder than acid‐softened dentine. This will maintain the patency of the tubule orifices, and at the same time cause the further loss of enamel and dentine.
Teeth with lesions of this kind are highly responsive to osmotic change, to changes in temperature and even to mild drying of the tooth surface during speaking or eating, thus causing pain at a high level of intensity.

The simplest and most effective treatment for this combination is to stop, or substantially reduce, the strong acid exposure and limit tooth cleaning to once per day. Under those circumstances the tubule orifices naturally close within a few days through precipitation of salivary components. Commercial dentifrices are also available that contain components that precipitate into tubule orifices and reduce the pain to some degree.
In order to be effective these must be used daily and the cause also recognized and eliminated.

Heightened sensibility due to pulpal inflammation
As described earlier in this chapter, any area of acute inflammation within the pulp will cause increased sensibility by lowering the critical firing threshold of pulpal sensory nerves. This is an unpleasant experience for the afflicted individual, but can be useful diagnostically. In general, if the pain is of short duration, and is elicited by cold, the inflammation may well be reversible once the causative factor or factors are removed. Pain that persists for minutes or more, and in particular long‐duration pain induced by heat, indicates more extensive tissue damage and a lower likelihood of resolution through healing.

### Tests of Pulpal and Periapical Status

No one test alone can give an accurate picture of the state of the dental pulp or peri‐apex. It is only by the correlation of all available information that the clinician can arrive at a diagnosis. A careful visual examination, using good illumination and magnification, plus a radiographic examination should be used in conjunction with the tests described below.

### Avoidance of Pulpal Damage Due to Caries

Most pulp death is due either directly or indirectly to dental caries
[3, 4]. Therefore, those strategies that prevent the initiation of carious lesions are likely to preserve pulp vitality at the same time as they conserve tooth tissue.
As the pulp will be involved almost immediately caries penetrates through the enamel and reaches the dentine, early interceptive therapy to arrest and cure carious lesions will also protect the pulp from damage. Dietary control, appropriate administration of fluoride, both systemic and topical, modification of the bacterial flora, fissure sealing, maintenance of salivary flow and good oral hygiene are, therefore, all pulp protective measures.
In the presence of active caries, placement of a restoration is designed to arrest, or at least slow down, the progress of the disease and will therefore contribute to the protection of the pulp.
As a general principle, a small restoration is less likely to damage the pulp than a large one. The early diagnosis of active caries followed by conservative restoration placement, when indicated, is therefore in the interest of the survival of the pulp, even when total control of the disease is not possible.

Indirect pulp therapy
The ability of the pulp to respond satisfactorily to ‘closed trauma’, such as a brief period of dehydration or heat during cavity preparation, is very good. However, the introduction of bacterial infection to an exposed pulp can bring about rapid pulp death. This means that exposure of the pulp to the oral environment carries major risks to the survival of the tissue. It is therefore desirable to keep the pulp covered at all times, providing this can be done without compromising other treatment goals. The old concept of removal of all softened demineralized dentine from the floor of a cavity is fraught with danger and it must be recognized that the lower layers of dentine, even though softened, are not necessarily infected. They may simply be demineralized and can often be retained [5].
It must be recognized that there are at least two clearly defined zones within a normal carious lesion but it must also be recognized that differentiation between these layers is very difficult [6]. However, isolation of the carious lesion from the oral environment, leading to removal of all sources of nutrition for the bacteria, will normally lead to successful control. The normal pulp has a good capacity to defend itself through normal biological processes against the acids and toxins arising from the advancing bacterial activity. Isolation of the lesion by sealing it from the oral environment, particularly with a bioactive adhesive material such as glass‐ionomer cement, will assist the pulp to marshal its own defences [7].

The defences of the pulp will be overcome and the dentine, in spite of the hyper‐mineralization, will become demineralized. Thereafter the outer layers of the lesion, adjacent to the oral cavity, will be heavily infected by bacteria and the dentine will become seriously involved. The collagen framework of the tubules, now totally demineralized, will be subject to disintegration by proteolytic bacteria and will become denatured and structureless. It will no longer be possible to remineralize this part of the tooth structure so it will need to be removed as part of the reconstruction and repair. This layer is known as the ‘infected layer’.

### ‘Affected layer’

However, the demineralization of the dentine will always progress in stages well in advance of the proteolysis and complete

breakdown. The lower layer of the lesion will now be softened but not infected. This zone of demineralized dentine will retain the basic dentine structure, with the collagen framework of the tubules still extant, but demineralized to some degree. It would appear that, up to the point where there is less than 10% of mineral remaining attached to the collagen, it is still possible to remineralize the tubules and heal the lesion. This layer will be relatively free of bacteria except for, possibly, a few ‘pioneer bacteria’ and therefore can be retained and probably healed. It will be preceded by the translucent layer of dentine representing the hyper‐mineralized layer stimulated by the pulp. The lateral canals will be sealed and this in turn will limit the speed of penetration of the acids and toxins being produced by the bacteria.
It is desirable, therefore, to retain this affected layer of dentine and offer it some stimulus to completely remineralize itself.
Removal of affected dentine is both unnecessary and undesirable because of the potential for further irritating the pulp or, worse still, developing a mechanical exposure, which will leave the pulp open to the direct introduction of bacteria. In other words, every effort should be expended to bring about healing of the affected layer rather than its removal. This should be regarded as one of the cornerstones of minimal intervention dentistry.

The removal of the superficial layer of infected dentine is a major component of the surgical treatment of an advancing carious lesion and this is relatively simple to accomplish.
However, the infected zone is not always easy to define because it is quite soft and about the same colour as the surrounding dentine, including the softened affected layer beneath it. The caries‐disclosing solutions that are presently available are not particularly reliable in differentiating between the two layers in as much as they disclose bacteria rather than demineralized dentine. There are certain commercial products that claim to reliably lead to removal of infected dentine only, but these also appear to allow excessive removal of dentine that can otherwise be remineralized. It is suggested, therefore, that caries control in an extensive lesion should be carried out in two stages. The first stage should be aimed at removal of the infected layer to the extent that there is now room for a transitional restoration. The second stage is directed at sealing the entire lesion to isolate any remaining bacteria from further nutrition and thus inactivate them.
Stage 1. Gain limited access to the lesion through the removal of enamel around the margins. Carry out limited debridement of the surface of the lesion to remove the infected dentine only and clean the walls around the complete circumference of the lesion sufficient only to expose sound healthy dentine. This will allow for the development of an ion exchange adhesion and a complete seal between the restoration and the tooth, thus eliminating microleakage. Remove sufficient tooth structure so that the temporary restoration will have the strength to withstand the occlusal load.
Stage 2. Now place a temporary restoration, using a material that has a level of bioactivity and is capable of providing a complete marginal seal. Leave it in place for three weeks at least.

This routine will allow time for any pulpal inflammation to subside and for the pulp to lay down an initial increment of reparative dentine in those areas closest to the lesion. Over time, secondary dentine formation will follow within the pulp chamber because the tissue fluid in the pulp is naturally supersaturated with calcium and phosphate ions and, in the absence of bacteria, the pulp has a high potential for repair. If the pulp survives the inflammation and retains its vitality, there will also be a degree of remineralization in the affected demineralized zone.
In the presence of a bioactive restorative material, this activity is likely to continue for some time although, at this time, it is not possible to guarantee full remineralization.

### Temporary (transitional) restoration

The main function of the temporary restoration is to provide a complete seal so that any remaining bacteria will be deprived of simple carbohydrate from the diet, and will therefore not be able to produce sufficient acid for demineralization to continue.
Further advantages will accrue if the temporary cement has an antiseptic or anti‐bacterial effect on the remaining micro‐ organisms. In addition, if it is at all bioactive, it will help stimulate remineralization of the collagen framework of the dentine tubules and the affected layer may heal entirely.

### Longevity of the temporary restoration

The temporary restoration should remain for a minimum of three weeks, but no longer than a maximum of six months, following which the situation should be re‐assessed. By this time pulpal inflammation should have subsided and there may have been some degree of secondary dentine formed within the pulp chamber. There will now be two clear alternatives available and these require a level of experience and clinical judgement.

### Alternative #1

There may be a sense of insecurity in leaving a relatively substantial layer of affected dentine behind so the operator may choose to remove the entire temporary restoration for reassurance of success. If glass‐ionomer cement was placed as the transitional restoration, there will have been a degree of remineralization in the pulp as well as the affected layer. There may be some discoloured, demineralized dentine remaining but, in view of the continuing potential for remineralization, some of this can be safely left on the floor of the cavity. In fact, cleaning the lesion down to a hard shiny dentine surface is unnecessary and undesirable because of the loss of dentine that may still be able to remineralize. Worse still, it may result in a pulp exposure.
Having determined that remaining demineralized dentine is now firmer and leathery in texture, a permanent restoration can be placed using, for preference, a glass‐ionomer cement lining or base. A similar situation may well arise following placement of a zinc oxide‐eugenol temporary restoration but, because this material hydrolyzes relatively rapidly, it should not remain beyond six weeks.

### Alternative #2

If the operator feels confident of success, there may be no need to remove all of the transitional restoration. The bulk can be left behind as a base for the final restoration. Cut it back far enough to make room for another material and laminate over it to restore full anatomy and physical properties in what is now a permanent restoration. Always keep in mind the physical properties of the laminate and make sufficient room to ensure longevity of that. Some materials can withstand occlusal load better than others.
The decision as to which alternative to undertake can only be decided by the clinician and will depend upon the initial assessment of the amount of affected dentine remaining beneath the restoration. If there is doubt and any insecurity, then it may be wise to remove the entire temporary and reassess knowing that the risk of an accidental exposure is always there. On the other hand, assuming the tooth is free of symptoms, the alternative technique is very conservative of tooth structure, and therefore the method of choice.
This two‐stage sequence of debridement, covering a period of time, is known as ‘indirect pulp therapy’ because it is designed to avoid pulp exposure while allowing the tissue to heal. It has also been called ‘indirect pulp capping’ because it is intended that the potential pulp exposure will be ‘capped’ by secondary dentine before the area of the potential exposure is reached, during the second stage of debridement. The term ‘transitional restoration’ is coming into common usage, particularly if the second alternative treatment is the one of choice. The life span of a ‘temporary’ glass‐ionomer cement can be quite lengthy, limited mainly by the difficulty of restoring full coronal and occlusal anatomy, with proper proximal contour and a well‐formed marginal ridge.

Choice of temporary restoration material
For many years the preferred temporary restoration material was a zinc oxide‐eugenol paste:
• It provides an adequate seal, excluding most if not all dietary substrate from the remaining micro‐organisms in dentine.
• It releases eugenol into subjacent dentine, including demineralized dentine, the rate of release being determined by the wetness of the tissue.
• Eugenol is effective in killing residual bacteria.
• Sufficient eugenol may diffuse through the dentine into the pulp space to inhibit inflammation and pain, as will be described below.
The principal shortcomings of zinc oxide‐eugenol in this role are its limited mechanical strength and its limited durability for the longer term, because it slowly degrades through hydrolysis. Various resins have been included to modify its formula in order to increase both strength and durability, but despite this, fracture strength and wear resistance are not sufficient and the expected life span is therefore limited.
Glass‐ionomer cement is now the material of choice in this situation because it is more durable and has the added advantage, through its bioactivity, of releasing calcium, strontium and phosphate ions as well as fluoride:
• Glass‐ionomer cement is relatively insoluble.
• It is sufficiently strong to withstand reasonable occlusal load.
• It is easily placed and relatively easy to remove.
• It releases fluoride which has the potential to kill bacteria in dentine.
• It releases calcium, strontium and phosphate ions, all of which contribute to the remineralization of adjacent hard tissues.

### Avoidance of Pulpal Damage during Cavity

### Preparation

There is good evidence that cutting dentine damages or kills odontoblasts. The odontoblastic processes may be cut, or sudden movement of fluid, even in the cell‐free areas of the tubule, may damage the odontoblasts or disrupt the cell membrane.
Deep or extensive cutting of dentine is therefore more likely to cause pulpal damage and should be avoided where possible.

Generation of heat
The generation of heat during tooth cutting has considerable potential to damage pulpal cells. The use of air/water spray coolant is essential to clean, lubricate and cool both the tooth and the bur when using high speed rotary cutting instruments.
The handpiece and the bur must also be in good condition and running concentrically because undue vibration can also cause damage to the pulp.

Drying the dentine
Dentine is traversed by tubules that normally contain both plasma ultrafiltrate (dentinal fluid) and odontoblastic processes.

Cut dentine is therefore naturally wet. However, it is necessary for the surface of dentine to be relatively dry for the successful placement of some restorative materials. Dentine can be dried using either a high pressure jet of air at ambient temperature or warm air at a lesser pressure from a triple syringe. Organic liquids such as acetone and ether can also be used to take surface water into solution as they wash the dentine and then evaporate.
Each method of drying brings with it the possibility of aspiration of odontoblastic cell bodies into dentinal tubules, followed by cell rupture. Prolonged drying is more likely to cause damage than that which is relatively brief and gentle, and a risk/benefit assessment may need to be made. The long‐term benefit may be a more effective bonding of a resin‐restorative material to the dry dentine surface, while the short‐term risk is pulpal damage through excessive dehydration.

Recovery from physical insult
Odontoblast damage may therefore be inevitable during the preparation of a cavity prior to placement of a restoration and it may be an acceptable risk, relative to the overall benefit to the patient. If the pulp is free of bacteria at the time of injury, there is a strong possibility that the inflammation, which will certainly follow cell damage, can fulfil its physiological function and the tissue will heal, even though leaving some degree of ‘premature aging’.

### Protective Measures during Restoration

### Placement

Minimizing bacterial microleakage
There is strong evidence that bacteria can grow beneath some restorative materials and that either bacterial by‐products or bacteria themselves can then move through dentine to induce pulpal damage, inflammation and death. This concept is generally described as bacterial microleakage. Positive correlations have been demonstrated between the presence of bacteria immediately beneath restorations and pulpal inflammation.
For example, the species Prevotella intermedia and Prevotella melaninogenica are known to be able to produce lipopolysaccaride which may then diffuse through dentine causing a chronic inflammatory response in the pulp, possibly leading on to cell death [8].
Those restorative materials that are most often associated with pulpal problems following clinical restoration are known to permit bacterial microleakage [9]. Those materials that are most effective in protecting the pulp probably do so because they provide a seal which is proof against such bacterial invasion.

### Placement of a lining or base to prevent subsequent

### microleakage

If it is necessary to place a restorative material that does not develop a union with dentine, a primary barrier should be laid down first using a lining or a base, such as glass‐ionomer

cement, which adheres to the tooth structure through an ion exchange. It may also be beneficial to first treat the cavity surface with an anti‐bacterial agent to kill residual bacteria before commencing restoration, providing the agent itself does no harm to the pulp and does not interfere with the adhesion of the subsequent lining or base [10, 11].

Chemical toxicity
It must be understood that all chemical substances are toxic.
How toxic they are in a particular circumstance depends upon the concentration of the substance in relation to a particular tissue, and the time for which it is present.
Dentine is an excellent acid buffer and will limit the diffusion rate of even the strongest acid to the extent that the pulp will suffer no ill effects, providing there is a reasonably substantial barrier of sound dentine between the two [12]. Dentine can also reduce the concentration of chemicals reaching the pulp when they are applied to the dentine surface because it is of limited permeability. Even materials such as zinc oxide‐eugenol, which are very toxic when in contact with soft tissue, can be safely applied to dentine because the release of eugenol into dentine is limited by its wetness, and the eugenol must then diffuse through dentine which is of limited permeability. These two factors lower the concentration of eugenol to below the toxic level in pulp tissue.
However, dentine is not a universal barrier and some restorative materials release chemicals that can diffuse through and damage the pulp. For example, moderate to severe pulpal inflammation may occur within days after placement of an unfilled bonding resin against deep, etched dentine because of the release of toxic chemicals from the resin. Low viscosity resin luting cements, which are placed under considerable pressure during crown seating, can also bypass the protective barrier effect by flowing into and through dentinal tubules, particularly if the tubules have been opened by acid etching.
Restorative materials that can be placed with relative safety on intact dentine may cause moderate but reversible pulpal damage if placed directly on exposed pulp tissue because the concentration of released chemicals is then much higher in that tissue [13].

Recovery from chemical insult
Healthy pulp tissue is able to tolerate mild or moderate physical or chemical damage to the odontoblast layer as long as bacteria and their toxins are not present. In general, in the absence of bacteria, the death of a limited number of cells will induce a transient, acute inflammation. New cells differentiate from the adjacent pulp to replace those that have died and the patient may only be aware of mild symptoms of pulpitis for a few days [14].

Chemical diffusion and fluid flow through dentine

are all possible contributors to adverse pulpal responses. It is therefore worthwhile reviewing briefly what is known about the potential pathways of movement through dentine [15].

### Permeability of dentine

Dentine behaves as an impermeable solid traversed by water‐ filled tubules. Fluids can diffuse through dentine only via the tubules and motile bacteria can grow and move within the tubule fluid. The term permeability is used to include all three types of potential movement: i.e. diffusion, fluid flow and bacterial passage through dentine. The degree of dentine permeability is determined by:
• tubule diameter – the diameter decreases with age;
• density – there are less tubules at the dentino‐enamel junction;
• length – proximity of cavity floor to the pulp;
• presence or absence of a smear layer;
• coagulated protein or calcific deposits, e.g. secondary dentine;
• restorative materials, e.g. cements, resins or varnishes may modify permeability.

### Modifying factors

Irrespective of other factors, the permeability of cut dentine is substantially increased by a factor of 4–5 times by removal of the smear layer that is generated by cavity preparation. The smear layer can be removed rapidly with strong acid etchants, such as phosphoric acid, and a little more slowly with weaker acids, such as ethyldiamine tetracetic acid (EDTA) or polyalkenoic acids.
While complete smear layer removal may increase the potential bond strength of some adhesive restorative materials to dentine, it also increases the potential for chemical diffusion from such materials through dentine to the pulp. It will also allow an outwards fluid flow through dentine and this may prevent the development of an effective seal for the restorative material, thus leading to later ingress of bacteria or their by‐products. This means that the decision whether or not to remove the smear layer involves an assessment of the risk/benefit ratio.
The relative area of dentine occupied by tubules increases as the cavity floor approaches the pulp. The number of tubules in coronal dentine is approximately:
• 20,000 per mm2 at the dentino‐enamel junction, i.e. 1% of dentine area;
• 45,000 per mm2 close to the roof of the pulp, i.e. 22% of dentine area.
Dentine permeability increases as remaining dentine thickness decreases towards the pulp because:
• tubules are tapered and become larger in diameter;
• they converge and become more densely packed;
• they become shorter.
Dentine becomes markedly more permeable over the pulp horns, where tubule density is highest and dentine is thinnest. It is also more permeable through the axial walls of prepared cavities than beneath the occlusal floor. The depth of the cavity, the location of the walls and floor relative to the pulp horns and the surface condition of dentine therefore control the potential for

pulpal damage during preparation and restoration placement.
All of these factors must influence decisions concerning the need for pulp protection.

### Risks to the Pulp from Plastic Restorative

### Materials

Glass‐ionomer cement
Although glass‐ionomer cement is inherently chemically toxic, the major toxin, unreacted acid, is well buffered by hydroxyapatite following placement into a cavity as long as the pulp is not exposed [16, 17]. Also the ion exchange union between the cement and the underlying tooth structure leads to the development of an ion‐enriched layer at the interface which effectively seals the tubules. Any subsequent failure of the union will be cohesive in the cement rather than adhesive at the interface, leaving a fine layer of the ion exchange layer still attached to the tooth, sealing the tubules and preventing microleakage [18].

Composite resin
Composite resins present two major problems for the pulp [19].
First, they release chemical toxins, particularly in the first few days after placement, and, second, they do not reliably adhere to dentine. Therefore, they may be subject to continuing microleakage. There is some variation between composite resins in toxin release pattern, and the degree of damage caused by these toxins will depend on the variables, discussed above, which influence dentine permeability.
There can also be some variation according to the cavity geometry. In an intra‐coronal box‐form cavity that is directly filled with a single increment of composite resin, there is a strong likelihood that a gap will form at one or more cavity walls due to the setting contraction of the resin. This is particularly true with the light‐activated composite resins and the problem needs to be controlled by careful incremental packing and curing. In addition, the larger the cavity, the greater the problem, because the total shrinkage is greater.
It has been shown that triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA), a small molecular weight diluent used in most composite resins, can traverse, in biologically significant quantities, through dentine in the first days after placement. Also, most resin restorative materials, particularly those that are light‐ activated, contain varying quantities of hydroxyl‐ethyl methacrylate (HEMA) and this is toxic in tissue culture. The dentine will modify the diffusion to the extent that the pulp response will be mitigated but there should be a degree of caution exercised. The following points should be noted:
• dentine which has just been debrided of active caries is more permeable;
• thickness of remaining dentine is very significant;
• acid etching of dentine will reduce its buffering effect.
It is therefore desirable to first place a base capable of sealing the dentine tubules [20]. Glass‐ionomer cement is the most effective

Dental amalgam
Amalgam has a low potential for chemical toxicity and seals the interface between restoration and tooth structure relatively well through corrosion, which commences shortly after placement. There is a moderate marginal gap in the first weeks after placement, but this decreases due to the formation of corrosion products and to the calcification of surface pellicle and other organic materials. If amalgam is used alone as a restorative material without a primary layer of resin, varnish, liner or base, there may be pulpal inflammation in the days following placement due to early microleakage. Thereafter, because an effective seal will be developed through corrosion, the pulpitis is likely to subside.
Copal varnish has been recognized for many years as providing an effective seal from microleakage in the short term and, as it washes out, the amalgam corrodes and develops its own organic seal. A low viscosity resin or a thinly mixed glass‐ ionomer has now been suggested and both will be effective over the longer term.

### Risks to the Pulp with Luted Restorations

There are additional risks to the pulp in larger, indirectly fabricated restorations and these are related to both the higher overall level of mechanical trauma and choices between and methods of use of either luting agents or of adhesive cements of various kinds. Those risks and the prevention and treatment of adverse effects in such cases are best covered in textbooks on fixed prosthodontics.

### Materials Used in Pulp Protection

Varnishes and other surface treatments

### Varnishes

A varnish is a material of very low viscosity containing a relatively large proportion by volume of a volatile solvent, and its prime use is for application to dentine in order to decrease

permeability. However, because of the high proportion of solvent, and subsequently the large volume reduction on drying, the ultimate film is relatively porous and therefore not very effective. It relies, to a degree, on the presence of an intact smear layer, with which it may combine, to reduce permeability. There are many versions on the market and they were originally used to decrease the early microleakage around amalgam restorations and then wash out to be replaced by corrosion products and this remains their best use.

### Resin sealants

The light‐activated, unfilled resins may be used as cavity primers and bonding agents with composite resins. They seal dentine more effectively after the smear layer has been removed. Most are relatively viscous and set by either chemical action following mixing, or light activation, or both. Some systems are relatively complex and require several sequential applications to achieve the desired bond. If properly applied, they reduce the potential for ingress of bacteria or their by‐products.
Whether or not the brief, chemical risk they pose to odontoblasts is outweighed by their possible longer‐term benefits, by enhancing the seal against microleakage, has yet to be determined.

### Remineralizing solutions

Several chemical treatments using topical fluoride or oxalate salts are designed to reduce dentinal permeability and therefore the risk of ingress of bacteria and their products. Most of these will successfully reduce dentinal sensibility but the long‐term benefits of such treatments for pulp health have not yet been established.

Liners and bases
The main differences between a liner and a base are the thickness and strength. The term liner is used for a thin wash. A base is a relatively thick material strong enough to provide resistance from and to become an intrinsic part of the ultimate restoration.
A base can be regarded as a dentine substitute.

### Calcium hydroxide

Calcium hydroxide was introduced for direct pulp capping on the assumption that it would promote calcification in the wounded tissue against which it was placed, for example, an exposed pulp. It was expected to provide calcium ions, thus aiding remineralization but this has since been discounted.
Because it is strongly alkaline at pH 12–13, there will be a degree of necrosis in adjacent soft tissue but, at the same time bacteria fail to thrive in its presence. Therefore, it will counter bacterial microleakage and, as it is not unduly toxic, exposed and relatively healthy pulp tissue in the vicinity usually survives. Calcific scar tissue may be laid down beyond any area of necrosis and may successfully bridge the lesion if no bacteria remain.
However, over time, calcium hydroxide is likely to dissolve and be washed out from under any restoration that does not have a complete marginal seal, thus its effect may be transitory.

### Zinc phosphate cement

This cement has been used for many decades as an ‘insulating’ base material beneath metallic restorations and also as a luting agent. Despite its acid nature, it is well tolerated by the pulp if placed on intact dentine, presumably because of buffering of the unreacted acid by hydroxyapatite. There may be immediate and short‐term pain if placed on the dentine of an unanaesthetized tooth, probably due to osmotic effects on dentine tubule fluid.
As it has no therapeutic effect upon the pulp, there now seem to be few indications for its use as a lining or base material.

### Zinc oxide‐eugenol (ZOE)

ZOE is a useful part of pulp therapy in the management of deep, active carious lesions and has also been used as a lining and base material. Like glass‐ionomer cement, it provides an effective anti‐bacterial seal, probably because any gap between the cement and dentine will contain a high concentration of eugenol, which is strongly bactericidal. Any available eugenol may also inhibit bacterial metabolism within dentine, and if the material is placed on intact dentine, it is unlikely to harm pulp cells. It is also possible for it to develop local anaesthetic and anti‐inflammatory reactions in adjacent pulp tissue. Despite these therapeutic benefits, the cement slowly hydrolyzes with time, leaving a residue of soft zinc hydroxide. Also, eugenol will inhibit the polymerization of composite resin, so it must not be used anywhere prior to, or in relation to, resin restorations or resin luting cements.
Neither should it be used in direct contact with exposed pulp tissue, since the release of eugenol by hydrolysis is markedly greater due to the wetness of the tissue. A concentration of eugenol sufficient to kill pulpal cells may develop rapidly in adjacent vital tissue and the level may be sustained for several days.
Indications for the use of zinc oxide‐eugenol are limited to those situations where the dentine is intact and some form of indirect pulp therapy or caries therapy is required. For example, it is an effective temporary restoration in those situations where it is intended to remove infected surface caries and leave demineralized affected caries behind. Generally, within three weeks it will be safe to proceed with a permanent restoration. Because of the problem of long‐term hydrolysis, its use should be limited to less than three months as a long‐term temporary restoration.

### Glass‐ionomer cement

Glass‐ionomer cement, when placed without pressure onto intact dentine, poses no chemical risk to the pulp. Because of the development of the ion exchange layer, it creates an effective anti‐bacterial seal. It shows a very low solubility, and therefore

appears to be the material of choice for use as a base, or dentine substitute, beneath all plastic restorations.
Glass‐ionomer cement also has considerable potential as a long‐term temporary restoration in the treatment of active caries. Following the removal of infected dentine, a strong mix of glass‐ionomer cement with a high powder/liquid ratio is placed in the cavity over the remaining affected dentine and allowed to set. In the presence of dentinal fluid from the affected dentine, there is likely to be a reasonable release of fluoride as well as strontium and phosphate ions from the cement and these will be useful in the remineralizing process. It will adhere to the remaining tooth structure through the ion exchange mechanism and, in addition, is apparently mildly anti‐bacterial because of the fluoride release.
Glass‐ionomer cement has been shown to be very effective in stimulating remineralization of ‘affected’ or partially demineralized dentine over a short period of time. Providing it is used with a high powder content, cement with high physical strength may be retained as a permanent restoration in the presence of high caries rates. If the occlusal load is expected to be high, it can be laminated at a later time with resin composite or amalgam to ensure longevity.

### Vital Pulp Therapy

In addition to procedures designed to keep the pulp alive by avoiding pulpal damage, there are several routine measures available which are designed to help heal a damaged pulp.
Whether the damaged pulp will heal with the aid of a particular therapy or not will depend to a large extent upon the balance between the damaging factors and the host response. The richness of the blood supply could well be a determining factor.
Therefore, the chances of success are probably better in youth than in the aging patient because continuing mineralization within the pulp chamber and root canals will reduce the circulation and therefore the ability to respond to stimulus.
It is impossible to accurately predict the outcome of a particular therapy when the principal pulpal irritant is microbial because both the microbial mix and the host response may vary greatly. These two factors can vary between individual patients, between teeth and, with time, in the same patient. Despite this, several therapies have been applied with sufficient likelihood of success to have made them acceptable and reasonable parts of the practice of dentistry.

Treatment of pulpitis under intact dentine
A transient acute pulpitis may occur due to a long‐term insult, such as:
• an advancing carious lesion;
• microleakage under a restoration;
• acute traumatic injury;
• exposure of extensive areas of healthy dentine during crown preparation.

Some form of interceptive therapy may sometimes be appropriate. The most effective therapy in these cases is to remove the insult, mainly by placement of a sound, well‐sealed temporary restoration. If the pulp is still alive and there is no bacterial infection, the potential for resolution of the inflammation and its symptoms is excellent.
Two types of material can be applied to dentine to help overcome or suppress pulpal inflammation. Zinc oxide‐eugenol mixtures release eugenol into the dentine and adjacent pulpal soft tissue by slow hydrolysis of the eugenolate. The eugenol can have anti‐inflammatory effects during the several days after placement. Materials containing corticosteroids can also be placed onto dentine and pulp, causing suppression of inflammation within hours and lasting for about a day. Neither of these symptomatic treatments appears to inhibit the normal progress of healing, provided that the causative factor, usually metabolically active bacteria in carious dentine or beneath a leaking restoration, is removed at the same time.

### Treatment of the Exposed Pulp

Pulp tissue may be exposed to the oral environment as a result of direct mechanical trauma, fracturing both enamel and dentine, or it may occur, inadvertently, during cavity preparation.
Various materials may be placed directly on the exposed pulp, but whether or not the pulp will then survive depends upon:
• the age of the patient – because of a relatively high vascularity, the younger the patient, the more likely the tooth will survive;
• the state of the pulp, including its cellularity and vascularity – previous injury or insult may have led to the development of fibrous repair tissue, thereby reducing vascularity and the ability of the tissue to recover;
• the nature and number of contaminating micro‐organisms – the presence of any micro‐organism is undesirable and attempts at sterilization with anti‐bacterial agents may cause further tissue damage. However, in the presence of high vascularity, the pulp tissue may survive minor infection with relatively benign bacteria.
• the material applied – if the pulp tissue is healthy and the area of exposed tissue relatively small, and if contaminating bacteria are either absent or benign, calcific healing will occur adjacent to many materials. Effectiveness of the seal over the long term, with prevention of further ingress of bacteria, should therefore be the principal consideration.
Several materials have been used over the last century in attempts to induce pulpal repair and to retain pulp vitality in exposed pulps. As noted immediately above, responses will be highly variable because of factors unrelated to the material chosen. However, some distinctions can be made between materials, as described below.
Calcium hydroxide, either as the pure compound mixed with saline, as a commercially available paste with hydroxyl‐ methylcellulose, or as a cement in combination with salicylate,

has proven to be well tolerated by young pulp tissue that is only mildly inflamed and has few or no bacteria are present. As described earlier in this chapter, the highly alkaline nature of the material kills adjacent cells and bacteria, often allowing calcific repair to occur beneath a thin necrotic layer.
A material generally called mineral trioxide aggregate, which is Portland cement plus bismuth trioxide to make it radio‐ opaque, can also be used in similar circumstances, with similar outcomes. Several similar compound materials, also containing calcium silicates, and that are closer in colour to dentine, are also commercially available.
Glass‐ionomer cement will create such a seal under most circumstances and the pulp may heal in direct contact with it. It has a very low pH before setting, but the acid is very well buffered by dentine and does not reach the pulp in significant concentrations. However, without intervening dentine, GIC is quite toxic to the exposed pulp tissue because of the low pH.
The response will be better if just the pulpal soft tissue is covered with auto‐cure calcium hydroxide cement or one of the calcium silicate materials described above, prior to being sealed with glass‐ionomer.
Despite its anti‐bacterial properties, zinc oxide‐eugenol is strongly contraindicated for direct contact with the exposed pulp. The material releases eugenol by hydrolysis, and in contact with wet tissue the amount of eugenol released is high enough to kill cells in most or all of the pulp. It has the same damaging effects if extruded through the root apex when used as a root‐filling paste in endodontic therapy, with potentially catastrophic consequences for the mandibular nerve if it enters the mandibular canal.
Placing composite resin directly onto exposed pulpal tissue is not advisable, for two reasons. First, there is the strong likelihood of immediate pulpal damage due to the release of unreacted monomer where there is no protective effect from intervening dentine. Second, with such materials in large restorations, the long‐term avoidance of bacterial ingress is, at best, uncertain.

Cvek pulpotomy
When a small area of uninfected pulp, such as a pulp horn, is exposed by tooth fracture in a young individual, the preferred treatment is a ‘Cvek pulpotomy’, named after the dentist who first described the technique. The problem is most likely to occur in permanent upper anterior teeth of teenagers and young adults during sport, rough play or fighting and it is likely that, at this age, the pulp tissue will be free of fibrotic degenerative tissue and the blood supply will be rich. The potential for calcific repair can be excellent under these circumstances.

The high success rate in young patients is probably due to a combination of factors, including the low level of bacterial contamination, a rich blood supply and a good response to trauma in that age group.

Deciduous and permanent tooth pulpotomy
If larger areas of pulp tissue are infected and need to be removed, the response is generally not so effective.
A total pulpotomy, in which the entire coronal pulp is removed, is often used as an alternative to full root canal therapy in deciduous teeth, and may be acceptable since failure is often slow enough to allow natural exfoliation after space retention for several years. Zinc oxide‐eugenol either alone or following brief, topical treatment with formaldehyde or glutaraldehyde generally creates a sterile pulp space, and kills residual tissue in the root canal system. There is, however, a risk to the calcification of succedaneous teeth with this approach, depending on their state of development at the time.
In permanent teeth, however, the predictability of a full coronal pulpotomy and its success rate are so poor as to contraindicate the procedure, except as a temporary or economic expedient.
Full endodontic or root canal therapy, which has a predictable and positive outcome if done well, is generally indicated.
