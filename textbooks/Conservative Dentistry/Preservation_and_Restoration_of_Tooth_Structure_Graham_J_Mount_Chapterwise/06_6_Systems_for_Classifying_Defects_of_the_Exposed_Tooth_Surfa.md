### Introduction

As has been described in Chapter 1, acquired defects on the crown or exposed root surface of a tooth can arise from one or more of the following causes:
• dental caries;
• chemical corrosion, either alone or in combination with abrasion;
• attrition, either alone or in combination with chemical corrosion;
• heavy occlusal load;
• external trauma.
Most defects can be prevented, stabilized or healed to some degree. If not managed effectively in their early stages, each can lead to damage or loss of tooth structure to the extent that traditional restorative materials are required for replacement of diseased or missing tooth structure including, possibly, the reconstruction of the occlusion.
Identifying and describing all lesions at the time of initial examination allow a comprehensive and logical treatment plan to be formulated both to eliminate the cause and repair the damage.
Over the past one hundred years the dental profession has used, almost exclusively, a description or classification of carious lesions proposed in 1908 by Dr. G.V. Black [1]. The word
‘classification’ means either the act of classifying lesions into groups, or one of the groups into which lesions have been classified.
The Black classification was welcomed by a profession that was actively seeking guidance for improvement. The classification initially had many beneficial effects. During the first half of the twentieth century, there was increasing acceptance within the profession that, in terms of long‐term patient health and well‐being, restoration of carious defects was preferable to tooth extraction. This was a very positive change that profoundly influenced the dental profession and has brought improved health to many hundreds of millions of people.
However, some important limitations with the Black classification system have been acknowledged. On the one hand, it is much less precise in describing the size or stage of development of early lesions than is desirable for optimal patient care. The
Black classification system did not describe the lesions, rather it described the final restoration location. This made abundant sense at the time as there was essentially only one treatment for dental caries: surgical intervention. As a consequence, Black’s classification became strongly linked conceptually to prescribed cavity forms that today are recognized as being substantially larger than necessary to restore both the form and function of teeth, both with both modern materials and the amalgam and gold of Black’s era. Further, Black’s classification did not allow monitoring of lesion size, progress and activity. For these reasons, other systems for describing and assessing acquired defects have been developed.

In this text it is suggested that the continued use of the Black classification system is no longer appropriate. It is, however, important for students and practitioners of dentistry to understand its fundamentals because it has profoundly influenced the care that many patients have received in the past and, in many cases, up to the present day.

### Black’s Classification of Carious Lesions

Black’s [1] classification is as follows.
• Class I lesions are those originating in pits or fissures on the tooth crown (including the lingual pits of maxillary incisors and the facial pit of madibular molars).
• Class II lesions are those originating at or near approximal contact areas in posterior teeth.
• Class III lesions are those originating at or near approximal contact areas in anterior teeth.
• Class IV lesions are those originating at or near approximal contact areas in anterior teeth to the extent that the incisal edge of the tooth crown has been compromised or failed.
• Class V lesions are those originating on surfaces in the gingival one third of the tooth crown, or on exposed root surfaces.
The cavity forms that Black recommended were linked to the classification system. He offered a thoughtfully formulated set of principles for cavity preparation. The resultant cavity preparations were of a predetermined minimum size, each very large by today’s ethical standards.
Several factors probably contributed to the strong linkage between lesion classification and relatively large cavity form:
1	 The system of classification, cavity design and preparation was created before the use of either radiographs or clinical magnification, so carious lesions were not detected until they were visible to the naked eye. That is, by present‐day standards, relatively large.
2	 There was no understanding of caries that allowed effective non‐surgical control, either arrest or reversal. Debridement and restoration were the primary methods of disease control, the only alternatives being to allow caries to progress unchecked, or to extract the tooth.
3	 There were limitations in the equipment available for cavity preparation and restoration, and dentists had difficulty seeing fine detail in the absence of artificial illumination and magnification.
Black therefore advised that it was necessary to do the following:
1	 to remove additional tooth structure to gain access to the lesion for good visibility;
2	 to remove all trace of demineralized enamel and dentine from the floor, walls and margins of the cavity, because remineralization or healing was not then possible;
3	 to extend the cavity outline to allow bulk in the restorative material to provide strength in the restoration;

4	 to provide mechanical interlocks for retention of the restoration within the tooth crown. Neither ion exchange nor other forms of adhesion were available.
5	 to extend the cavity to ‘self‐cleansing’ areas in an attempt to avoid recurrent caries, a concept called at the time ‘extension for prevention’.
In his recommended cavity designs, Black showed commendable respect for the remaining tooth structure wherever this was possible, but for the reasons outlined above it was necessary to sacrifice relatively extensive areas of enamel and dentine to achieve his goal of creating restorations that would last for several years, rather than a few months.
The result of these factors was that, regardless of the size of the lesion itself, a specific and prescribed cavity design was recommended for each class of lesion. The resultant cavities and restorations were, by today’s standards, very large.
Current knowledge offers alternatives to the approach that
Black described:
• earlier diagnosis of caries activity;
• effective control of the disease;
• healing through remineralization of early lesions;
• application of adhesive and bioactive restorative materials;
• better illumination and magnification;
• smaller instruments for cavity preparation.
At this time it seems it is clear that the system of lesion classification requires modification to bring it into good alignment with present‐day standards of care. In the presence of a better understanding of caries and other causes of loss of tooth structure, it is appropriate to consider alternative methods of describing and classifying carious and other lesions on the tooth crown.

### Alternative Systems for the Description

### of Lesions

Two different approaches to describing acquired lesions are described below:
• they recognize the variations in cavity design for differences in lesion size;
• they are not linked conceptually to particular cavity forms;
• they recognize modern understanding of the causes of loss of tooth structure.
The first approach to be described is designed primarily for use in dental teaching and for general dental practice, the second is an emerging system for practice, education and for epidemiological studies. Each has positive and useful features.

A description of lesions by Site and Size
A new method for describing lesions of the tooth crown and exposed root surface for use in dental teaching and in general dental practice was proposed in 1997 [2] as a ‘revised classification system for carious lesions by site and size’. Those words made an appropriate reference to the broadly‐accepted concept

of a ‘classification system’ as used by Black. It might now be appropriately termed ‘a method for describing lesions by Site and Size’, to distinguish it from the conceptual linkage element of the Black classification between ‘detection of a lesion and a prescribed cavity form’.
The method is based on the need to describe both carious and other lesions, such as abrasion, erosion, abfraction and trauma and at the same time to identify the increasing complexity of restorative techniques as the lesion enlarges. Advantages are:
• It allows the more accurate recording of a disease state than the Black classification, in particular, relative to the stage or size of the lesion.
• It offers guidance for appropriate management of lesions relevant to the present understanding of the disease and the use of restorative materials.
The classification therefore describes the presence of lesions by both Site and Size.

### Site

Carious lesions generally begin at three sites on the crown or root of a tooth. These are areas that are subject to the accumulation of plaque, where plaque can remain relatively undisturbed, and where both salivary access and flow are limited.
Therefore, the first parameters for the description are these sites are:
• Site 1 – pits, fissures and enamel defects on occlusal surfaces of posterior teeth, cingulum pits on anteriors or other smooth surfaces.
• Site 2 – approximal surfaces in relation to areas in contact with adjacent teeth, both anterior and posterior.
• Site 3 – the cervical one‐third of the crown or, following gingival recession, the exposed root surface.

### Size

Because caries is a progressive disease, and as preferred methods of management differ according to size as well as site, it is necessary to be able to define the size or extent of the lesion at the time of identification.
It is possible to define five distinct and clinically‐relevant sizes as the lesion progresses:
• Size 0 – the earliest identifiable lesion in the first stages of demineralization. This needs to be recorded and, if possible, the cause eliminated and the lesion remineralized and healed.
For historical purposes it can be further noted using a letter code: progressing [P], non‐progressing [N], or reversed [R].
• Size 1 – minimal surface cavitation with dentine involvement just beyond treatment by remineralization alone. Some form of restoration is required to restore the smooth surface and prevent further plaque accumulation.
• Size 2 – progress with moderate involvement of dentine. The remaining tooth structure is sufficiently strong to support a limited restoration.
• Size 3 – the lesion in dentine is beyond moderate. Remaining tooth structure is weakened to the extent that cusps or incisal edges may be split, or are likely to fail if left exposed to occlusal load. The cavity design needs to be modified so the restoration can provide support to the remaining tooth structure.
• Size 4 – extensive caries or bulk loss of tooth structure, e.g.
loss of a complete cusp or incisal edge, has already occurred.
Many corrosion‐abrasion lesions and most abfraction lesions occur in the same area as Site 3 carious lesions. As is the case with caries, these lesions will naturally increase in size over time unless the cause of the particular problem is addressed.
The system can therefore be used to describe the size of these lesions as well [3]. Attributing a size to them in a patient record will be helpful in monitoring any response to treatment over time.
This classification system is designed to support the identification of lesions from the very earliest stage of demineralization and to define the increasing complexity as the lesion extends in size. This level of identification is now relevant to clinical decisions in patient care and it provides guidance on how to restore more advanced defects. In particular, it makes it clear that the size of restoration should be determined principally by the size of the lesion to be restored, and not by the fact that a lesion has simply been detected.
The Site and Size description system is used throughout this book for three main reasons.
1	 It is a logical way of structuring descriptions of how best to manage lesions at different sites and of different sizes.
2	 It highlights through its structure the need for the ethical clinician to distinguish beween lesions that can be managed without cavity preparation and those that require the placement of restorative materials.
3	 It attempts to break the conceptual connection between detection of a lesion and the preparation of a cavity of a prescribed shape and size.

The International Caries Detection and
Assessment System (ICDAS) and the International
Caries Classification and Management System (ICCMS)
ICDAS, a system that classifies caries lesions based on the visual appearance of the lesion, was developed in 2002 in recognition that better quality information about caries detection, assessment and diagnosis was needed to describe the disease for practice, education, research and public health. It is a coded description of the status of each tooth surface. It is composed of a numerical grading system based on the depth of penetration of the lesion into the tooth and recording the status of the surface of each tooth or the condition of the restoration that is present [4].
The system was designed to improve consistency among dental examiners in epidemiological studies. It has been recognized that, for statistical purposes, there is a need for consistency in the detection and recording by code of carious lesions at particular stages of their development.
The ICDAS system forms part of an overall dental record protocol that:
• identifies the tooth using a numerical code system;
• identifies the tooth surface involved: distal, mesial, occlusal, buccal or lingual;
• identifies, with a numerical code, the surface condition or the restoration present on that surface;
• identifies, with a numerical code, the caries status (if any) of that surface.

### Surface condition or restoration code

0 – not restored or sealed

1 – partially sealed

2 – fully sealed

3 – tooth‐coloured restoration

4 – amalgam restoration

5 – stainless steel crown

6 – a crown or veneer

7 – lost or broken restoration

8 – temporary restoration

### Caries code

0 – sound

1 – first visual change in enamel

2 – distinct change in enamel

3 – enamel breakdown, no dentine visible

4 – underlying dentinal shadow (not cavitated into dentine)

5 – distinct cavity within dentine

6 – extensive distinct cavity within dentine

A particular surface is therefore coded with two numbers, the first for surface condition, the second for caries. 01, for example, is the code for a surface that has not been restored or sealed and which has the first visual signs of caries in enamel. Similarly, 13 is code for a surface which has been partially sealed and which has a visible carious lesion involving breakdown of enamel but not dentine.

### Missing teeth

There are also codes for missing teeth, for the epidemiological record:

97 – missing due to caries

98 – missing for other reasons

99 – unerupted

While the value of the system for epidemiological studies is readily apparent, there are limitations relative to the nature and needs of general dental practice.
The system is based on visual examination and assessment only, without the use of radiographs or explorers. Radiographs may not always be appropriate for epidemiological studies but of course are ethically essential for the detection of approximal and other lesions in general practice. Early detection of these lesions and early institution of appropriate remineralizing strategies can have beneficial effects for the patient, as is described in
Chapter 2 and elsewhere in this text.
The system makes multiple classifications of lesions and it may be difficult for clinicians to remain calibrated with the assessment. Also it may serve little purpose to the practising dentist.
For example, there are two classifications of caries deeply and obviously penetrating into dentin. However, the system does not suggest, either through the assigned code or any other part of the record, any guidance on subsequent clinical decisions that may be related specifically to site groups.
These limitations notwithstanding, the potental applications of the system in general dental practice at present are:
• as a numerically coded system for recording examination data for different stages of early carious lesions;
• to provide a means of comparison for changes of early carious lesions over time.
The ICDAS system has undergone a series of evolutions that have worked to develop a patient‐centred caries management system that can better aid in management decisions. The
International Caries Classification and Management System (ICCMS) has been developed to improve the decision‐making process and overall caries management. The seven ICDAS codes have been merged to four codes (sound, initial decay/ demineralization, moderate decay and severe decay). The coding of this system remains extensive, but is significantly simpler

than the ICDAS system and makes it somewhat more applicable to the needs of general dental practitioners and their patients [5].

### Conclusion

There is not yet one ideal system for describing all acquired lesions of the exposed tooth surface. Strong efforts are being made in that direction.
Over the last decade a number of authors, research groups and professional bodies have considered the topic and a number of variations of one proposed new classification have been published. The FDI World Dental Federation Science Committee collated a range of these in 2012 and suggested a ‘new model for caries classification and management’ in an attempt to consolidate existing concepts and develop a new and acceptable classification [6]. No system other than that of Black is as yet, however, widely accepted.
It is suggested that the following basic principles should be incorporated into any future method of description or classification of defects to be used in general practice:
• It should be simple and readily understood.
• It should identify the progress of a carious or non‐carious lesion from the earliest stage of ‘remineralizable’ to the stage where a protective restoration is required to support weakened and/or lost coronal tooth structure.
• It should not be related to, nor indicate, any specific restorative material.
• It should remain flexible and subject to modification as may be required by future advances in knowledge or technology.
