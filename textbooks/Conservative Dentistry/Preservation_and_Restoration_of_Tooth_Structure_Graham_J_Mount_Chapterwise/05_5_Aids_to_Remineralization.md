The mainstay of remineralization products has been fluoride. However, calcium and phosphate ions must also be present to allow mineral formation. Considerable attention has therefore been directed towards various strategies that can boost levels of calcium and other ions at the biofilm–tooth interface.

### Introduction

Dentistry has entered an era of personalized care using the medical model for caries management, where the aetiologic disease‐driving agents are balanced against protective factors.
In combination with risk assessment [1], this approach offers the possibility of individual disease prevention and management before there is irreversible damage done to the teeth and the need for restorative treatment [2, 3]. This approach requires differentiation between the caries disease process and the caries lesion, and a shift of focus from primary surgical intervention directed at the ‘cavity’ to a ‘disease prevention, reversal and tooth‐preservation‐first’ approach focused on disease management and remineralization [4].
The selection of a suggested therapeutic approach requires significant consideration. Not every product is the same because it contains the same or similar active ingredients. Selection must consider:
• clinical research providing evidence of clinical efficacy;
• bioavailability: the product must have the active ingredient;
• the product must be able to release it into plaque;
• potency: the active ingredient must be able to produce a change;
• substantivity: it must have therapeutic activity for a substantial period.
Always consider the age of the patient in selecting an appropriate vehicle for a preventive strategy. For example, the use of rinses or gums/lozenges is generally not recommended for safety reasons for very young children, who may not expectorate as required.
Numerous caries‐preventive therapies are available today.
The level of evidence of effect is variable, as well as their availability in different parts of the world and clinicians should take this into consideration when developing a personalized caries management plan. Options available to aid in caries management will vary when placing emphasis on the level of evidence supporting them. Two preventive strategies have the highest level of supporting evidence; topical fluorides and pit and fissure sealants. Sealants are discussed in Chapter 14. Fluorides and other non‐fluoride strategies will be discussed below.

### Fluoride Therapy in Dentistry

Mechanism of action: enhancing remineralization and inhibiting demineralization
The mineral in dentine and enamel is not pure hydroxyapatite.
It is a mixture of inorganic calcium phosphate compounds, including a number of carbonated apatites, with traces of proteins. Within the apatites, fluorapatite is less soluble in strong organic and mineral acids than hydroxyapatite, and this in turn has lower solubility than carbonated apatites. Fluoride assists the natural repair process for non‐cavitated caries

lesions on enamel and root surfaces because crystal remnants acquire a new surface that is less acid‐soluble than the original mineral.
It has been accepted for many years that fluoride can alter the properties of dental enamel, both as it forms and after eruption, to increase acid resistance. Fluoride therapy should be seen in the larger context of remineralization. This process requires that calcium and phosphate be present at or near saturation levels, and in the presence of fluoride as well, this can lead to the formation of fluorapaptite. However, it is important to remember that at the atomic level, less than 5% of the atoms (2 out of
42) that are involved in forming fluorapatite are fluorine atoms, indicating that other components, particularly calcium, will be the components that limit the rate.
For some time after tooth eruption, the enamel remains porous enough to absorb fluoride relatively easily, as well as salivary calcium and phosphate, a process known as post‐eruption maturation. Throughout life, fluoride can be absorbed readily at any site where the enamel is porous or there is ongoing demineralization‐remineralization. When acid production by dental plaque bacteria causes demineralization and increases the porosity of surface enamel, the uptake of fluoride is enhanced. If no additional acid is supplied, the buffering of saliva will result in a rise in the pH of the plaque. Then dissolved mineral remaining in the plaque will become saturated and will re‐precipitate on the tooth surface. In the presence of fluoride, the precipitated apatite will contain fluorapatite. The newly created fluorapatite requires a lower pH (a greater acid challenge) to dissolve it than before [5, 6].

Mechanisms of action against bacteria
The role of fluoride is clear, with both chemical effects at low concentrations and additional antimicrobial effects at high concentrations. The anti‐bacterial actions include:
1	 to impair glycolysis, improve energy utilization, and metabolic processes in plaque;
2	 to form hydrofluoric acid, which acidifies the interior of bacterial cells;
3	 to inactivate metabolic enzymes;
4	 to interfere with bacterial membrane permeability to ionic transfer;
5	 to inhibit the synthesis of intracellular polymers;
6	 to inhibit key enzyme systems such as enolase and ATPase.
High concentrations of fluoride (0.16–0.3 mol/L, equivalent to
3,000–5,600 ppm) will exert toxic effects on dental plaque, and thus provide a modest level of chemical plaque control, for a limited time period after application [7–9].

The intra‐oral fluoride reservoir
The principal benefits of fluoride derive from the presence of small (sub‐ppm) concentrations in plaque fluid and saliva over many hours. This alters the dynamics of mineral exchange in favour of mineral gain and encourages the formation of

acid‐resistant fluorapatite. The ingestion of fluoridated water and the use of fluoridated dentifrice will maintain low levels of fluoride in the oral environment for substantial periods of time.
In addition, glass‐ionomer cement restorations release fluoride and can be recharged when fluoride dentifrices or fluoride gels are applied to their surface.
Correct loading of the intra‐oral fluoride reservoir is an important therapeutic strategy for patients at high risk of dental caries or erosion, because this will ensure that mineral loss is minimized and that mineral gain is optimized. Fluoride can be used in various combinations and preparations, including neutral and acidic forms. High concentration fluoride therapies lead to deposition of globules or aggregates of calcium fluoride on the surface of teeth and in plaque, which then act as a short‐term reservoir of fluoride. The rate of fluoride release is enhanced at lower pH levels. A pH less than 5 causes loss of adsorbed phosphate, and triggers a slow dissolution of the calcium fluoride aggregates [10].

In‐office topical fluoride therapy
A range of fluoride‐containing solutions have been developed for topical use, including
• NaF – sodium fluoride
• APF, acidulated phosphate fluoride
• amine fluoride
• iron‐aluminium‐fluoride
• iron‐aluminium‐manganese‐fluoride
• stannous fluoride
• silver‐fluoride.
The latter three have been used to arrest caries on proximal enamel and cervical root surfaces, although problems with stability and discoloration have occurred with all three. Formulation changes now minimize this problem. Chemical modifications have included adjunctive use of potassium iodide after silver fluoride, and the addition of sodium hexametaphosphate to stannous fluoride.
In‐office topical fluoride applications employ various protocols to maximize benefits and reduce the risks of toxicity or other complications. Thin dental plaque biofilms can uptake fluoride but very thick dental plaque needs to be removed before topical fluorides are applied. Vigorous rinsing after topical fluoride applications in the dental office is not advised. Following application, calcium fluoride aggregates form rapidly in the first minute after application. For a high risk patient, fluoride varnish applied at 3–4‐month intervals is preferred, because aggregates form readily on tooth surfaces, and serve as a demineralization inhibitor. Also, surface uptake of fluoride can be enhanced by using acidulated fluoride products, although there are potential concerns with dental erosion of hard tissues and some restorative materials, as well as dentinal hypersensitivity. The low pH (∼2–3) and high titratable acidity of acidulated phosphate fluoride products can cause erosive damage to glass‐ionomer restorations and the glass‐based

­fillers in composite resin, as well as loss of glaze from porcelain restorations, particularly when used frequently. The low pH of acidulated fluoride products leads to a strong acidic taste, provoking marked salivation. This raises the risk of ingestion from inadvertent swallowing by children, so fluoride varnish is preferred for the younger age group.
Fluoride varnishes are an excellent means of achieving prolonged fluoride exposure of enamel or root surfaces affected by incipient caries. Clinical trials show reductions in caries incidence ranging from 18–70%. Fluoride varnishes contain the highest levels of fluoride of any therapeutic product (22,600–
25,000 ppm). A typical varnish with a concentration of 5%
NaF by weight gives 22,600 ppm of fluoride. Maximum benefit occurs with repeated application of varnish several times per year for the high risk patient. A frequency of every three months aligns with a suitable recall interval for high risk patients, such as those with profound salivary dysfunction.

Safety issues with in‐office fluoride applications
Fluoride can be delivered through numerous therapies (Table 5.2).
• low levels, parts per million (ppm) for the general community;
• higher levels, 200–5,000 ppm, for consumer‐level dental products;
• maximum levels, 20,000 ppm, for professionally applied products.
The increase in fluoride levels in the various products is associated with a corresponding reduction in the amount of product applied, so that the levels of fluoride ingested do not pose concerns for systemic health. At the one extreme, a dentist can apply microlitre amounts of a fluoride varnish to a tooth surface, while at the other extreme a patient could ingest a litre of fluoridated water as part of their normal lifestyle [14].
Fluoride mouthwashes, gels and all fluoride products for home use must be monitored carefully. Acceptable limits per application are 200 mL of mouthwash, or 30 mL of gel, and the excess expectorated after use. Potential for toxic effects is minimal, but all products should be stored securely with other medicines out of reach of children.
As a result of concerns regarding the potential ingestion, in‐office fluoride gels and foams should not be used for young children. At‐home fluoride products
Self‐applied fluoride therapies include dentifrices, mouthwashes, gels and therapeutic oral care products to which fluoride has been added. Fluoride concentrations vary and some are only provided by dentist prescription:
• 200 ppm in daily fluoride rinses;
• 400 ppm upwards in child‐strength fluoride dentifrice;
• 900 ppm in weekly fluoride mouthwashes;
• 1000 ppm upwards for adult‐strength fluoride dentifrices;
• 5,000 ppm upwards in high strength dentifrices and gels.
The evidence on efficacy of fluoride gels suggests that similar levels of caries prevention will occur with acidulated fluorides, sodium fluoride and stannous fluoride. As all such products enrich the levels of fluoride in the intra‐oral fluoride reservoir, the benefits of self‐applied fluoride therapies will add to those of fluoride delivered through the reticulated water system (typically at levels of 0.4–1.0 ppm).
Fluoride formulation in a conventional dentifrice may be sodium fluoride, monofluorophosphate (MFP), or stannous fluoride. Fluoride dentifrice, used regularly two or three times per day, provides fluoride ions in low concentrations to inhibit demineralization and enhance remineralization. They are expected to reduce the incidence of dental caries by 20–25%. A fluoride dentifrice is the mainstay of caries prevention and is recommended for patients of all ages, whether or not they live in communities with fluoridated reticulated water [15].

### Calcium‐Based Remineralizing Agents

The use of fluoride toothpaste has markedly reduced caries rates. However, remineralization of caries lesions can also play

an important role. Calcium‐based remineralizing technologies offer the targeted delivery of calcium in the right place and at the right time to the right individuals. For patients for whom current fluoride toothpastes are insufficiently effective, there is the alternative of targeted delivery of calcium to the interface between plaque and early enamel lesions [16]. Subsequent controlled release during acidic challenges may be an effective means of enhancing remineralization.
It has been assumed that, since plaque fluid is already supersaturated with respect to calcium phosphate at neutral pH, full remineralization may be difficult to achieve with extra calcium.
This may cause premature lesion arrest through surface‐zone remineralization at the expense of the lesion body [17]. However, at a cariogenic pH, lesions may be more porous to the ingress of mineral, and controlled release of calcium may reduce under‐ saturation with respect to enamel and accelerate deposition of fluorhydroxyapatite [17]. An additional benefit of an elevated calcium concentration may be an elevated plaque fluoride concentration because this concentration is dependent upon plaque calcium concentrations [18]. Calcium is needed for fluoride to be affective at promoting remineralization [19]. This means that the calcium supply may increase fluoride uptake to plaque when applied before fluoride [20]. Thus, even a modest increase in calcium concentration may enhance fluoride‐induced remineralization [20]. Many remineralization agents are designed to potentiate fluoride by elevating oral calcium concentrations.
This can be either directly, by delivery into enamel lesions, or indirectly, by elevating concentrations in plaque and saliva [16].
A number of remineralizing technologies based on calcium and/or phosphate agents with and without fluoride are currently incorporated into toothpastes, mouthrinses, varnishes, chewing gum and topical creams that are available to dental professionals
[21] and are discussed below.
It is important to note that any of these technologies, not combined with fluoride in the formulation, should only be used as an adjunct to fluoride use, particularly in high caries‐risk individuals.

Functionalized β‐tricalcium phosphate
The challenge in using calcium to enhance the effectiveness of fluoride in remineralizing caries lesions is to keep the calcium separate from the fluoride until they can jointly interact with the tooth surface. This is the basis of the strategy developed with functionalized β‐tricalcium phosphate (β‐TCP). There are two types of this system available in commercial topical dental formulations. These preparations include β‐tricalcium phosphate functionalized with sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) or alternatively, with fumaric acid (FA) [22–24]. The principal difference between these two systems is the degree of solubility that differs by a factor of almost 1000x.
By design, the SLS version binds strongly to the calcium polyhedra to limit the potential interactions with fluoride in a water‐ based NaF formulation [22, 23]. SLS is a known detergent that helps remove fatty substances. Casein phosphopeptide‐amorphous calcium phosphate
Casein phosphopeptide‐amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP‐
ACP) nano‐complexes in topical pastes, creams, rinses and chewing gum have been shown to promote enamel remineralization and provide anti‐cariogenic activity [29–32]. It seems they stabilize calcium and phosphate ions by formation of amorphous nano‐complexes. Also, calcium and phosphate ions from these complexes are biologically available to augment fluoride in inhibiting demineralization and enhancing the remineralization of initial caries lesions. Fluoride can be incorporated to provide casein phosphopeptide‐stabilized amorphous calcium fluoride phosphate (CPP‐ACFP) with similar characteristics. Both CPP‐ACP and CPP‐ACFP nano‐complexes readily dissolve in saliva [33] and become localized in plaque [34] where they enter the caries lesion fluid as an intact complex. Alternatively, they release the calcium and phosphate ions through equilibrium release, competition release, pH, and hydrolytic release, into the plaque fluid to then diffuse into the lesion. CPP‐ACP complexes that enter the lesion intact will release their complex‐bound ions that, together with ions diffusing directly from plaque fluid, are used in the formation of mineral consistent with hydroxyapatite or fluorapatite [35].

It is thought that the CPP‐ACP mechanism of action allows ions to penetrate more deeply, leading to remineralization throughout the body of the lesion rather than just in the surface layer. This improves the aesthetics [35] and strength of the lesion [36] and deposits acid‐resistant minerals, thus improving the resistance of this area to future acid challenges. The remineralization efficacy of vehicles containing CPP‐ACP has been identified in several in situ studies. These demonstrate that sugar‐free gum, containing CPP‐ACP, can promote net remineralization, even in enamel subsurface lesions under plaque‐laden gauze in the presence of diet‐induced demineralization challenges [31, 32]. Some existing clinical studies have shown that CPP‐ACP can slow lesion progression, promote lesion regression, prevent new caries initiation and provide additional caries‐preventive benefits when used in combination with fluoride [37]. However, there is not yet longterm clinical evidence for caries control. Topical dental preparations containing CPP‐ACP are commercially available to dentists as topical pastes, creams, rinses, varnishes and toothpastes.

Amorphous calcium phosphate
Technology using amorphous calcium phosphate compounds (ACPs) has been applied in the form of a dentifrice with a partitioned delivery system to separate the active ingredients. The tube contains sodium fluoride and phosphate on one side and a calcium component on the other side [38]. When applied intra‐orally, the salts mix with saliva and dissolve, releasing calcium and phosphate ions. The mixing results in the immediate precipitation of ACP or, in the presence of fluoride ions, amorphous calcium fluoride phosphate (ACFP). These phases (ACP and ACFP) are potentially very unstable and may rapidly transform into a more thermodynamically stable, crystalline phase such as hydroxyapatite and fluorhydroxyapatite and thus have lower substantivity. However, before phase transformation, calcium and phosphate ions may be transiently bioavailable to promote enamel subsurface lesion remineralization [39]. It was considered a prime candidate for remineralization therapy due to the high solubility under oral conditions and the ability to rapidly hydrolyze to form apatite [38]. A human clinical trial showed the partitioned dentifrice to be better than the conventional fluoride dentifrice in preventing root caries but not in preventing coronal caries [40].

Nano‐hyroxyapatite
Several nano‐technological approaches have been reported for remineralization of carious lesions. Biomimetic treatment of early lesions, through the application of various types of nano‐ sized hydroxyapatite or calcium carbonate, has been reported
[41–43]. Nano‐hydroxyapatite (nHA) is considered one of the most biocompatible and bioactive materials. Toothpastes and topical creams containing varying levels of nano‐hydroxyapatite (nHA) have been commercially available in Japan for over 30 years, and were approved as an anti‐caries agent in 1993 based on randomized anti‐caries field trials in Japanese school children
[44]. Nano‐particulate hydroxyapatite may act as a calcium

Arginine bicarbonate
Sodium monofluorophosphate dentifrice containing arginine bicarbonate complex, an amino acid complexed with particles of calcium carbonate, is commercially available for caries control and hypersensitivity treatment. When applied to the tooth surface, calcium carbonate dissolves slowly to release calcium, which is available to remineralize caries lesions. The release of carbonate as well as the metabolism of arginine in dental plaque and saliva will raise the local pH [52, 53]. A statistically significant reduction in dental caries was reported following 12 months use of dentifrice [54] and mints [55] containing arginine bicarbonate.

Calcium carbonate
Calcium carbonate provides an insoluble compound that has been applied either as an abrasive in sodium monofluorophosphate (MFP) dentifrice or as a nano‐particulate in dentifrice for biomimetic treatment of early caries lesions. An experimental dentifrice containing 1% nano‐sized amorphous calcium carbonate particles, applied twice a day over 20 days, yielded statistically significant mineral gain and remineralization of artificial caries lesions in an in vitro system [56]. The use of a lozenge that contained calcium carbonate in a clinical trial with human subjects resulted in elevated salivary level of calcium for the first
2–4 minutes only after subjects sucked the lozenge [57]. However, the efficacy of calcium carbonate in dentifrice, either as an abrasive or as a nano‐particulate, in enhancing remineralization and preventing demineralization, has yet to be proven in randomized clinical trials.

Dicalcium phosphate dihydrate
The effectiveness of fluoride in dentifrice products is greatly influenced by the compatibility of fluoride with other

ingredients. Dicalcium phosphate dihydrate (DCPD) is commonly used as an abrasive in both a sodium fluoride (NaF) and a sodium monofluorophosphate (MFP) dentifrice. It has a lower solubility and has been studied in clinical trials using chewing gum as well as in dentifrice studies. Caries inhibition was reported with the addition of varying concentrations (2–48%) of dicalcium phosphate dihydrate in flour, sugar, chewing gum or combined with either NaF or MFP in a dentifrice [58–63]. The inclusion in a sodium fluoride dentifrice resulted in a greater reduction of root caries compared with a conventional fluoride dentifrice [64] but there was no reduction in the coronal caries.
Enhancement of remineralization [65–67] and elevation of calcium levels in saliva and plaque [66, 67] were reported with the use of dicalcium phosphate dihydrate in a dentifrice or chewing gum containing NaF or MFP.

Calcium sodium phosphosilicate bioactive glass (NovaMin®)
NovaMin is calcium sodium phosphosilicate‐bioglass technology. Presently, calcium sodium phosphosilicate bioactive is available in a commercial dentifrice in two forms. It may be the sole active ingredient for treatment of hypersensitivity through physical occlusion of exposed dentinal tubules [68]. Alternatively it can be combined with NaF to aid in remineralization of caries lesions and/or prevent demineralization of tooth surfaces
[69]. The mode of action is based on the chemical reactivity with aqueous solutions. When introduced into the oral environment, the material releases sodium, calcium and phosphate ions which then interact with the oral fluids and result in the formation of a crystalline hydroxycarbonate apatite layer that is structurally and chemically similar to natural tooth mineral [69]. The calcium and phosphate ions are protected by glass and the glass particles need to be trapped for the calcium and phosphate to be localized. In an in vitro study, the combination of therapeutic levels of fluoride with calcium sodium phosphosilicate bioactive increased the remineralization of a carious lesion better than either of them used alone [69]. However, there is no clinical data supporting the efficacy of calcium sodium phosphosilicate bioactive, either alone or in combination with fluoride, in enhancing remineralization and preventing demineralization.

### Antimicrobials

Dental caries is a disease process driven by bacteria. Thus, it is not surprising that antimicrobial strategies have been developed to manage dental caries. The understanding of dental plaque and its role in oral diseases, including dental caries, has changed over the years. Dental plaque must now be considered a mixed bacterial biofilm [70]. New paradigms on caries aetiology must focus on ecological factors and multiple bacterial species, rather than just on the traditional caries pathogens [71].
Dentistry is evolving from the simple understanding that all plaque is pathogenic and needs to be removed (non‐specific

The present understanding is that dental caries is the result of an enrichment of plaque, driven by nutrients that encourage acidogenic and acid‐tolerant bacteria (ecological plaque hypothesis) [73].
An oral environment rich in sugar will result in acidogenic pathogens flourishing and a lower plaque pH. Over time, this will favour a change in the bacterial composition of plaque and the metabolism of the biofilm. This will promote the survival of those species that thrive in a low pH environment and, given suitable environmental conditions (e.g. sufficient fermentable carbohydrate), it will maintain a low pH.
As discussed in this chapter, caries preventive and management strategies include promotion of an appropriate diet and frequent removal of plaque. However, evidence suggests that oral hygiene alone, without the use of a fluoride toothpaste, is limited in caries prevention unless fluoride application is carried out professionally [74]. At the same time, modern dietary routines have led to higher and more frequent consumption of sugars and caries‐promoting foods. Recent studies have shown that, while the use of fluoride may counteract caries to some degree, there is a limit to its potential [71].
Even with regular fluoride use, caries can still develop if sugar intake occurs too frequently. In addition, as the population ages and more tooth roots are exposed, the potential for root caries increases due to the greater solubility of dentine to acid attack. As the root surface is dentine, with no enamel protection it is more prone to acid dissolution and the fluoride ion may be insufficient to prevent dental caries. This justifies the search for strategies to reduce the amount and virulence of dental plaque and could work synergistically with fluoride.
For caries, the question should be raised whether anti‐microbial therapy should be available in a toothpaste for daily use, or only administered professionally. Whatever the method, the aims should be:
• inhibition of plaque formation and/or growth;
• killing or reducing growth of specific bacteria;
• affecting virulence factors;
• modifying enzymes controlling acid formation and plaque
[71].
Anti‐microbials can be used to prevent caries (chemo‐prophylactic) or to treat the caries disease process when present (chemo‐therapeutic). Most of the research on the caries prevention potential of antimicrobials has been conducted on chlorhexidine or sugar alcohol‐based products. From the antimicrobial perspective, at this time there is evidence in the literature to support only chlorhexidine and some fluorides and xylitol to arrest caries and/or to stimulate remineralization. These are the ones discussed below.
Note that other approaches are being developed to keep the oral biofilm ‘healthy’ but there is limited evidence yet to support their regular use in practice. For example, attention is being devoted to extracts from plants and natural products [75].

Photodynamic therapy has been developed using a dye that is photo‐activated, leading to bactericidal properties [76]. Antimicrobial peptides in saliva and their synthetic analogues may have potential [77]. Replacement therapy has been researched for over 30 years, with cariogenic bacteria being modified to make them less acidogenic, while keeping their colonization‐ resistance properties [78]. Currently there is a product available on the US market (Evora) that introduces genetically modified bacteria for patient use. The application of probiotics, used in treatment of intestinal diseases, is now being investigated for oral diseases [79]. Most attention is given to the Lactobacillus species, in particular, those that produce bacteriocins, in milk‐ based formulations.
Biofilm research shows that bacteria in a biofilm interact through peptides to regulate their metabolism. Quorum‐sensing peptides are secreted, and when these exceed threshold concentrations, bacteria modify their physiology. Quorum‐sensing peptide‐like molecules have been tested to upset bacterial
‘societies’ [80]. Ozone is a potent antimicrobial, which can be delivered to the oral cavity using the Healozone instrument.
However, evidence is still questionable. In fact, according to the
Cochrane Collaboration, ‘there is no evidence that ozone therapy can reverse or stop tooth decay’ [81].
For each of these new approaches, questions remain about their efficiency and cost‐effectiveness, and converting them into usable products for caries management. Once formulated, there must be enough evidence to support their use in practice [71].

Chlorhexidine
Chlorhexidine is a non‐specific antimicrobial agent that is particularly effective in reducing dental plaque, especially the levels of mutans streptococci in saliva [82]. It is a strong base and, at low concentrations, it is bacteriostatic. At higher concentrations, it is bactericidal. It is effective on Gram‐positive and
Gram‐negative bacteria, fungi and yeast. Its efficacy is related to both concentration and frequency of application. Its substantivity is facilitated by its adsorption onto tooth surfaces, pellicle, plaque and mucous membranes. Local side effects may include surface staining of teeth, tongue and some dental restorative materials, as well as desquamation and soreness of oral mucosa and taste disturbances.
Chlorhexidine has been studied extensively both as an anti‐ plaque and anti‐gingivitis agent and for its potential to prevent and control dental caries. It is available in a variety of formulations including a mouthrinse, gel, varnish and toothpaste.
Concentrations vary from a 0.12% rinse, to varnishes ranging between 1.0–40%. Chlorhexidine varnish was developed to prolong the contact of the drug with the teeth and to provide its sustained release for increased effectiveness.
There have been many studies, and summaries of studies, on the anti‐caries effectiveness of chlorhexidine varnish [83–86].
For coronal caries the results are inconclusive. However, there is evidence to support its effectiveness in the treatment of root caries [87, 88] (Table 5.3).

In everyday usage the term sugar is reserved for what is known as sucrose, which is a carbohydrate. In fact, these organic compounds are divided into four groups: (i) monosaccharides (e.g.
glucose and fructose); (ii) disaccharides (e.g. lactose, maltose and sucrose); (iii) oligosaccharides; and (iv) polysaccharides.

### Xylitol dosage and safety

Clinical trials have shown a significant decrease in ­Streptococcus mutans load after five weeks of chewing gum containing xylitol as a sweetener. The minimal effective dose was around 6.5g per day, divided into at least three chewing periods [94, 95]. It was found that at 10g per day, the reduction of S. mutans is linearly related to the daily frequency of exposure [94]. Xylitol is safe at the above recommended dose, the main side‐effect of this, and other sugar alcohols, being a mild laxative effect. The threshold is about 50g per day, but with prolonged exposure these sugar

alcohols can become better tolerated and the threshold may increase.
Sugarless chewing gum is widely regarded as beneficial to oral health because it mechanically removes food debris, reduces plaque mass and stimulates salivary flow leading to an increase in salivary and plaque pH. Generally it is composed of a gum base (chicle), flavouring and a sweetener. The benefit of chewing gums that contain the appropriate amount of xylitol, and are chewed more than three times per day, is proven.
However, chewing gum is not a well‐accepted habit in some cultures. In this case, the use of xylitol‐containing lozenges is a viable alternative as long as the above daily dosage is observed.
Patients who are suffering from dry mouth may find sucking lozenges uncomfortable as the lack of saliva means that the rate of dissolution of hard candy is slow, so the daily use of a syrup may be another alternative [96].

### Conclusion

Based on the number of studies, the quality and the findings, fluoride interventions using vehicles such as varnishes, gels, rinses and toothpaste seem to have the most consistent benefit in preventing the development, and decreasing the progression and incidence of non‐cavitated caries lesions. The studies where interventions relied on the use of xylitol, chlorhexidine and calcium therapy vehicles alone, or in combination with fluoride therapy, are very limited in number and in the majority of the cases the evidence is still contradictory. Thus, these therapies should be considered as adjuncts to strategies with higher levels of evidence for caries control, such as fluorides and sealants.
