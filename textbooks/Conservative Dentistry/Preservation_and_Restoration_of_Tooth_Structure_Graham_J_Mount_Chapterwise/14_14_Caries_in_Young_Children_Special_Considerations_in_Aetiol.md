Newly‐erupted enamel is incompletely mineralized and is therefore at increased risk of decalcification. Salivary calcium and phosphate will, in time, complete the mineralization process, which can be augmented by the availability of fluoride in various forms.
However, the high metabolic demands of children often result in patterns of frequent eating, and if sucrose and other simple sugars form a significant part of the diet, the risk of developing carious lesions is high, particularly during the early years after tooth eruption, before mineralization is complete. ### Introduction

Despite preventive efforts worldwide, recent reports show that dental caries remains one of the most prevalent health prob‐ lems in young children [1]. Epidemiological studies suggest that caries in that age group has reached pandemic proportions with the highest rates encountered in low‐income, socially‐ disadvantaged, immigrant and Indigenous groups. In Australia, over 25% of children at childcare facilities and 80% of Indig‐ enous children have caries [2, 3].
Caries has significant impact on the child, the family and community [4]. It is the most common cause of toothache, oral abscesses, tooth loss and general anaesthesia for young children.
However, despite extensive treatment, many children with car‐ ies in the primary dentition show relapse, and continue to be at risk for caries in later childhood and adulthood. This is most likely due to persistence of the caries risk factors and lack of follow‐up.
Caries management and the restoration of carious primary teeth are influenced by special considerations that are encoun‐ tered in young children. Although the primary teeth are shed after a few years, they have important functions for aesthetics, mastication and growth and development of the permanent dentition. Many of the risk factors for caries, such as sugar consumption and oral hygiene, are determined by the mother or carer. In addition, the enamel and dentine of primary teeth are relatively thin so that caries reaches the pulp much faster compared to the permanent teeth. Furthermore, although the biological mechanisms involved in caries in young children are similar to other forms of caries, the early onset after tooth erup‐ tion and the rapid rate of tooth destruction suggest the presence of severe risk factors in some young children’s mouths that cre‐ ate highly favourable conditions for the teeth to be attacked by caries soon after eruption. In addition, at approximately 6 years of age, the newly erupted first permanent molars may show developmental enamel defects that render them highly suscep‐ tible to caries [5].
The aim of this chapter is to provide an overview of caries in young children from 0–6 years of age to highlight the special predisposing conditions in this age group and review current techniques for prevention and management.

Early childhood caries (ECC)
The term, ‘Early Childhood Caries’ or ECC is defined by the
American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry as the presence of one or more carious lesions in a child younger than 6 years of age [6]. As shown in Figure 14.1, ECC may present as a general type of caries that involves only a few surfaces with an onset after
2–3 years of age. It can also present as a rampant or severe ECC (s‐ECC) which involves multiple surfaces in children younger than 4 years of age [6]. As shown in Figure 14.2, s‐ECC usually attacks the smooth surfaces of the maxillary anterior teeth as well as the occlusal surfaces of first molars but the mandibular incisors are relatively spared.

At approximately 6 years of age, the caries risk in the child increases greatly with the eruption of the first permanent molars. The occlusal surfaces of the newly erupted teeth are par‐ ticularly vulnerable due to the immaturity of the enamel, and the presence of fissures that are highly plaque‐retentive.

### Special Predisposing Conditions for Caries

### in Young Children

In children aged 0–6 years, special conditions that predispose the primary dentition to caries include:
• early colonization with cariogenic bacteria;
• high frequency of sugar consumption;
• lack of regular tooth cleaning;
• developmental defects of enamel.

Colonization of the mouth with cariogenic bacteria
Mutans streptococci (MS), mainly the sub‐species Streptococcus mutans and Streptococcus sobrinus, are the main groups of cariogenic bacteria isolated from children with ECC [7]. MS
­constitutes over 80% of the biofilm flora in the most severe forms of ECC, compared to less than 3% in caries‐free children
[8]. Due to their strong acidogenic and acidophilic properties,

MS are considered one of the most virulent cariogenic microor‐ ganisms in the dental biofilm in all age groups.
There is now much evidence to support the fact that most children acquire MS from their mother, although acquisition from the father and siblings are also possible [9]. The risk of MS transmission to the children increases with increasing maternal
MS counts, and mothers harbouring over a million bacteria/ml of saliva have the highest rates of transmission [10]. Mothers who are at risk of infecting their children with MS usually have active dental caries themselves, poor periodontal health and engage in child‐rearing practices that promote the transmission of bacteria, such as sharing of eating utensils [11].
Initial colonization by MS in the child may occur well before the teeth emerge into the oral cavity, although the highest rates are found after eruption of the teeth [12]. MS colonization at all ages is strongly encouraged by the addition of:
• sugars to the feeding bottle and pacifiers;
• increased frequency of feeding;
• early weaning using sweet solids;
• lack of regular tooth cleaning [13].
The time of commencement of MS colonization and the quan‐ tity of MS present in a child’s mouth impact on caries risk.
Children who are colonized with MS at ages younger than 18 months are at higher risk of ECC compared to children who are colonized at older ages [13].

Dietary characteristics that promote caries in young children
It is now well established that a high frequency of sugar con‐ sumption plays a central role in the aetiology of ECC [14].
Severe ECC is often associated with a history of taking a sweet‐ ened feeding bottle to bed. During sleep, the sugar substrates from the bottle contents coat the teeth and promote the growth of acidogenic microorganisms in the dental biofilm. The thick‐ ness of plaque is increased in the presence of sugars and there is a shift of plaque pH to the acidic ranges that promote deminer‐ alization. When the child is asleep, the acid attack on the teeth occurs when protection from saliva is minimal so that the dem‐ ineralization effects become greatly magnified and prolonged.
In many children with ECC, most of the sugars associated with caries are found in drinks such as cordials and fruit juices that are consumed from a feeding bottle or drinking cup. After weaning, the sugars present in the solid foods become increasingly impor‐ tant in the aetiology of caries [13]. Many children with ECC report a habit of constantly consuming or ‘grazing’ with sugar‐ containing drinks and snacks. This pattern of high frequency of sugar consumption greatly magnifies the duration and intensity of acid formation by the cariogenic bacteria in the biofilm.
Breast milk and cow’s milk are associated with low cari‐ ogenicity as they contain only 5–7% of lactose. Animal studies have shown that rats fed constantly with milk do not develop caries [15]. Furthermore, milk provides protection from caries due to the presence of high concentrations of calcium and phos‐ phate ions that promote remineralization. Thus, fully breast‐fed

children should not be at risk for ECC unless the child con‐ sumes sugar frequently in other foods and drinks.

Lack of tooth cleaning
The presence of plaque on the primary incisor teeth and lack of toothbrushing have been consistently reported to be a risk indica‐ tor for ECC [3]. Although the efficacy of low dose fluoride tooth‐ paste (less than 500 ppm) in decreasing caries rates has not been established, recent studies have demonstrated that twice‐daily toothbrushing with low dose fluoride toothpaste can reduce the prevalence of ECC in a socially disadvantaged community [16]. In these studies, it is possible that the caries‐reducing effects of tooth‐ brushing may have resulted from the physical disruption of plaque and the removal of MS. Supporting evidence for this may be dem‐ onstrated by the fact that commencement of daily toothbrushing in young children who had acquired MS in their mouths resulted in removal of the bacteria after a few weeks of toothbrushing [17].

Newly erupted enamel
Newly erupted enamel has yet to undergo the post‐eruption accumulation of mineral, usually referred to as maturation of enamel, which is non‐pathological and chemically based. This process is quite distinct from enamel hypoplasia which is a path‐ ological state resulting in both chemical and structural defects at a macroscopic level. In the presence of other caries‐risk fac‐ tors such as high counts of MS, thick bacterial plaques, and high frequency of sugar consumption, newly erupted teeth are more vulnerable to demineralization compared with teeth that have been erupted for a longer period of time.
The hydroxyl apatite (HAP) found in matured enamel is in the form of [Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2], and that is the most stable form of apatite at a biological pH. However, during the formative stage, prior to eruption, the tooth germ is bathed in a body fluid that contains various ions, any of which can be incorporated into the crystallite structure of HAP as impurities.
The calcium ion has many possible substitutions by cations such as sodium, magnesium, strontium, and zinc. The phosphate can be replaced by carbonate while hydroxyl is normally replaced by fluoride [18]. The type and concentration of these substitu‐ tions represent impurities that can be inserted depending on the availability of the various ions during the formation of the enamel
[19]. Two of these impurities, carbonate and fluoride, deserve special attention because they affect the reactivity and solubility of the HAP and are present in sufficiently large concentrations in pre‐eruptive enamel and dentine. The acid solubility of the car‐ bonated apatite increases markedly in proportion to carbonate inclusion, while fluoride has a protective effect on HAP.
Upon eruption, the immature enamel begins a process of maturation that increases its resistance to external physical and chemical forces. The erupted tooth exists entirely in the presence of saliva and is subjected constantly to cycles of de‐ and rem‐ ineralization, during which carbonate is replaced by phosphate and hydroxyl by fluoride. Both ions are present in abundance in healthy saliva and plaque fluid. In summary, maturation of

Enamel hypoplasia as predisposing factor for caries in young children
Added to the immaturity of newly erupted enamel in young chil‐ dren, many primary teeth have developmental defects of enamel (DDE) that are currently recognized as one of the most signifi‐ cant predisposing factors for ECC [14]. DDE may present as an opacity, where the enamel surface is intact but shows altered translucency due to reduced mineralization. It can also present as enamel hypoplasia where there is deficiency of enamel that may be expressed as pits, grooves or absent enamel. DDE in the primary dentition arise as a result of adverse conditions occur‐ ring during the development of enamel. Thus, insults which are sustained by the developing enamel during the time period from 3 months in utero, when the primary incisors begin to form, until approximately 2.5–3 years, when the second primary molars erupt, have the potential to cause DDE in the primary dentition. These aetiologic factors include a range of prenatal, perinatal, neonatal and early childhood conditions such as [21]:
• maternal illnesses during pregnancy, e.g. pre‐eclampsia;
• metabolic conditions, e.g. hypocalcaemia;
• malnutrition, e.g. Vitamin D deficiency;
• infections, e.g. chicken‐pox;
• neoplastic conditions, e.g. leukaemia;
• drugs, e.g. tetracyclines.
Teeth with DDE have increased susceptibility to caries due to the loss of enamel, as well as the rough surfaces that encourage reten‐ tion of plaque and bacteria. There is often difficulty with tooth‐ brushing at these sites due to tooth sensitivity because of the lack of enamel. In addition, loss of mineral caused by bacterial acids is likely to be enhanced due to the prior lack of mineralization.
As DDE in the primary dentition are present in approximately
30–50% of children, they are likely to play a major role in the aetiology of ECC [21]. A form of s‐ECC that is strongly associ‐ ated with enamel hypoplasia has been recently assigned a novel name, hypoplasia‐associated ECC or HAS‐ECC [22]. A child with HAS‐ECC is shown in Figure 14.3. This condition is often encountered in children who are at high risk of enamel hypopla‐ sia, such as indigenous peoples from many parts of the world.
Newly erupted permanent first molars and incisors may show a type of acquired developmental enamel defect known as molar‐incisor hypomineralization (MIH) where the teeth appear chalky or show cloudy or demarcated creamy yellow or light brown opacities [23]. In molars that are severely affected, there is usually rapid loss of tooth structure soon after emer‐ gence and high susceptibility to caries. In addition, the affected teeth are usually very sensitive to hot and cold and there may be difficulty in achieving anaesthesia for restorative treatment.

The reported rates of MIH worldwide are variable, ranging from approximately 4–38% in Europe to 40%, 22% and 3% in Brazil, Iraq and Hong Kong respectively [24]. In Australia,
37–47% of first permanent molars have been reported to show enamel defects [25]. The systemic disturbances which cause enamel defects in the permanent molars and incisors probably occur between the period of birth to approximately 3 years of age. The aetiological factors for MIH are most likely the same as those already known to cause DDE in other permanent teeth.
These include neonatal conditions such as preterm birth, mal‐ nutrition, and common childhood infections such as chicken‐ pox, otitis media, respiratory and urinary tract infections.

### Prevention and Caries Management

### for Young Children

Dental care for children aged 0–6 years should be focused on caries prevention. For prevention to be effective, assessment and diagnosis of caries risk should commence at the children’s first visit to the dentist, which should occur by 12 months of age
[26]. After this initial oral examination, a risk assessment can be undertaken and a plan for caries management and prevention put in place [26]. A ‘dental home’ or regular preventive manage‐ ment by a dentist should be established for every child at the first dental visit.

General caries preventive strategies for young children
During pregnancy, mothers should ensure that their own oral health is optimal in order to reduce the risk of transmitting cari‐ ogenic microflora, such as MS, to their children. MS counts of the mothers may be reduced through:
• completion of restorative dental care;
• training in good oral hygiene;
• education regarding a healthy diet and reduced sugar con‐ sumption;
• application of remineralizing agents, such as fluoride;
• application of anti‐MS agents, such as chlorhexidine.
As soon as the child’s first teeth emerge, they should be brushed twice daily, using a soft toothbrush with a small head. A smear of children’s toothpaste, containing less than 500 ppm fluoride, may be used for children younger than 4 years of age, while older children may use a toothpaste with 1000 ppm fluoride.
Mothers should be counselled regarding good feeding habits for young children. Infants should not be put to sleep with a feed‐ ing bottle filled with liquids containing sugars. Infants should drink from a cup from the first year and should be weaned from the bottle between 12–18 months. They must avoid consum‐ ing liquids containing sugar, particularly sweetened fruit juices and soft drinks, in a baby bottle or training cup. Snacks given to children should not contain added sugar. Cheese, milk and unsweetened fruit and vegetables are ideal snacks for young children.

Identifying children at moderate to high risk for caries
The routine preventive care recommended for all children may be supplemented by additional preventive strategies for those children who are considered at moderate to high risk for caries.
For the purposes of caries risk assessment, the American Acad‐ emy of Paediatric Dentistry has proposed the following socio‐ demographic characteristics as moderate to high risk indicators for ECC:
• mother/carer has active caries;
• carer has low socio‐economic status;
• child is a recent immigrant;
• child has special health care needs;
• child has more than three between‐meal sugar‐containing snacks or beverages per day;
• child takes a bottle containing sugar to bed.
Clinical high risk indicators for caries include [6]: plaque present on the teeth, child has more than one carious lesion or active white spot lesion or enamel defects or MS counts of greater than
10,000 per ml saliva.
The effects of the high caries risk factors may be reduced by the presence of protective factors which include:
• regular dental care;
• consuming fluoridated drinking water or fluoride supple‐ ments;
• toothbrushing daily with fluoridated toothpaste;
• topical fluoride from health professional [6].
After assessing the balance of risk and protective factors, an overall caries risk level can be determined so that a caries man‐ agement plan, appropriate for each child, can be devised.

Caries management for moderate to high caries risk young children
In addition to these general preventive methods, 1–4‐year‐olds who are considered to have moderate to high caries risk should be recalled three‐monthly and the mothers counselled to assess the daily sugar frequency and toothbrushing effectiveness.
Incipient lesions should be monitored carefully as cavitation can occur rapidly in the primary dentition. Fluoride varnish may be applied on all tooth surfaces. Although fluoride varnish applied at ages 0–4 years increases the risk for fluorosis, the benefits of this preventive technique must be weighed against the risks.
The majority of children younger than 4 years of age will most likely require general anaesthesia for restorative work. Small carious cavities and enamel defects, such as pits in the anterior teeth, may be restored with composite resins or glass‐ionomer cements. In the primary molars of young children, stainless steel crowns are the restoration of choice for teeth which are carious or have enamel hypoplasia. These are durable restorations that are likely to survive the lifespan of the primary molars. Figures

Children aged 5–6 years who are considered to be at mod‐ erate to high risk for caries should be recalled at 3–6 monthly intervals, depending on risk level. They must be monitored for:
• daily sugar frequency;
• toothbrushing effectiveness;
• oral MS counts;
• progress of incipient lesions.
Bitewing radiographs should be exposed at 6‐monthly intervals as soon as the child is cooperative. Fluoride varnish or topi‐ cal fluoride gels may be applied at every recall. Fissure sealants should be placed on the occlusal and buccal/palatal pits as soon as the surfaces are exposed and adequate moisture control can be attained. Incisors with developmental enamel defects may be restored with composite resins. Hypoplastic first permanent molars may be temporarily restored with glass‐ionomer cements while they are emerging. When these teeth are fully erupted, stainless steel crowns may be placed to preserve them until a decision can be made regarding their retention for orthodon‐ tic reasons. To preserve the hypoplastic first permanent molars,

a conservative technique involving minimal tooth preparation and proximal tooth separation may be employed for stainless steel crown construction [27, 28].
Pulps that are vital in both permanent and deciduous teeth should be treated conservatively whenever possible. Indirect pulp capping is preferred over a pulpotomy as there are few pulpotomy agents which are ideal. In both dentitions the suc‐ cess of restorations depends on obtaining a marginal seal which protects the dentine and pulp from bacterial invasion. A stain‐ less steel crown cemented with glass‐ionomer cement is the pre‐ ferred technique for primary molar teeth after any form of pulp therapy.

Pit and fissure sealants for permanent molars
The following discussion on pit and fissure sealants is based on a previous review of this topic [29]. Readers are referred to this text for a description of other types of dental sealant, e.g. pre‐ fissure sealant and proximal/smooth surface sealant. These are novel ways of protecting erupting permanent molars and pre‐ venting proximal caries in children with high caries risk.
Epidemiological data suggest that dental caries in pits and fissures (Site 1) is a major issue in school‐aged children, with
90% of carious lesions occurring in the first permanent molars
[30, 31]. Pit and fissure sealants have been used both as primary and secondary preventive measures for many years and their effectiveness, particularly for high caries risk groups, has been demonstrated in many school‐based preventive programmes.
The desired outcome of sealants is to prevent the initiation and the progression of carious lesions. This means that the material used must provide good retention, long‐term seal and be both ‘technique‐tolerant’ and easy to place. Sealants may be applied using glass‐ionomer cements (GIC) or resins which may be filled or unfilled. As neither of these materials can be regarded as ideal and the differences between the two are pro‐ found, it is important for clinicians to understand their advan‐ tages and disadvantages, so the material that is appropriate for the clinical situation may be selected and inserted correctly.

Glass‐ionomer sealants
The main advantages of a glass‐ionomer cement are its ability to adhere chemically to both enamel and dentine [32], and its pro‐ longed release of fluoride [33]. Both resins and glass‐ionomer cements require a dry field for optimum retention and sealing efficacy and glass‐ionomer cements are more technique tolerant.
In an ideal situation, a rubber dam should be used. However, for many young children with limited cooperation, an acceptable alternative is a combination of cotton roll isolation, high speed suction and a well‐trained dental assistant using four‐handed dentistry. A glass‐ionomer sealant can be tooth‐coloured or pink, for ease of later identification. In situations where the tooth is partially erupted with the operculum persisting at the level of the distal marginal ridge (see Figures 14.8 and 14.9), a glass‐ionomer cement is the material of choice as good moisture control may be difficult.

### Glass‐ionomer sealants: placement protocol

The placement protocol includes the following steps:
1	 Remove debris from the occlusal surface with a slurry of pumice and water, using a brush or rubber cup, in a speed‐ reducing handpiece at 400 rpm (Figure 14.7). The pumice is then rinsed away using an air/water spray from a triple syringe for 10 seconds.
2	 Condition the tooth surface with an aqueous solution of
10–20% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds (Figure 14.7).
3	 Maintain a dry operating field during the sealing procedure using cotton roll isolation. This can be adequate in the major‐ ity of cases (Figure 14.8) but apply a rubber dam where pos‐ sible.
4	 Protect the immature glass‐ionomer cement with a thin coat of unfilled resin to prevent surface drying and crazing, that may readily occur within a short time, as shown in
Figure 14.10.

Resin sealants
Resin sealants will adhere to etched enamel through micro‐ mechanical interlocking. This process requires a material of low viscosity with good wettability on the tooth surface. As it is vital that resin sealants flow well and penetrate deeply into the pit and fissure systems of the tooth surface, an adhesive primer need not be applied. However, the use of a primer may improve resin sealant penetration into the pits and fissures and offer a long‐term seal [34]. This technique helps to overcome the dif‐ ficulty of resin penetration into the surface that results from the presence of organic material around the opening of the pits and fissures.
The current generation of resin sealants is still based on dimethacrylate technology, introduced to the profession by
Bowen in the 1970s so they contain either a bis‐GMA dimeth‐ acrylate or a urethane dimethacrylate. Clinically, resin fissure sealants can be differentiated based on filler content (filled or unfilled), appearance (clear, tinted, opaque or colour‐ changing), and mode of setting initiation (chemical or light cure) [29].There is no evidence to suggest that these variations have an influence on clinical performance so the choice remains with the operator.

Selection of materials
The routine use of glass‐ionomer sealants received guarded recommendation in a recent systematic review, with the com‐ ment that ‘Glass‐ionomer cement may be used as an interim preventive agent when there are indications for placement of a resin‐based sealant but concerns about moisture control may compromise such placement’ [37]. This statement may be related to the poor retention rates that were reported in some studies [38, 39]. It is important to recognize that the earlier studies used a first generation GIC fissure sealant, e.g. Fuji III (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), which has long been super‐ seded by later generations of GIC that have much higher phys‐ ical and mechanical properties. Most literature reviews rec‐ ognize the role of GIC sealants and there is strong anecdotal evidence to support its usage, especially under the conditions identified below.
Sealants are applied to prevent the initiation and progression of carious lesions on occlusal surfaces. There is evidence to sug‐ gest that glass‐ionomer sealants give superior protection against caries compared to resin sealants [40–42]. However, due to pre‐ viously reported high retention rates, most publications recom‐ mend that the material of first choice for a fissure sealant should be resin‐based [36, 37, 38] (Figures 14.17–14.21).
Based on current evidence, the following recommendations can be made:
1	 In caries‐active individuals, glass‐ionomer cements show caries‐preventive effects resulting from the long‐term fluo‐ ride release and the persistent ion exchange layer remaining on the surface after bulk loss.

5	 Repair of glass‐ionomer cements may be possible, at a later date, as there is chemical bonding between old and new glass‐ ionomer cements.
6	 GIC sealants are best suited for protecting erupting teeth when the sealant has to be inserted under the operculum.
Sealants are now well established as a method of caries preven‐ tion in pits and fissures of both primary and permanent molar teeth. They are particularly suitable on occlusal surfaces that have steep cuspal inclines and deep and complex fissures that are highly predisposed to caries. The acid etch technique using resins is highly efficacious, with high rates of retention and car‐ ies prevention in fully erupted teeth. In partially erupted teeth, the glass‐ionomer cements are the materials of choice as they are more moisture‐tolerant and release fluoride which will provide additional caries prevention. After placement, sealants should be reviewed regularly for integrity of the seal. They should be considered an important preventive strategy for all children in both the primary and permanent dentitions.
