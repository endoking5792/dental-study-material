### Introduction

Any material comprising a matrix surrounding at least one other phase is called a ‘composite’. In this regard, both dental amalgam and glass‐ionomer cements are ‘composites’.
Dental resin‐based composites (RBCs), also called composite resins, consist of a resin matrix that can harden through a monomer to polymer conversion plus filler particles, usually silica glass‐based, of different sizes, generally in the order of a few microns or less. After hardening, the resin matrix becomes bonded either directly or indirectly to the filler particles. In current usage, the materials can also bond micro‐mechanically to enamel and dentine.
As is the case with all resin monomer to polymer conversions, the resin shrinks on curing. The inclusion of filler particles reduces the overall degree of polymerization shrinkage of the material to what can be, with careful clinical handling and particularly in smaller restorations, an acceptable level.
When first introduced by Rafael Bowen in 1963, dental RBCs were referred to as ‘silica‐reinforced polymers’ [1]. Bowen demonstrated that it was possible to incorporate silica glass particles into a matrix of bisphenol A glycidyldimethacrylate (bisGMA), a high viscosity monomer, which could then be polymerized and hardened, via the catalyst benzoyl peroxide. The resultant reinforced polymer was sufficiently fracture‐ and abrasion‐resistant that it could serve as a dental restorative material.
BisGMA has continued to be used widely in RBC formulations since that time. A diluent monomer, triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA) was added to decrease the viscosity of the unpolymerized material sufficiently to allow flow and ‘packability’ during clinical placement. The glass particles could be treated to simulate tooth colour, and were given a silane coating to increase the wetting before curing. The improved wetting also improved the strength of bond between the resins and the silica glass filler after curing.
Subsequently, it was demonstrated that low‐viscosity RBCs could, before polymerization, wet acid‐treated enamel sufficiently well before polymerization that they adhered to it after hardening, a clinically useful phenomenon called ‘enamel bonding’. Enamel bonding had earlier been demonstrated with acrylic resins after acid treatment of enamel by Buonocore in
1955 [2]. Acid treatment of enamel, wetting it with low viscosity resin, then placing a higher viscosity RBC over the bond enabled several new approaches to restoration of defects in tooth structure. These included fissure sealing, aesthetic veneers over discoloured enamel, and the restoration of fractured incisal edges of anterior teeth. More recently, systems that also allow micromechanical bonding to dentine have been developed.
Bonding of RBCs to dentine is less strong and less reliable than to enamel, but it has become clinically relevant and is now widely used.
Resin‐based composites, as a class of dental restorative material, with their many minor variations, are not the ideal direct material for all the different restorative needs of a patient, but to

many patients they are more aesthetically acceptable than dental amalgam. They also avoid the environmental concerns about mercury that are linked to the use of amalgam [3]. They are stronger than glass‐ionomer cements under occlusal or incisal load, so they are likely to remain, for some time, a significant part of restorative practice.
Resin‐based composites, placed in appropriate tooth preparations and managed properly, have the following positive features:
• aesthetic qualities that imitate variations in shade in the original tooth under varying light conditions;
• adhesion to tooth structure, with the ability, under ideal conditions, to create both an immediate and long‐lasting seal at enamel margins;
• conservative cavity designs because their adhesive qualities allow preservation of sound tooth structure;
• the ability to seal pits and fissures, eliminating the need for
‘extension for prevention’ in cavity preparation;
• the capacity to be repaired and/or resurfaced without the need for complete restoration replacement.
RBCs also suffer from several shortcomings. They shrink on curing, which creates challenges in developing and maintaining an antibacterial seal at the interface between the restoration and tooth surface. Their surface degrades slowly by hydrolysis over time and they tend to take up extrinsic stains. However, with careful clinical use, the effects of these shortcomings can be minimized.

### Composition and Use of Resin‐Based

### Composites

Resin‐based composites, when used as direct placement restorative materials, are provided as a paste that is a combination of dimethacrylacrylate‐based resin monomers or oligomers and filler particles. The resins, when polymerized, provide a highly cross‐linked polymer network that is strong and of low solubility. The most commonly used monomers are BisGMA,
TEGDMA and urethane dimethacrylate (UDMA). BisGMA is highly viscous as a monomer, and wets filler particles poorly, but provides good strength when polymerized. TEGMA, a smaller molecule, is used to reduce the viscosity of the mix. Its wetting properties allow higher filler loading, increased strength overall and lower shrinkage than would otherwise be the case, as well as increased clinical usability.
Two or more reactive groups are necessary on each monomer molecule to provide a cross‐linked structure. Cross‐linking of the molecules causes the material to transition from a fluid to a solid state, and is accomplished by free radical addition polymerization. During this polymerization, shrinkage of the mass occurs. The volume of the polymerization shrinkage is largely negated by the filler particles. The higher the molecular weight of the dimethacrylate monomers, the lower the volume change occurs during polymerization. Alternative monomer systems,

The resin matrix makes the resin‐based composites largely hydrophobic, but RBCs do absorb small amounts of water over time. Thus, they are not fully stable over many years of clinical service, and surface degradation over time is to be expected.

Unreacted monomer, particularly the lower molecular weight
TEGDMA, leaches out of RBCs, immediately after clinical placement, in concentrations sufficient to cause inflammatory responses in the dental pulp.
The filler particles are generally radiopaque silica glasses with barium or strontium additions, and in some cases zirconia/silica glasses [5]. They vary in size, and are all coated with silane to allow better bonding with the resin when it is polymerized. The coated particles, if sufficiently small and of sufficient quantity to create a high level of filler loading, add important physical properties to the resin composites
­including:
• higher strength (compressive, tensile and to a limited degree, fracture toughness);
• higher elastic modulus (stiffness approaching or in the range of dentin 10–20 GPa);
• reduced polymerization shrinkage (in the order of 1.5–3.0% by volume);
• reduced coefficient of thermal expansion (20–40 ppm/°C);
• improved wear resistance. Generalized wear of resin composites is in the order of 10–20 μm per year, which is similar to enamel wear.
• light scattering, adding tooth‐like aesthetic properties.
In general, the higher the filler particle loading, the better the resin composite’s physical properties. Most RBCs in clinical use have filler content in the range of 50–80% by weight, or 30–65% by volume.
Health authorities in a number of countries are trying to phase out silver amalgam use due to concerns associated with mercury accumulation in the environment. Consequently, composite resin materials have been widely advocated as a potential replacement for silver amalgam in the restoration of defects in tooth structure in posterior teeth, in some cases since the early
1970s.
RBCs are well suited to restoration of defects in anterior teeth, in particular, in areas where there is incisal load. Some
RBC restorations have served well for a decade or more. However, many of the early posterior restorations failed and have required replacement. It is useful to understand the history of the clinical development of these materials both to understand the reasons why some RBC restorations continue to fail, and to reduce the incidence of such failures in the future. There is now every reason to predict good longevity for RBC restorations that are placed well in appropriate situations, with proper cavity

design. Fastidious moisture control, ideally with a rubber dam is essential. A brief review of the history of the clinical use of RBC is useful in this regard.

Enamel bonding for RBCs is described in detail in a subsequent section of this chapter.
Initially, RBCs were available as a two‐paste system, and required mixing to initiate polymerization prior to placement.
The mixing of two pastes had the potential to create porosities and voids that compromised the aesthetics, marginal seal and wear resistance of the restoration. To overcome this problem and to simplify clinical usage, photo‐initiation of curing was introduced using an intense ultra‐violet light source. This avoided mixing of two pastes with incorporation of porosities and allowed precise control of the timing of polymerization.

The limited depth of penetration of the UV curing light led to the need to place the material in shallow incremental layers, in the order of 1.0 mm or less. Subsequently, curing using a white light source with a wavelength between 400 and 500 nm was developed. This allowed the material to be placed in layers of up to 2.0 mm maximum and then shaped prior to polymerization rather than having to accomplish most of the contouring with a rotary cutting instrument after the restoration had polymerized.
Finally, a single paste, light‐cured formulation was developed and this could be placed almost porosity‐free.
Post‐operative hypersensitivity (hypersensibility) was a common problem with early RBC restorations. Initially this was overcome by the placement of a cement base, usually a dual‐ mix calcium hydroxide‐salicylate cement, and later, when glass‐ ionomer cements (GIC) became widely adopted, by the use of a GIC. Early post‐operative hypersensitivity, in the days and weeks after placement, was probably due to the release of unreacted TEGDMA into and through dentine, and this damaged pulpal cells. Hypersensitivity, in the later months and years after placement, was probably due to bacterial micro‐leakage. The use of a liner or base reduced or avoided these problems. Glass‐ ionomer cement with high tensile strength is now the ideal base material for use under RBCs.
With good moisture control, it became possible to create satisfactory RBC restorations for carious and other minor defects in anterior teeth, even without the need for a liner or base. Acid etching for 10 seconds of both enamel and dentine in small restorations allowed the development of better adhesion, and less overall micro‐leakage. This was advocated by some clinical teachers as ‘total etch, total seal’, something that was theoretically possible in the restoration of very small cavities. However, it was less likely to be successfully achieved with increasing defect size, because of the unavoidable effects of bulk shrinkage of RBC on curing. With larger restorations, the use of a GIC base before RBC placement gave a much better chance of preventing access of the oral microflora to dentine, and reduced the risk of pulpal irritation and inflammation and, in some cases, pulpal death.

Posterior RBC restorations
Given the aesthetics, strength and wear resistance of the resin‐based composites, the profession began to explore their use for posterior restorations while, in most cases, continuing to use the style of cavity preparations described by Black for amalgam. The significant volume of material within these posterior multi‐walled tooth preparations made the effects of RBC polymerization shrinkage still more apparent. If not carefully controlled, moisture could also compromise the bonding to etched enamel. The total shrinkage could open an enamel margin, particularly in the gingival portion of a deep proximal box where there may be little or no enamel remaining. While not apparent at the time, phosphoric acid etching of the cavity surface to create the conditions necessary for enamel bonding removed the dentine smear layer and opened dentinal tubules and allowed dentine fluid flow. Polymerization shrinkage can create gaps between the resin bonded composite and the dentin but not necessarily at the enamel margin [8]. The gaps may fill with dentine fluid and, with mastication, the tooth and restoration deformation can cause a reverse fluid flow into the tubules, eliciting a pulpal response. The result was termed ‘occlusal loading sensitivity’ [9]. Application of a liner or base to protect the dentine, prior to etching the enamel, helped reduce this problem, just as it reduced the other forms of hypersensitivity described above.

In general, the resin‐based composite formulations at that time could be properly polymerized at a conversion rate of 60–70%, to a depth of 2 mm or less. ### Wear: clinical findings

Since the 1970s, a great deal of effort has been expended in determining how filler loading and filler particle size influence clinical wear of resin bonded composite restorations. During this time the size was reduced and the size range of the filler particles was increased.
Wear can be generalized across the surface and is more apparent at occlusal and approximal contact areas. Once manufacturers were able to develop composites with filler particles below
5 μm in size and increase the loading to above 60% by weight, wear rates were reduced. This is attributed to the inter‐particle distance being sufficiently small that resin between the particles is protected from wear caused by a food bolus or the opposing tooth structure [10]. Overall, wear is now a limited clinical problem, even over periods as long as 22 years [11]. The width of the cavity preparation directly influences the wear rate over time. The wider the preparation, the less the enamel protection, the greater the load, and therefore the amount of loss from abrasion, foods and the opposing dentition [12].
In one large clinical study, multi‐surface posterior resin‐ based composite restorations had a long‐term performance over 10 years equivalent to, or better than, amalgam, depending upon the caries risk classification of the participants [13].

Recent resin‐based composite formulations have demonstrated wear characteristics similar to dental amalgam.

RBCs intended for restoration of posterior teeth or ‘universal application’ contain high filler loadings to achieve high strength and wear resistance and to reduce polymerization shrinkage.
However, if the filler load is too high, the material becomes stiff and dry, making it difficult to adapt to the cavity walls.
The polymerization shrinkage of the original versions of the
RBCs ranged between 2.2–3.0% by volume. Recent versions show polymerization shrinkage reduced to the range 0.9–1.7% by volume. While apparently an improvement, these have yet to be shown to have a clinical advantage. Currently, minimization of the polymerization shrinkage is best accomplished by placing the restoration in small increments and in thin layers, no greater than 2.0 mm, initiating curing in each layer for the full advised time of light exposure before placing the next. This ensures as complete a polymerization as possible in each increment and allows for shrinkage of a small bulk within each increment.
Careful construction of a resin bonded composite restoration must therefore be carried out incrementally. Once the cavity design is completed and the glass‐ionomer cement base is in place, both enamel and dentine should be etched with 10% orthophosphoric acid for 10 seconds only and then covered with a thin coat of a resin bonding agent. This must be light‐ activated. Small increments of the resin composite, no more that
2.0 mm thick, can then be placed and light‐activated one increment at a time. Build carefully on one side of the cavity, and then the other, to allow the curing shrinkage to be limited in area and to minimize the stress developed across the tooth crown.
This will reduce the tendency to pull the resin from one or other margin, thus minimizing marginal leakage.
Subsequent layers bond to the prior layer by attaching to an unpolymerized air‐inhibited surface layer on the prior layer.
When the next layer is added, the oxygen is cut off from the surface and the layer polymerizes. Final increments should be individually contoured to reduce the need for final contouring and polishing with disks and burs. Properly performed, the layering process will reduce cusp deflection, microleakage at the margin and post‐operative sensitivity [21].
‘Flowable’ RBCs are less heavily filled and fall within the range of 60–70% by weight, that is 35–45% by volume. This will lower the viscosity and allow more convenient application to small cavity preparations and non‐carious cervical lesions [20]. They are popular for those applications where polymerization shrinkage and wear are less important. Flowable RBCs are also employed by some clinicians as liners under a posterior restoration in the belief that they provide better adaptation and will reduce voids, thus reducing post‐operative hypersensitivity and, in the long term, reduce secondary caries. This contention has been questioned as a laboratory study indicates that use of the flowable material, unless left uncured before application of heavily filled
RBC, does not reduce voids [22]. Practice‐based research suggests that use of a flowable RBC ‘liner’ does not reduce postoperative hypersensitivity in a Class I restoration [23].

Classification of RBCs by filler particle size and loading
Differences in filler particle size and loading percentage will influence the strength, shrinkage, polishability, and optical properties of RBCs.
The convention is as follows:
• macrofill (particle size > 10 μm)
• midifill (particle size < 10μm > 0.1 μm)
• microfill (particle size < 0.1μm > 0.01 μm)
• hybrid (combinations of macro, midi and/or microfill filler particles)
• nanofill (particle size < 0.01 > 0.0.001 μm).
The finest particle size hybrids demonstrate the best potential polishability, while the macrofill composites have the greatest likelihood of wearing away opposing tooth structure. The hybrid combinations afford the greatest potential for wear resistance, exceptional polishability as well as light reflectance and shade matching [18].

### Enamel Bonding

Bonding of RBCs to enamel is entirely micro‐mechanical. The enamel surface should be etched with phosphoric acid for 15 seconds then washed thoroughly to remove all etchant and debris. Acid etching results in selective dissolution of the outer surface of the enamel rods leading to the development of micro‐ porosities of up to 20 μm in depth and a greatly increased surface area (see Figure 10.12). A low viscosity resin is then applied, wetting the etched enamel surface with resin. The resin envelops the enamel rods and offers a micro‐attachment to them at some depth.
Initially, and very successfully, hydrophobic resin bonding agents have been applied to air‐dried, acid‐etched enamel. More recently, in order to make clinical procedures as simple as possible, more hydrophilic resin primers have been developed for use in dentine bonding, see below, and have been advocated for enamel bonding, treating enamel and dentine in the same way throughout the process.
Although resin bonding to enamel can be both reliable and strong there are a number of factors that can reduce its efficiency:
• poor clinical techniques;
• contamination after etching with saliva or crevicular fluid;
• microcracks in the enamel;
• unsupported or fractured enamel margins.

### Dentine Bonding

Dentine bonding agents (DBAs) currently available fall into three general categories: ‘three‐step’, ‘two‐step’ and ‘one‐step’.
This designation corresponds to the number of components and the number of separate applications to dentine involved.

All dentin bonding agents depend upon phosphoric acid etching for removal of the smear layer created on enamel and dentin during cavity preparation. The smear layer is a 3–5 μm‐ thick layer, loosely bound to the underlying dentin or enamel surface and comprised of disrupted tooth structure; debris of hydroxyapatite particles, collagen fibres and dentine tubule contents (see Figures 10.13–10.15).
As the dentinal tubules are opened during the etching process, dentine fluid within the tubule becomes an issue. To be effective, dentin bonding agents must be hydrophilic, in order to penetrate the dentinal tubules, which are full of tissue fluid. Hydrophilic components, such as alcohol or acetone, are included in the bonding agents to assist in the drying process and they must first be carefully evaporated, prior to placement and polymerization of the bonding agent. A hydrophilic resin, hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA), is used in many dentine bonding systems as a primer.

Drying dentine with air prior to placement of a primer can cause significant and enduring post‐operative sensitivity, so manufacturers have developed dentine bonding systems that move from one liquid phase to another without the need for air drying. These systems are called ‘moist’ or ‘wet’ bonding, but it should be clearly understood that this does not imply wetness with either saliva or crevicular fluid. Contamination with either prevents bonding to dentine. For this reason, regions where strict isolation, preferably

with a rubber dam, cannot be maintained, including at the gingival margins of cavities, contra‐indicate the use of bonded RBCs alone.
In those circumstances, the use of a glass‐ionomer cement base and the lamination or sandwich technique described above can be a useful adjunct to creating a satisfactory restoration.
Dentine bonding agents depend upon penetration into the prepared dentine surface, ideally to create what has been termed a ‘three‐dimensional zipper’ effect. As the resin infiltrates tubule openings in the dentine surface and is then polymerized, something analogous to resin penetration into etched enamel [24] may occur. Currently, dentin bonding agents require an acid etching technique in which dentin and enamel are etched at the same time by the same agent, such as 10% orthophosphoric acid. Table 10.1 describes, in summary form, the eight ‘generations’ of dentine bonding systems that have been marketed to the present time [25].
In contrast with these eight ‘generations’ of dentine bonding approaches, over a similar time period, there has been only one generation of enamel bonding, and one generation of glass‐ionomer cement chemical adhesion. The latter two adhesion mechanisms have been strong and reliable throughout.
An important requirement with dentin bonding agents, often not appreciated, is the need for longer curing times in deep cavity preparations. Insufficient curing reduces the bond strength, making polymerization shrinkage more likely to cause lack of adhesion along the pulpal wall. With incomplete polymerization, uncured monomer may also diffuse through dentine to the pulp and cause inflammation. Both of these consequences can lead to post‐operative hypersensitivity.

### Changing cavity designs for RBCs

The advent of relatively reliable dentin bonding has dramatically changed cavity design (see also Chapter 7) because some of the bonds have initial shear and tensile strengths approaching those to etched enamel [26, 27]. Adhesive‐style cavity designs allow a reduction in the opening to a caries lesion thus preserving enamel and can be positioned on the tooth to allow direct access to the defect. Based upon preservation of tooth structure through bonding, the American Dental Association in 1998 recommended that
RBC was the material of choice for initial lesions in posterior teeth
[28]. In the United States, since 1999, the number of posterior resin‐based composite restorations has surpassed the number of silver amalgam restorations [29].
Cavity preparation designs have changed. It is no longer necessary to decide the restorative material required prior to beginning the preparation. The initial surgical procedures should be focused on the removal of carious tooth structure and any unsupported enamel.
The Black cavity design system then required extension of the cavity to provide for mechanical retention of the restoration and to include areas of disease risk, such as the pits and fissures. This is now regarded as unnecessary in the presence of adhesive materials because these areas can be sealed, reducing the risk of developing caries.
After removal of the appropriate amount of caries, as is described in detail in Chapter 12, the preparation must be evaluated for several conditions to determine the ideal restorative material to be placed for restoration:
• the ability to isolate the cavity preparation from saliva contamination during placement of the restoration;
• the ability to obtain a tight contact with the adjacent tooth;
• the ability to restore correct tooth morphology;
• size and complexity of the final restoration.
The ability to achieve a tight approximal contact with RBC is a significant challenge. RBCs, unlike amalgam, do not have high packability. Even with modification of the filler particles, the
RBC materials flow under compression rather than resist. Simple and adequate condensation of the material will not achieve the tight contact necessary to maintain periodontal health and to prevent food impaction.
The restoration of the approximal contact requires care to develop the appropriate contour and some form of tooth separation is necessary to compensate for the thickness of the matrix material. Translucent matrix retaining bands are often too flat, and metal matrix bands are too thick, to develop appropriate approximal contours. The matrix should be convex from the gingival margin to the contact area and the contact should be in the occlusal/incisal one third of the tooth crown. Even with the use of a thin metal matrix band, the use of a matrix retaining devices such as a Tofflemiere retainer may lead to a flat contact. Separation can be achieved with a wooden wedge at the gingival base of the matrix and the use of specially designed clamps or separators will hold the matrix in place.

### Adequate light curing

Light curing of RBC requires delivery of an adequate level of energy (irradiance) to the material at a wavelength within the

range of 410–470 nm [30]. The minimum acceptable light output for an activator light is 400 mW/cm2 and many lights can deliver much higher intensities, that should be checked regularly..
A 2.0 mm‐thick increment of a resin‐based composite can be cured in 10 seconds with this output, providing that the light tip is not more than 2.0 mm from the surface of the restoration.
Given the inverse square law of light intensity with distance, with the light tip at 4 mm vs. 2 mm, the curing time would be 40 seconds [31]. This means that care must be taken when curing RBC, or resin bonding agents in deep preparations, to increase the cure time. In addition, as the light output can vary across the light tip, small movements of the light source while curing is recommended. The curing area in large restorations should be overlapped during placement especially when placing a three‐surface restoration in a posterior tooth [32].

### Finishing and polishing resin‐based composite

### restorations

Achieving a smooth and highly polished surface on a restoration is critical both for aesthetic purposes and to reduce plaque retention.
Polishing is the process of progressively reducing finer and finer scratches on the surface of a restoration until the surface develops a high gloss. Multiple systems have been developed to polish composite resin surfaces. Coarse diamond or tungsten carbide burs can be used to grossly reduce and shape the restoration. If the restoration is properly placed initially, the need to perform gross recontouring will be minimal. Diamond burs are used with graduated grits followed by rubber cups of different shapes, and polishing pastes of graduated coarseness to establish a high gloss on the surface [33].

### Conclusion

Resin‐based composite restorations are now frequently placed in dental practice. They offer the advantages of being highly aesthetic on initial placement and mercury‐free, and these features means that a patient may select this material as the restoration of their choice. However, it is essential that the operator offers proper information and assists in the final choice.
Resin‐based composite restorative materials can bond to tooth structure and offer the practitioner the option to minimize restoration size, thereby reducing the amount of tooth structure removed during the cavity preparation.
The materials are very technique‐sensitive and require careful and skilful handling. Fastidious moisture control, ideally using rubber dam, is essential for the creation of an effective bond to enamel or dentine.
