Dental caries is a common, preventable disease that can have significant financial and quality of life costs for both the individual and the community. Assessment of caries activity at the time of examination;
determining which factors are most probably causing the activity; and assessing the risk of a patient developing the disease in the future are all key steps toward the provision of good oral health care. Together these actions are called ‘activity and risk assessment’.
With such assessment it becomes possible to accurately align a patient’s preventative, non‐surgical curative and surgical repair treatment to their true needs.
Administering expensive preventive therapies to an individual who does not have or is never likely to develop caries is an expenditure that has no chance of being recovered in future cost savings. Conversely, not providing such therapies to the fullest extent possible for an individual who either has active disease or is likely in the future to develop such disease can have severe, long‐ term deleterious consequences.
Caries activity and risk assessment, as applied to an individual, is a method of designing a customized patient‐centred oral health care plan that matches patient need to best‐evidence‐based care. ### Introduction

The recognition in the late nineteenth century and early twentieth century of dental caries as a disease related to bacteria led to concepts of its aetiology and management that were relatively simple, but that unfortunately gave little guidance to effective prevention or cure. Descriptions of the disease followed the model used at the time for other infectious diseases, where a single pathogen was implicated as the cause. At the same time there was recognition that caries was related in some way to dietary behaviours. The concept therefore arose that a single bacterial species in saliva, lactobacilli, broke down sugars from food remaining on the teeth to make acids that dissolved the adjacent tooth mineral. Since oral bacteria were ubiquitous, and since eating food is essential for survival, all individuals were assumed to be at high risk of the disease. With that concept of the nature of the disease, removing food from the teeth after eating, by rinsing or by using a toothbrush, was the only logical approach to disease prevention. Unfortunately it had little effect. Nor did the concept provide a logical path to cure of the disease once it was present because, as already noted, bacteria were ubiquitous, and food is essential to survival. The only logical treatment options for the disease were surgical, by either tooth extraction (which did cure the disease once all of the teeth were gone) or debridement of the lesion and filling the resultant defect (which did not cure caries – the restorations failed if the disease state continued).
During the second half of the twentieth century oral microbiological research and the recognition of the probable role of dental plaque, or biofilm, on tooth surfaces in caries initiation and progression led to the specific plaque hypothesis. This suggested that relatively few bacterial species within biofilm, such as mutans Streptococci and lactobacilli, caused dental caries.
These species were commonly found at higher levels in the biofilm of individuals suffering from active dental caries. The introduction of fluoride in water supplies, and then through toothpaste marketing in the 1960s, reduced caries rates in some communities and in consequence some individuals became and remained caries‐free. The specific plaque hypothesis provided a rational basis for attempts to reduce caries rates in those patients who, despite exposure to fluoride, still had the disease.
Efforts to prevent caries became focused on removal of dental plaque from teeth, and this was an improvement over the food removal strategy. Plaque control strategies were proving very effective in the prevention and treatment of periodontal disease. However, this strategy proved to be inadequate either for predictable prevention or for the cure of caries, probably because complete biofilm removal is extremely difficult to achieve and maintain.
More recently, there has been recognition that dental caries shares common features with many other chronic diseases in that it has a complex aetiology. Chronic diseases in general are characterized by multiple contributing factors,

long latency periods, a prolonged course of illness and, if untreated, functional impairment. Dental caries fits that model well. There is now an understanding that bacteria exist on oral surfaces as components of multi‐species biofilms. Thus, the microbial contribution to dental caries is the sum of the acid‐producing activity of all the bacteria found in that biofilm. Whether or not the tooth surface dissolves over time, or whether partially dissolved mineral crystals just beneath the tooth surface are able to regrow, is determined by the time balance between acid, base and neutral conditions at the biofilm–tooth interface. Caries is therefore reasonably thought of in terms of a balance between demineralization and remineralization. It can be managed by changing that balance, through attention to several factors at the same time, rather than one or two. This concept has proven to be markedly more effective as a rational basis for preventing, managing and, particularly during the last decade, curing the disease through awareness, education, motivation and monitoring than either the salivary lactobacillus‐residual food or the specific plaque hypothesis.

### Assessing the Risk of Developing Caries

### in the Future

There is high value to individual patients and, in public health settings, to the community, in assessing the risk of a particular disease occurring in the future for each patient. Doing so allows resources to be focused on those individuals, converting them from high risk to low risk at relatively low cost. Making the change usually requires significant behavioural modification, including a personalized care plan and an effective, long‐term preventive programme.
When managing multifactorial chronic diseases, many doctors have searched for individual markers, or surrogates of the disease, that will provide an assessment of future disease risk. An example of a surrogate marker used in cardio‐ vascular disease management is low‐density lipoproteins (LDL) in the blood. Elevated LDL serves well as a predictor of future disease. The accuracy of prediction is improved when combined with other markers, such as high‐density and total lipoproteins, blood pressure, the habit of smoking and body mass index.
In the case of dental caries, the predictors are similarly complex, and the accuracy of prediction is likely to increase as more indicators are considered. Many contributory factors can be considered. Some of them can be measured in ways that are objective and reproducible, others may be assessed more subjectively. None of them alone has a high predictive value for caries
[1, 2]. However, as is the case with assessment of other chronic diseases, combining a number of factors adds substantially to the predictive power.

### Caries Activity Assessment

Detection of existing carious lesions does not determine their activity state. Even a frank cavity may have developed months or years earlier and may no longer be actively progressing.
Alternatively, even one small, initial lesion may represent the beginning of very rapid loss of structure at that site as well as many others. Determining accurately and quickly the difference between these two circumstances is extremely important to the well‐being of the patient. By knowing, with a reasonable degree of certainty, the level of activity, the most effective treatment of the disease can be designed and delivered.
Historically, one way of determining caries activity state was to observe the progress of individual lesions over time through either visual or radiographic examination. Such observations were made at intervals of six months or more, sufficient time for lesion progression to be clearly apparent. However, waiting for a lesion to develop further is now hard to justify ethically, because biochemical reversal of an early lesion has a predictable clinical outcome. Ethical care now requires a very different and much more nuanced approach.
Whether an individual lesion is active is based on many factors but a single evaluation may reveal some indication of the lesion’s progression. Carious lesions that appear frosty, feel rough, show loss of lustre and appear opaque are signs of ongoing acid exposure and are probably lesions that are active. These signs in the presence of plaque or gingival inflammation from plaque (that the patient may have just brushed off) are indications of active lesions requiring risk‐based interventions (not necessarily surgical). Conversely, lesions that are brownish or black in colour, even when cavitated, may not be active and may not require surgical intervention [3, 4].
As is the case with prediction of future disease risk, disease activity can be estimated by inquiring into or by measuring multiple factors that are known to contribute to, or are likely to contribute to, the disease, then adding the resultant data together to create a summative index of disease activity. Indicators of activity may include clinical observations as well as medical and social data. The act of gathering data is itself an intervention of value to future oral health because it is likely to influence patient understanding and behaviour. If a determination is made that the disease is active, the data can be used to design targeted advice and intervention with a much higher degree of confidence than would otherwise be the case. Conversely, if a determination is made that there is no active disease, time and resources can be better applied to other tasks.

### Methods of Assessing Activity and Risk

This chapter will include descriptions of three methods of caries activity and risk assessment: (i) one that is primarily focused on the assessment of future risk of developing caries; (ii) one

that provides information about both present caries activity state and future risk; and (iii) one that is primarily focused on the immediate caries activity state. Each of the methods to be described is designed to help both the patient and the dental team focus on modifying those factors that are most likely to contribute to the disease.
The three methods have in common a focus on at least five primary factors that are known to contribute to caries activity and caries risk. These five factors are:
• the microbial mix in biofilm, or plaque;
• the frequency of daily exposures of the biofilm to simple, fermentable sugars;
• past and current exposure to fluoride;
• the observed, stimulated salivary flow rate, and factors that may reduce salivary flow;
• recent caries experience.
Each of the three assessment methods uses these parameters in slightly different ways, and each considers other related factors as well. Each of the three can be a very beneficial addition to the ethical and effective care of dental patients.

### The Cariogram

The Cariogram is a computer‐assisted assessment system that uses nine weighted parameters to help identify patients at high risk of developing caries. It is expressed graphically as the percentage chance of avoiding caries in the future, ranging from 0% (a very low chance of avoiding caries in the future, that is, a very high caries risk) to 100% (a very high chance of avoiding future caries, that is, a very low caries risk). Developed at the Dental
School in Malmö, Sweden, building on earlier work in Sweden and elsewhere, a ‘Cariogram Manual’ was first published in 1997
[5] and later became available as an internet version (Version
2.01, 2004).
The reader is referred to the internet version of the Cariogram Manual. It is freely available and very clearly expressed.
The brevity of the following description is a tribute to the quality and detail of that published material, which we shall not attempt to replicate.
In brief, the system measures or estimates and then records the following nine factors [6]:
• General health
• Diet/consumption of refined carbohydrates
• Oral hygiene
• The use of fluoride
• Mutans streptococci and lactobacilli counts in saliva
• Salivary buffering capacity
• Stimulated salivary secretion rate
• Caries to the dentino‐enamel junction, assessed radiographically
• Decayed, missing and filled surfaces/decayed, missing and filled teeth.

Each factor receives a weighted rating, the weighting being based upon prior research. The Cariogram computer‐assisted rating then provides a chance of avoiding caries ranging from 0–100% expressed both numerically and visually. A 0% reading is a high caries risk, with what is estimated to be a 50 times greater chance of developing caries than the lowest risk reading, a 100% chance of avoidance. The visual representation is by the relative size of the green section of a ‘pie’ chart (see Figure 3.1). The larger the green section, the lower the risk of developing caries in the future;
the smaller it is, the greater the risk. Through increased understanding, advice and monitoring, patients, or their caregivers, are helped to change circumstances so that the size of the green segments increases over time, until in the future, they reach a high percentage chance of avoiding caries.
Multiple studies have demonstrated the accuracy of the predictive value of the Cariogram. For example, Petersson et al.
found that 92% of those rated with the highest risk of developing caries did so within two years and 83% of those found to be at the lowest risk did not develop caries [1]. The Cariogram

is therefore highly predictive and specific at both ends of the spectrum.
The Cariogram does not weight social factors toward caries risk. Bratthall explained that social factors do not act directly upon the tooth surface and therefore are not included[7].

### Caries Management by Risk Assessment

### (CAMBRA)

Initially created through collaboration between the California
Dental Association Foundation and the University of California, San Francisco, School of Dentistry, Caries Management by Risk Assessment (CAMBRA) was introduced through publication in the Journal of the California Dental Association in
2003 [8].
CAMBRA is designed for a dental practitioner or dental team to use in any setting to help actively engage individual patients or their caregivers in a joint effort to improve and maintain oral health, with a focus on reducing and eliminating caries. It is, like the other systems described in this chapter, based on identifying factors that indicate present caries activity and future caries risk and then addressing these factors in a therapeutic partnership with patients or caregivers.
CAMBRA overtly recognizes that if the caries activity state is high or very high, it is economically unwise to have expensive aesthetic, orthodontic or restorative services performed until the risk level is reduced. It uses the analogy of testing for blood lipids and other factors in predicting the risk of cardiovascular disease to make the point that spending money on diagnostic and preventive services makes good economic sense.
Risk in the CAMBRA system is categorized as one of four levels: extremely high, high, moderate and low.
Based largely on the concept of caries balance/imbalance,
CAMBRA recognizes that whether a tooth demineralizes or remineralizes is caused by many factors [8].
Balance/imbalance can be evident through recent activity indicators including:
• new active white spot lesions;
• new or recent restorations for restoration of active caries;
• new/active approximal enamel lesions;
• new visible and radiographic lesions to the dentino‐enamel junction.
Recent caries activity is considered to be the development of any new lesions during the past three years. In the CAMBRA assessment system the presence of any of these recent activity indicators automatically identifies a patient as being in the high risk category.
Predictive risk factors include:
• elevated lactobacillus and mutans Streptococcus counts;
• decreased salivary flow rates;
• anatomic deformities of the teeth;
• heavy plaque;

These factors elevate the assessed risk to high, except when there is the presence of one or more of the above risk indicators plus salivary hypofunction, in which case the patient is raised to the extremely high risk category.
The dentist can then make recommendations for treatment based on the level of risk assessed:
• Patients at low risk may receive recommendations for oral home care preventive products to keep risk levels low, and are told that, while they remain low risk, they will be better candidates for, say, elective cosmetic procedures.
• Patients at high risk may require therapeutic intervention in the form of oral rinses, gels, gums, and sprays, as well as restoration of any existing carious lesions. High risk patients may also receive recommendations to put off elective cosmetic dental procedures or orthodontic treatment until risk levels can be controlled. The dental intervention will include treating the disease in addition to restoring defects or improving aesthetics, the former actions being based on the patient’s specific risk factors.
• Patients who are assessed as high or extremely high risk are told that they are more likely to have failures of expensive dental work due to recurrent caries. They are encouraged to delay such work until the disease risk becomes low.
The CAMBRA system is designed to be presented as a service that dental teams can provide, in a similar way to providing dental hygiene services. It is well recognized that dental hygiene services will reduce the risk of periodontal disease, and that, at the same time as promoting good health overall, will reduce or avoid the cost of either periodontal surgery or tooth replacement. In a similar way, the CAMBRA approach can ethically and appropriately be advocated to patients and their caregivers as something that will reduce the risk of restorative or cosmetic failure due to recurrent caries, and therefore has immediate and long‐term financial value to the patient or their family. In addition, it will lead to the less readily quantifiable benefits of improved dental health throughout life.

### The Traffic Light‐Matrix (TL‐M) System

The Traffic Light‐Matrix model was first described in 2005, having been developed independently of Cariogram or CAMBRA, to address the question of how best to treat patients with active dental caries using an individualized, patient‐centred approach
[9]. While apparently more complex than either Cariogram or
CAMBRA in the number and extent of parameters considered, it is also, like them, both a useful tool for treatment planning and a focused communication tool to explain the issues with each patient or their caregiver. It offers a systematic approach to assess all the factors that might contribute to caries activity, as part of a complete oral examination and history‐taking, then to design targeted or individualized preventative and curative therapy, and finally to monitor the outcomes of that therapy over time.

The Traffic Light allocates a ‘threshold value’ for each risk factor. If the information elicited from questioning or by clinical testing yields results that exceed the predetermined threshold values, then the model alerts the clinician and the patient to a problem area that needs to be addressed. The system does not make any assumptions about either the relative importance of individual risk factors or their relationship to each other.
The system scores a ‘red light’, a ‘yellow light’ or a ‘green light’ for each risk factor depending upon predetermined criteria.
Tests can be carried out either by a dentist or an auxiliary, who has been trained to collect the data, and recorded on a form, see
Figure 3.18 on p. 000). The use of auxiliary personnel to assist in data gathering makes the model more economically attractive for everyday clinical practice.
The Matrix is designed as a means of assessing the patients’ present disease status relative to their attitude to maintaining their own dental health. It is not intended to be anything more than a subjective assessment by the clinician on a particular day based on the clinician’s instinctive understanding of that patient. However, collecting and re‐collecting this information over a period of time gives the clinician a very useful measure of the patient’s ability, or willingness, to comply with treatment advice. It is also a simple way to provide information about the potential for patient compliance between different operators in the same practice or upon referral to another practitioner.
Attitude towards dental health is scored as A, B or C and recorded on the vertical axis of the grid shown in Figure 3.2.
Current disease status is scored as 1, 2 or 3 and is recorded on the horizontal axis. The two scores position the patient within the grid (Figure 3.2).

### Stimulated saliva flow rate

This is the one salivary factor that is most likely to be highly correlated with caries activity. The composition of stimulated saliva is dependent on the flow rate and it represents the combined production from both major and minor glands. The stimulated saliva flow rate, measured in a healthy adult population, shows a wide variation across the group but consistency within the individual when measured at different times. The mean flow rate among the large group of patients studied was 1.6 ml/min. [16].
A stimulated salivary flow of 0.7 ml/min. is accepted as the threshold below which there is an increased risk of caries [17].

Assessment of the biofilm
The bacteria most commonly implicated in the caries process,
S. Mutans, S. Sobrinus and Lactobacillus, belong to a group of about 40 species in this population, all of which have a cariogenic potential, and they need to be identified and controlled.
About 50% of the organisms in plaque cannot be cultured with current laboratory technology. They interact with neighbouring cells and they function as a coordinated and metabolically integrated microbial community [18].
The matrix of polysaccharide varies in density, so there are concentration gradients that will influence the diffusion properties of the mature biofilm [19]. This means, the thicker the biofilm, the more difficult it is for saliva to buffer the acids within.
It is possible to roughly gauge the diffusion gradient with a disclosing gel that stains thin plaque red and thick plaque blue (Figures 3.14 and 3.15).

### Streptococcus mutans

Streptococcus mutans is a general term describing several closely related species of streptococcus. The specific name S.
mutans is now given to human isolates and it is the species most commonly found in human dental plaque. There is an association [18] between the salivary level of S. mutans and the number of carious lesions and it is a particularly accurate predictor of caries activity in small children [20].

### Lactobacilli

Lactobacilli require retentive sites and are therefore found in deeper lesions. The population level is strongly influenced by dietary sugar intake and, in the absence of open lesions, a high level suggests a diet high in fermentable carbohydrate.

### Test methods

The test currently available for both of these species is semi‐ quantitative and involves placing a saliva sample on a culture medium and incubating for 48 hours (Figure 3.16). The bacterial count is obtained by comparing the culture to a chart supplied by the manufacturer. There are clinical limitations because of the time needed to incubate the sample and also correct interpretation of the results requires training.

The acid change in plaque is time‐dependent as illustrated by the series of Stephan curves in Figure 3.17. Following exposure to sugar, the pH of the biofilm drops sharply and will take over

There is overwhelming evidence that frequent intake of fermentable carbohydrate increases the risk of caries. Individuals with high sugar diets consistently show elevated levels of

It has also been shown that chewing sugared gum twice a day leads to a significantly higher caries increment after 24 months [26]. Based on this the TL‐M system assigned the red score to a patient with more than two exposures to sugar in between the main meals.

Dietary sources of acid, such as carbonated soft drinks, fruit juices and sports drinks have pH lower than the critical pH of apatite (pH 5.5) and they may therefore contribute directly to corrosion, as outlined in Chapter 1. While probably not a direct or primary cause of dental caries, they may be a contributing factor, by lowering overall oral and therefore biofilm pH during the time for which they are present in the mouth. Fluoride provides protection against caries at three levels:
• It increases the resistance of enamel to demineralization.
• It arrests the progression of white spot lesions.
• At high concentrations, it interferes with cell metabolism in the plaque.
It is now accepted that constant exposure to low levels of fluoride in drinking water and toothpaste will have an effect on enamel throughout the life of a tooth. However, the effectiveness of fluoride is reduced as the pH of the saliva falls and this has important implications when interpreting the interrelationship of the risk factors.

Modifying factors

### Dental history

The presence of restorations and/or active carious lesions is the strongest evidence of high caries activity even if they are identified as white spot lesions only. Useful information can often be derived from the location and pattern of these lesions. For example, active lesions in regions where there is normally saliva accumulation, such as lower anteriors or buccal surfaces of upper molars, tend to indicate high caries activity.

### Lifestyle

Lifestyle can be defined as a set of consciously chosen personal behaviour patterns. While it does not actually cause caries, it may predispose the patient through risky behaviour and environment. Occupational conditions and recreational activities can lead to obvious risks. The patient will not necessarily relate the behaviour to problems in the oral cavity and a high level of education may be necessary to develop recognition.
A contemporary example is young adults with an active
‘sporting life style’. Many of these activities lead to dehydration that is quenched by frequent intake of high energy ‘sports drinks’. These are high in sugar and low in pH, and this is a dangerous combination. The cariogenic effect can be minimized by drinking a large quantity at once rather than sipping small amounts frequently.
The following list covers the obvious lines of enquiry the dentist should follow to try to elicit the required information to lead to a correct diagnosis. This is covered later in this book so the list is offered only to draw attention to the detail:

### Medical history

There are numerous medical conditions and medications that can result in altered salivary function. Common systemic conditions associated with reduced salivary flow include diabetes, depression and rheumatoid conditions such as Sjögren’s Syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis and irradiation of salivary glands for head and neck cancer.

### Prescription medications

There are literally hundreds of prescription medications that lead to reduced salivary flow as a side‐effect. The most common of these are anti‐depressants, anti‐hypertensives, anti‐cholinergics, anti‐psychotics, diuretics and anti‐Parkinson’s drugs.

### Self‐administered medications (OTC)

Many patients self‐medicate and take supplements or alternative remedies that they do not consider to be medication. The active ingredients of these may reduce salivary flow and the outer coating of a tablet may have a high sucrose content. Some of these are prescribed as lozenges to be sucked over time so the ambient pH will be lowered for extended periods. Some ‘over‐ the‐counter’ (OTC) medications for allergy problems, such as hay fever and asthma, will affect saliva flow and lower the pH.

### Recreational drugs

Caffeine, tobacco, alcohol, marijuana and amphetamines all have the effect of reducing salivary flow and all, at least to some degree, are addictive. ‘Cola‐type’ soft drinks and ‘high‐energy’ athletic drinks are particularly destructive as they contain significant amounts of caffeine and create an addiction to a product that has high sugar levels and a low pH. Even the so‐called
‘sugar‐free’ varieties have a significantly low pH and therefore increase caries risk.
Patients have a very restricted view of what they consider to be medications and they have no idea of the potentially harmful effects of many of the substances they ingest. It is therefore, extremely important that a careful history be taken if the risk factors are to be fully and accurately determined.

### Socio‐economic status

Social factors can have an overriding influence on general health status and this is especially true for multifactorial and complex problems such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease. Children from lower socio‐economic strata whose parents have low educational levels are at a much greater risk of developing caries and the potential for changing their lifestyle is influenced by the parents’ level of understanding and motivation. However, this is difficult to assess and it is most unwise to jump to conclusions.
The effect of financial stringency can affect dental treatment at two levels:
• Compliance: is there spare money in the family budget for the patient to comply with the clinicians’ instructions? This factor cannot be readily changed so it is important to devise acceptable home and professional care regimes.
• Indirect costs: for some patients, even a free visit to the surgery can be expensive in term of loss of earning, child care and travel expenses.

### Compliance

While it is important to discover as much as possible about the various risk factors, it is more important that the patient is willing to make the necessary changes to eliminate or at least modify these factors. Compliance is dependent on two factors:
• patients’ attitude to improving their own oral health: the patient has to take ‘ownership’ of the problems;
• dentists’ ability to devise an acceptable treatment regime.

### Conclusion

Caries activity and risk assessments are low‐cost, high‐value and readily determined methodologies for customizing patient care. They allow the transfer of basic dental science knowledge about the caries process into a working methodology of improving oral health. They allow therapeutic recommendations to be directly applied to those patients who are most likely to benefit from the recommendations.
None of the methods described above for activity and risk assessment is at present likely to be perfect. Perfection will be difficult to achieve, given the complexity of dental caries as a disease process and the variability between individuals, their behaviour patterns and their resident micro flora. It is likely that the methods described will continue to be refined over time. It is now accepted that accurately predicting future risk for individuals for chronic disease with complex aetiology is extremely difficult [27].
There are two major benefits in using such systems of assessment that deserve emphasis:
1	 If patients believe that the therapeutic recommendations being offered by their dentist are, in fact, customized to their personal needs, there is higher likelihood that they will understand and take personal ownership of the management of the disease. They will be more compliant in following recommendations and therefore be more successful in achieving long‐term oral health.
2	 The ability to determine those who are at high risk allows resources to be focused on the most effective factors at the right time to optimize health outcomes. Identifying those at low risk will lower health care costs by avoiding the application of therapies to those who have a low risk or no risk at all [6, 28].

Assessment of the caries activity state and the risk of developing the disease in the future are now ethical and necessary parts of general dental practice.
