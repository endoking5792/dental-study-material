### Introduction

In this chapter the restorative treatment of all lesions of Size 1 and above will be described and discussed. Since there are fundamental differences in how defects are restored between the three Site groups the description is organized within the basic principles set out in the proposed classification by Site and Size (see Chapter 6).
In every case it will be assumed that active intervention to control the underlying disease state is required at the time of restorative intervention, and that the need will continue thereafter, as has been described in Chapter 2.

### Site 1 Lesions

Site 1 lesions most commonly commence in fissures on the occlusal surface of a posterior tooth. Pits are also found on the lingual of upper anterior teeth and may occur on the buccal surface of lower molars and the lingual extension of the distal occlusal groove of upper molars. Corrosion and attrition lesions on the occlusal surfaces of posteriors and the incisal edges of anteriors are also included within this classification because the principles behind their restoration are the same.
Site 1 – Size 0 (1.0): A pit or fissure on any tooth or an erosion lesion on an incisal edge that is regarded as suspicious and must be recorded and kept under obervation.
Site 1 – Size 1 (1.1): Small defective pit with early caries in one section or more of a fissure. Often restored in combination with a fissure seal on any remaining fissure system.
Site 1 – Size 2 (1.2): Moderate size lesion with at least one significant section of a fissure showing active caries.
Site 1 – Size 3 (1.3): A larger lesion requiring some level of protection from occlusal load of one or more cusps within the design to support or prevent a split at the base of a cusp.
Site 1– Size 4 (1.4): Extensive lesion with one or more cusps already missing.

Site 1 – Size 1, designated 1.1
Lesions identified under this classification will generally commence in fissures on the occlusal surface of a posterior tooth.
Pits on the lingual of upper anterior teeth are not uncommon and similar lesions may also occur on the buccal surface of lower molars or the lingual extension of the distal occlusal groove of upper molars. Corrosion and attrition lesions on the occlusal surfaces of posteriors and the incisal edges of anteriors should also be included once they are extensive enough to require restoration.
As has been described in Chapter 2, the complex anatomy of a fissure lends itself readily to the admission of bacterial plaque into the restricted depths along with periodic infusions of refined carbohydrate for nutrition. Demineralization can then progress intermittently without being readily observed from the outside.
The speed of penetration will be determined by the caries activity level in the particular patient, and progress can be very fast with devastating results. This is particularly so in the presence of well‐ fluoridated enamel which is likely to be very strong and resistant to fracture under occlusal load. In these circumstances it is possible

for the dentine to become involved to the extent that the pulp is also involved before the tooth exhibits any visual signs or symptoms.
Generally, progress will be slower and possibly intermittent. In many cases, only one section of a fissure system will be involved at a time.
Before the availability of adhesive restorative materials, when amalgam was the material of choice for the restoration of such lesions, it was necessary to eliminate the entire fissure system by cutting out or trenching, because it is not possible to effectively end an amalgam restoration part way along a fissure. If an attempt is made to do so, there will always be a defect at the interface between amalgam and the fissure in the enamel that will remain at high risk.
With the adhesive materials now available, it is possible to be much more selective and to surgically remove only the pits involved and that section of the fissure that is obviously demineralized or cavitated and then simply seal the remainder of the fissure system at the time of placement of the restoration (see Figures 7.1–7.6, which are intentional duplicates of Figures 2.8–2.12).
This technique can be used under many circumstances with safety and will result in saving considerable areas of natural tooth structure. For example, when restoring a proximal Site 2 lesion, the occlusal fissure will sometimes become involved in the cavity design. Previously, removal of the entire fissure was mandatory, partly to ensure that caries would not develop later in the fissure and partly as one element in the retention of a non‐adhesive restoration.
At this time the fissure can be sealed at the time of placement of the proximal restoration using an adhesive material. The materials used for sealing fissures are described and discussed in Chapter 2.

### Preparation

It is virtually impossible to completely clean a fissure system once a tooth has erupted into the oral environment. In many cases it will be desirable to mechanically clean the fissure even though this involves a limited sacrifice of some enamel. There is a variety of methods to achieve this with minimal reduction of tooth structure. These variations are discussed in Chapter
8 and include a very fine tapered diamond bur, a small tungsten carbide bur, a laser or air abrasion. Whichever preparation method is applied, preservation of natural tooth structure is of paramount importance, along with long‐term adhesion of the restorative material. A diamond or tungsten carbide bur offers the simplest and most effective method mainly because the operator retains some degree of both visual and tactile sense and is therefore less likely to over‐extend the preparation. As the preparation is primarily in enamel, local anaesthesia is not generally required. The tungsten carbide bur with a short head is less desirable because it is easy to lose sight of the head and therefore lose control of the depth of penetration.
Having gained access to a carious area, it is only necessary to develop clean walls around the lesion to ensure proper adhesion.

### Restoration

Of the restorative materials available, amalgam is the material of choice for such an extensive restoration. It is easier to build and to carve the occlusal anatomy to the extent required and the wear factor is similar to natural tooth structure. Also, amalgam has a superior resistance to flexure and is therefore better able to provide positive protection to the weakened tooth structure. Eventually it will make a more satisfactory base for the crown which, under these circumstances, will probably be required at a later stage.
When using amalgam as the restorative material, a lining of glass‐ionomer cement should be maintained over the floor of the cavity at least 0.5 mm thick to optimize the bioactivity available from the cement. However, the lining should not significantly reduce the bulk of the restorative material because it may then reduce its physical properties.
The remaining enamel is generally too weak to withstand the stress of the setting contraction of composite resin, even when the restoration is built incrementally. It may be possible to use a

lamination technique over glass‐ionomer, as described in Chapter 9, providing the occlusal load is not excessive.
Observe carefully the anatomy of the opposing tooth and be prepared to modify the length of an opposing working cusp to minimize the depth of the intercuspation between the two teeth.
This will reduce the splitting stress on the heavily restored split cusp and minimize undesirable contacts during lateral excursions (see Chapter 18).

### Restoration

If amalgam is to be used as the restorative material, mechanical interlocks with the remaining tooth structure are essential using ditches and grooves strategically placed in the gingival area. A resin or glass‐ionomer cement bond may enhance retention still further. Generally, one or more of the remaining cusps will need protection as described before. It will also be necessary to place a matrix to compensate for the missing enamel wall. Build the amalgam incrementally and condense well. Overbuild the occlusal contour and be prepared to adjust an opposing working cusp to minimize the degree of intercuspation and free the occlusion in lateral excursions (Chapter 18).

### Site 2 Lesions

These are lesions which arise on the proximal surface of either an anterior or a posterior tooth beginning in the region of the proximal contact area.
Site 2 – Size 0 (2.0): This lesion represents early demineralization of the enamel without surface cavitation. It can be healed through elimination of the disease and remineralization.
Site 2 – Size 1 (2.1): Surface cavitation of the enamel with minimal dentine involvement which has reached a point beyond stabilizing or repair through remineralization only. It will be identified by radiographs or transillumination.
Site 2 – Size 2 (2.2): More extensive involvement through the enamel into the dentine with the marginal ridge weakened or broken down but sufficient tooth structure remaining, following cavity preparation, to support the restoration. It may be a replacement for a small Black Class II or Class III restoration.
Site 2 – Size 3 (2.3): On a posterior tooth, there will be considerable involvement of the dentine with a split at the base of a cusp – or at least the potential for a split – with the need to protect one or more cuspal inclines from occlusal load. On an anterior tooth there will be extensive proximal caries with

loss of support for the incisal corner which will be deeply undermined.
Site 2 – Size 4 (2.4): There will be complete loss of at least one cusp from a posterior tooth or some loss of the incisal edge of an anterior tooth as a result of either caries or trauma.
The early lesion (Size 0 to Size 2) in a posterior tooth will often have to be identified by radiograph or by transillumination.
However, neither method is completely accurate and there may remain doubt as to the presence or absence of surface cavitation.
As long as the surface of the enamel is smooth, there is the possibility of remineralization in conjunction with elimination of the disease so a finite decision is required (Figures 7.17–7.20).

### Internal occlusal fossa approach (tunnel preparation)

When the enamel lesion is at least 3.00 mm apical to the crest of the marginal ridge or the corner of the incisal edge, the simplest and most conservative approach is through the occlusal fossa, just medial to the marginal ridge, using the internal occlusal fossa or tunnel approach [9–12]. The initial access should be as small as possible to preserve the natural tooth structure. Maintenance of the original proximal contour with a normal contact area is desirable so removal of enamel should be minimal. The entry should begin just medial to the marginal ridge with careful preservation of remaining enamel.

### Slot cavity approach

If the lesion is closer than 3.00 mm to the crest of the marginal ridge, a ‘tunnel approach’ is likely to leave the ridge too weak to be maintained. Access can be gained through the marginal ridge itself. This design has been called a ‘slot cavity’ and is probably more commonly used in posterior teeth and restored with amalgam (Figures 7.37–7.40). In a load‐bearing posterior tooth, amalgam may be considered desirable.

The initial lesion generally commences in the enamel immediately below the contact area between two teeth and extends facially and lingually in an elliptical shape controlled by the extent of the contact area. It will generally not involve the contact area itself nor undermine the marginal ridge or incisal corner to any extent until it has progressed to Size 2. It is often possible, therefore, to maintain almost the entire proximal surface in enamel. Early demineralization can penetrate the full depth of the enamel but, providing the prism structure does not collapse, it can be remineralized sufficiently to regain its original physical strength. It may be stained and disfigured, but, following removal of the infected dentine and sealing with an ion‐releasing glass‐ionomer cement, the proximal wall may remineralize and remain intact. If enamel caries has progressed further and there is actual cavitation of the surface, there may be a need to tunnel through to the exterior, to a limited extent, via the dentine cavity, so that the cavitation can be obturated to eliminate further plaque retention, while the remainder of the proximal wall remains intact. In the presence of ions released from a glass‐ionomer cement restoration, as well as external topical applications of fluoride, remineralization will support and reinforce the enamel and prevent further breakdown.
All of these considerations apply to both anterior and posterior teeth and three different approaches can be considered for the restoration of these lesions depending upon the position in relation to the marginal ridge or the presence of a larger lesion in the adjacent tooth [6–8]: (i) internal occlusal fossa approach;
(ii) the slot cavity approach; or (iii) the proximal approach.

The Site 3 lesion occurs in the gingival one‐third of the crown or on the exposed root surface of any tooth. Lesions can occur on the open surfaces (facial or lingual) in relation to the contours of the gingival tissue or interproximally, well below, and not related to, the contact area following gingival recession.
Site 3 – Size 0 (3.0): There is no equivalent in the Black classification. Early signs of either active caries or non‐carious tooth loss at the gingival margin of any tooth around the full circumference.
Site 3 – Size 1 (3.1): More extensive early active caries or non‐ carious tooth loss in the cervical region of any tooth around the full circumference, requiring restoration.
Site 3 – Size 2 (3.2): Advancing caries, replacement of failed restoration or extensive non‐carious tooth loss in the facial or lingual cervical region of any tooth.
Site 3 – Size 3 (3.3): Advancing caries or extensive non‐carious tooth loss at the cervical margin interproximally between any pair of teeth (root surface caries), unrelated to the contact area.
Site 3 – Size 4 (3.4): Advanced caries or non‐carious tooth loss at the cervical margin of any tooth where more than one surface is involved and a complex matrix system is required during restoration.
Caries can occur anywhere around the full circumference of a tooth as a result of reduced salivary flow or lack of hygiene leading to plaque accumulation. The carious lesion may have an enamel margin around the full circumference but the usual cavity in this area has an occlusal (incisal) margin in enamel and a gingival margin in dentine.
Root surface caries can also occur anywhere on the root surface following gingival recession. If it occurs interproximally, the lesion will be well below, and unrelated to, the contact area.
It will be classified as a Site 3 lesion and restoration will depend

on access and convenience, from either the facial or lingual rather than from the occlusal. If the lesion is approached from the occlusal, then it will be classified as a Site 2 lesion,
Abrasion/corrosion non‐carious lesions are also included in this category. There are three possible causes (see Chapter 1) and it is desirable to determine the cause and eliminate it at the time of treatment otherwise the tooth loss may continue around the restoration.

### Preparation

A Site 3 carious lesion is generally found at the gingival margin in the presence of a high caries rate and poor oral hygiene routines (Figures 7.76–7.79). A decision will need to be made as to whether remineralization and improved hygiene will be sufficient to stabilize the situation or whether a restoration should be placed. If a restoration is required, remove the carious dentine only using small round burs. Occasionally, if the enamel is very friable, it may be necessary to extend the cavity out on to the enamel crown but there will be no need to remove all the demineralized enamel – just that which is beyond remineralization. Use a small diamond cylinder at intermediate high speed under air/water spray for limited extension. If glass‐ionomer cement is to be placed, the state of the enamel is not important because the continuing fluoride release will encourage remineralization. If the cavity is to be restored with composite resin, the outline will need to be extended to reach sound, fully mineralized enamel which can be safely etched to provide micro‐mechanical attachment. As an adhesive material will be used for restoration, it is unnecessary to develop a retentive design.

A corrosion/abrasion lesion should be lightly cleaned with a brief scrub with pumice and water on a small rubber cup to remove the biofilm. Do not use proprietary prophylaxis pastes, whether they contain fluoride or not, because they tend to leave a smear layer which will interfere with the adhesion and the ion exchange. Both an erosion lesion or a carious cavity should then be conditioned with 10% polyacrylic acid for 10 seconds, washed thoroughly and dried lightly.
When a resin‐modified glass‐ionomer is to be placed, contour and adapt the cement with a translucent matrix, light‐ activate for 20 seconds, and remove the matrix. Light‐activate for a further 20 seconds at least to ensure complete activation. Contour and polish immediately with very fine diamonds under air/water spray. Finally, apply a thin coat of the appropriate glaze to seal any remaining surface porosities and scratches.
When using an auto‐cure glass‐ionomer cement, prepare the cavity as above (Chapter 9). Use a cement with a high powder/liquid ratio, preferably capsulated, and place it using a syringe. Apply a soft tin matrix to adapt the cement well to the surface of the tooth and leave it to set for approximately four minutes. As soon as it is set, remove any excess cement from around the matrix, lift off the matrix and immediately cover the cement with a generous layer of a single component, very low viscosity resin enamel bond to stabilize the cement and avoid water uptake or water loss. Trim the cement further, if required, using a sharp blade only but do not contaminate with water. Develop a reasonable contour and cover with additional resin bond to complete the seal. Light‐activate the bond and trim the gingival margin if there is an excess of sealant remaining. Complete the contour and polish a minimum of one week later. Seal again with a low viscosity resin to cover porosities and scratches.
If, after a few days, the aesthetics of the glass‐ionomer cement is unsatisfactory, it can be trimmed back with a fine diamond bur under air/water spray to allow for lamination with composite resin. Clean all the enamel at the occlusal margin and bevel it lightly so as to develop optimum micro‐ mechanical union. Etch the enamel and the cement for 15 seconds, wash thoroughly and dry lightly. Apply a thin layer of an enamel resin bonding agent, remove the excess and light‐activate it. Build the composite resin incrementally, beginning at the gingival margin.

### Restoration

The material of choice is a Type II. 1 restorative aesthetic glass‐ ionomer cement, either auto‐cure or resin‐modified, because the aesthetic result is very similar to that which can be achieved with composite resin and the adhesion and fluoride release are superior. Alternatively, if the colour and translucency are not entirely satisfactory, the cement can be laminated subsequently with composite resin.

### Preparation

Retain as much natural tooth structure as possible, paying particular attention to the pulpal floor of the cavity. The demineralized affected dentine can often be remineralized and, if possible, should not be removed. Clean the peripheral margins only and maintain as much enamel as possible. Condition the cavity as usual prior to placement of the restoration.

### Restoration

Glass‐ionomer cement is the material of choice because of the adhesion and fluoride release. The greatest problem will be to construct a suitable matrix to facilitate placement of the cement.
One technique is to cut a soft tin matrix to shape and then cut a small hole in an appropriate position through which to syringe the cement. The matrix can then be placed and held with green stick compound or similar and left in place until the cement is set.
An alternative technique is to use a resin‐modified glass‐ ionomer cement and build the restoration incrementally with careful light curing at each stage.
