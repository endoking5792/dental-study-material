### Silver Amalgam as a Restorative Material

In the early 1800s, it was recognized that various alloys of silver could be amalgamated with mercury to make composite, metallic compounds that could replace small to moderate‐sized areas of tooth structure lost to the carious process. The composite or compound materials were called ‘silver amalgam’, ‘dental amalgam’, or simply ‘amalgam’.
The mixtures were initially putty‐like, so they could be compressed into a prepared cavity where, over subsequent minutes, then hours, they progressively hardened. The mixtures were soft enough to be shaped with hand instruments by carving and burnishing during the minutes after mixing and placement. After a few hours they could, at best, withstand the normal forces of occlusion and could then persist as functional restorations for many years.
In the early decades of their use, however, the silver alloys were highly variable in composition, as were the mixing proportions with mercury, and the systems of mixing the two components. The resultant materials therefore varied greatly in physical properties. In consequence, many amalgam restorations failed in clinical use only a few years after placement, and the materials gained a poor reputation.
Recognizing the material’s potential as well as its problems,
G.V. Black devoted considerable time in the late 1800s to the study of the physical properties of various silver alloys when mixed with mercury, to various proportions and systems of mixing (or ‘trituration’) and to clinical parameters used during placement. He and his colleagues were able to produce a standard product with predictable physical properties when used following carefully prescribed clinical guidelines, and therefore improved longevity.
Due to Black’s systematic work and strong advocacy, silver amalgam became well accepted within the dental profession by the turn of the twentieth century. It was progressively adopted as a standard, ethical material for the restoration of carious lesion defects, and it has remained so since that time.
In the latter half of the twentieth century, there was further systematic study of various silver alloys, leading to materials with excellent physical properties and therefore clinical durability. It then became possible to create amalgam restorations that would, in the absence of ongoing disease, serve for many decades without the need for replacement.
During the last two decades of the twentieth century questions also arose about the environmental safety of silver amalgam because of its mercury content. Although good arguments can be put forward suggesting that silver amalgam must in time be replaced with proper alternatives for this reason, at present the benefits in terms of reliability, durability, ease of placement and low cost are so strong and (with proper handling) the environmental risks sufficiently low that the material should remain as a key part of the ethical practice of dentistry [1].

The arguments in favour of the continued use of silver amalgam include the following:

1	 Amalgam is an economical choice; it is relatively simple and cheap to produce and to use, and, when used well, it can last for most if not all of a patient’s life without requiring replacement.
2	 The steps necessary for proper placement are relatively technique‐tolerant and can be accomplished with minimal instrumentation.
3	 Amalgam is resistant to marginal leakage; excellent marginal seal is achieved by the accumulation of corrosion products soon after placement.
4	 It has good physical properties, in particular, sufficient strength to withstand normal oral stresses.
5	 The wear rate in the oral environment is similar to that of normal tooth structure.
6	 Amalgam has sufficient strength to support remaining weakened tooth structure when restoring an extensive lesion, with appropriate cavity design.
On the other hand, a number of arguments can be proposed suggesting that its usage should be phased down:

1	 Amalgam is, to most people, unsightly since its colour ranges from silver when newly placed and polished to dark grey after many years without polishing.
2	 Amalgam contains mercury and the placement of new restorations may therefore pose a health risk to the dental team and to the environment.
3	 Removal of old amalgam restorations may pose a health risk to the patient, to the operating team and to the environment.
4	 Disposal of amalgam waste may pose risks to the environment.

The challenge facing the dental profession at this time is to find a truly viable alternative to amalgam as a restorative material for the repair of medium‐sized to large defects on posterior teeth
[2]. A number of alternatives have been suggested but at this time they appear to all have shortcomings, as will be discussed in a later chapter [3–5].

### The Influence of Amalgam on Cavity Form

As described in detail in Chapter 7 for defects at different sites and of different size, cavity form is determined principally by the extent of the defect to be restored. For adhesive materials, little or no modification of cavity form is necessary except in those circumstances where the strength of the restored tooth would otherwise be compromised.
Silver amalgam is not capable of a chemical or physical union with tooth structure. The lack of union means that the cavity for an amalgam restoration must have retentive features included in the design. Mechanical retention can be created through the use of undercuts, dovetails, retentive grooves, and parallel or convergent walls.

The creation of such features in addition to the removal of carious enamel and dentine requires access for the necessary cutting instruments. Effective placement of amalgam also requires physical condensation of the material into all areas of the cavity with flat‐ended instruments at least 1 millimetre across.
Amalgam lacks strength in thin section. The cavity form for an amalgam restoration should therefore allow sufficient thickness, with margins at or near 900, on all surfaces. This may lead to further sacrifice of natural tooth structure.
The development of these three features – sufficient access for instrumentation, the creation of retention form and the development of sufficient bulk at margins – means that the prepared cavity may have to be enlarged significantly beyond the dimensions of the original lesion so that the material can be used in a way that creates a strong and enduring restoration.
There is, therefore, a minimum outline for a cavity designed to repair a lesion, regardless of its initial size, to be restored with amalgam. A cavity designed for placement of amalgam will, in most cases, be larger than might otherwise be needed to replace structure lost to the carious process.
As tooth structure is relatively brittle, any weakening of the crown can lead to development of a split at the base of a cusp resulting in cusp fracture and therefore complications in further restoration. When restoring relatively large defects with amalgam, it is therefore appropriate to carefully observe the opposing tooth or teeth and the relationship to the one being restored. There is a reasonable possibility that the level of intercuspation with the opposing dentition can be reduced simply by reducing the tip of an opposing cusp to a minor degree. Doing so may well reduce the lateral load on restored tooth structure and minimize the potential for cusp fracture.

### Progressive Advances in Silver Amalgam

### Formulation

Formula according to Black [6]
Following some years of intense research G.V. Black decided that the preferred formula for a viable silver amalgam alloy should be approximately as follows:

Silver
65% by weight

Tin
29% by weight

Copper
> 6% by weight

Zinc
Trace only < 0.01% by weight

Originally the metals were melted together in an oven and cast as an ingot. The ingot was then held in the oven at a given

temperature for up to 24 hours to allow the phases to homogenize. Once cooled, the ingot was ground on a lathe to a relatively coarse particle size. Of course, this rather gross manipulation led to a mixture of particle sizes ranging from long pieces up to 50μ in length to a fine dust‐like powder. Ultimately, during the course of trituration with mercury, the longer particles were broken down, leading to a more homogeneous mass. However, the finished restoration was relatively ‘rough’ with reduced physical properties because of the inclusion of such a wide range of particle size and shape. These ‘rough cut’ alloys were further sophisticated over time by ‘washing out’ the finest powder particles, thus improving the ultimate physical properties (Figures
11.1–11.3).

Further modifications to the formula
In the 1960s, the problems arising from the use of the rough cut alloy led to the development of techniques for melting the metal ingot and atomizing the alloy into a neutral atmosphere, thus forming spherical particles of alloy of a more controlled and regular size. As with the lathe‐cut alloys, the spherical particles are subject to a prolonged heat treatment to coarsen the grains and enhance the reaction when incorporated with mercury.
One of the advantages with the spherical particles is that they do not require as much mercury to wet the surface of each particle during trituration. Add to that the fact that the particles move past each other more readily and the mixed alloy is then easier to condense into the cavity. Over time some of the alloys have been available in various forms so there is now a number of combinations available each with their proposed advantages:
• lathe‐cut – as produced from the lathe‐cut ingot;
• spherical – produced by air blasting the molten alloy into a neutral atmosphere;
• admixture – a combination of lathe‐cut and spherical particles.
Silver and tin are the primary constituents of the alloy and the trace of zinc is present as an oxygen scavenger that is only of value while the ingot is being melted. If there is more than
0.01% zinc present in the finished product, it may lead to water uptake during manipulation and condensation in the oral environment with a loss of physical properties, increased setting expansion and fragile margins. It is now possible to eliminate zinc entirely during the initial melt of the alloy with the result that the modern amalgams (identified as ‘zinc‐free’) will not expand following water uptake in the early stages after trituration.

Increase in copper content
Further research led to the development of ‘high copper’ alloys in which the copper content was increased beyond
6%, with some alloys containing as much as 28% copper [7].
Alloys with a content of at least 12% copper will show an increase of physical properties as a result of the reduction in the gamma 2 phase, with less creep and improved marginal integrity leading to a superior restoration. They are generally regarded as the preferred product at present (Figures 11.4 (a)–(d)).

Control of mercury content
Both the presence of excess mercury and the failure to reduce the excess matrix phase are a hazard to the physical properties of the finished restoration. On the one hand, it is essential to coat every particle of alloy with mercury but, on the other hand, excess retained mercury in the finished mix can be detrimental to the physical properties. When first developed, the amalgamation between mercury and the silver alloy could only be successfully conducted using a glass mortar and pestle. Removal of excess mercury was a challenge to the operator during placement but if it was not removed, the result would be a reduction in both physical properties as well as the longevity of the restoration. This all meant that the placement of a well‐constructed restoration required a careful balance and good communications between the dental assistant who prepared the mix and the operator who placed and condensed it into place.
At this time it is routine to dispense the amalgam alloy in a capsule to be machine‐mixed. In reusable capsules, the alloy powder is compressed into small tablets and the mercury is dispensed into the capsule at the time of trituration, however, this is no longer popular because of the risk associated with keeping mercury in bulk and the risk of spilling during loading of the capsule. Today the most common form is disposable capsules;
the alloy remains in powder form and the mercury is held in a separate compartment until activated. Apart from safety, this form of delivery also ensure a more consistent mercury‐to‐alloy ratio.
The setting reaction of an amalgam begins with the release of silver and tin from the alloy particles to form gamma 1 crystals followed shortly thereafter by the deposition of gamma 2 into the matrix phase. Formation of the matrix continues for some time until the free mercury is finally consumed by the reaction. However, the matrix phase represents the weak link in the strength of the material and any remaining free mercury will be weaker still. This means it is desirable to limit the reaction as far as possible to maintain the strength of the original alloy particles. That is to say, the time of trituration must be controlled.

### Trituration

By hand
Historically, the alloy powder and the mercury were dispensed from separate containers and measured by weight on a small set of scales and then decanted into a glass mortar and pestle. The components were then mixed, or triturated together by hand.

By machine
In the 1950s and 1960s research was devoted to the development of machines for standardized mechanical trituration of amalgam. This required the amalgam alloy and the mercury to be dispensed accurately by the manufacturer in pre‐measured capsules with the two components kept separate until mixing commenced. The capsule could then be ‘collapsed’ to allow the

contents to be united and mixed by machine for a standard period of time.
Once triturated, the amalgam was then dispensed into a carrier for placement into the cavity ready for condensation. This allowed placement with no potential for contamination through handling. At the same time the use of a rubber dam became more acceptable in general practice for the isolation of teeth to be restored which also improved clinical outcomes.

Construction of a matrix
As discussed above, an essential requirement for the proper placement of an amalgam restoration is the ability to condense it fully and properly to eliminate excess mercury that is likely to compromise the strength of the restoration. A number of materials have been tested over the years and the essential requirements are as follows:
4.1.1 The best basic material is a fine gauge metal strip capable of withstanding considerable pressure without distortion.

4.1.4 It should be thin enough to allow full restoration of the interproximal contact areas.

### Matrix band placement

There is a variety of designs for matrix bands and choice is personal with no particular preference, providing it is simple to handle and reliable. Historically, copper bands were used, especially where both proximal surfaces of a tooth are in need of restoration at the same time. Such bands can be trimmed and swaged to the required contour and then supported by wooden wedges or greenstick compound to ensure full adaptation at the gingival margin and stability during condensation. Once condensation is complete, the band can be cut through and removed laterally to avoid disturbing the restoration.
In view of the load that is likely to be placed upon it during condensation, the matrix band should be strong in itself and well supported against the tooth to be restored. As the lesion is a Site 2 on the approximal surface of a tooth, there is usually an adjacent tooth that can be used for support, using a reasonably strong wooden wedge forced interproximally at the gingival margin. The wedge assists in keeping the gingival margin of the band closed during condensation and helps separate the teeth slightly, ensuring a tight contact. If the lesion is large and the matrix requires further support, a small section of greenstick compound can be warmed and softened over a Bunsen burner and moulded into place. This is best achieved with a rubber dam in place to ensure protection for the soft tissues and the avoidance of saliva contamination that may prevent it sticking to the matrix or the tooth. Once condensation is complete, the greenstick can be softened again by thrusting a warmed flat plastic instrument into the middle of the greenstick. The softened material can be peeled away without stressing the partly set amalgam.

Condensation
As described above, one of the most important steps in the successful placement of an amalgam restoration is condensation of the material into the cavity [9, 10]. The freshly mixed material will have developed some level of matrix representing dissolution of the surface of the alloy particles into the mercury. In addition, there may well be some level of excess mercury that, for best results, should be expressed during condensation and not left behind. Excess mercury will be seen as a soft, shiny layer that must be removed before adding and condensing the next increment, thus maintaining an even consistency throughout the restoration. The matrix phase will set over a short period and then offers support for the alloy particles to hold the mass together [11].
It is important that the different segments of amalgam, placed incrementally, be joined together and intermixed into a homogeneous mass, free of porosities and properly adapted to the cavity floor and walls. This requires attention to detail and care to ensure that all parts of each increment are subject to a similar load and no increment is neglected.
Several amalgam condensers are available, mostly double‐ ended, with a variety of sizes and shapes. It is recommended that the face of the condenser should be flat and smooth because it will apply even pressure to the increment and will not get clogged up with amalgam from the last increment. The larger the dimensions of the face, the lower the load that can be applied with a given downward force but, on the other hand, the smaller the condenser face, the more likely it is to merely penetrate the last increment and not condense it fully. In the face of choice, the operator has to make a decision and use a condenser that shows some level of resistance to the load and expresses the excess matrix and mercury. Technically the load should be in the vicinity of 2–5 kg/sq.cm for the average amalgam although it will be closer to 2 kg for a spherical alloy.
The condenser should be moved in a stepwise fashion overlapping the prior condensation with the following condensation. Amalgam should be placed in small increments rather than large bulk, enabling a more thorough condensation of the mass.
As the amalgam mass is undergoing a setting reaction from the beginning of trituration, there will be a limited time for condensation. The time required for setting will vary from one amalgam to the next, but it is generally apparent when the initial set is being achieved. At this point, further manipulation should be curtailed because at some stage the amalgam will simply crack and be weakened and no further matrix can be expressed and removed. Also, further additions of newly mixed material will not unite with the partially set material, leading to an in‐ built defect in the restoration.

However, take care not to disturb the integrity of the restoration because it is still fragile at this point. It is undesirable to leave an excess over the margins because the restoration will almost certainly be in supra‐ occlusion. At the same time the amalgam is relatively brittle in thin section and will easily fracture away. As the amalgam sets further and becomes harder to carve, it may occasionally become necessary to use a rotary cutting instrument.
Take great care not to over‐carve the occlusal anatomy. It may look good but it will increase the angle of the cusp inclines and leave them more subject to lateral stresses and interferences in excursions. Specially where the cusps have not been protected by amalgam, they remain subject to splits at the base.
If the cusps have been cut back to be protected by an amalgam over‐build, begin contouring on the occlusal by freeing the amalgam from the matrix using a sharp probe. Subsequently, the final anatomy of the cusp and its level of protection can be determined once the matrix is removed. A working cusp generally requires protection of about 1.5 mm of set amalgam but a non‐working cusp requires only about 0.5 mm. of cover (Figures 11.5 and 11.6).

Burnishing
Having completed placement of the amalgam and carved the anatomy, it is generally a good idea to lightly burnish or swage the outer surface on to the tooth crown to ensure closure of any discrepancy in adaptation to the cavity margin and minimize the need for polishing [12]. If this option is chosen, use a smooth surface round or oval instrument and burnish from the centre of the restoration to the outer margin. Apply a light load only to move the still pliable mass over the cavity margin and effectively close it while at the same time improving the surface of the restoration and making it easier to polish.

Final polish and occlusal adjustment
Many consider the final polish a critical step in amalgam restoration. Polishing improves appearance, although there is no evidence that it increases restoration longevity. It will take several days for an amalgam restoration to mature so a final polish appointment should be delayed. At the polish appointment, attention should be paid to the occlusion because if the restoration is under stress in any excursion, the tooth will remain sensitive to changes in temperature in particular. Generally there will be a shiny spot on the surface of the restoration if it is in supra‐ occlusion. Use fine articulating paper and check both intercuspation and lateral excursions. ### The Mercury Hazard

The biocompatibility of amalgam has been the subject of extensive investigation, particularly in relation to the presence of mercury [13]. In 2009, there was a meeting of WHO in Geneva concerning amalgam. The final statement from that meeting began as follows:

The meeting encourages a global ‘phasing‐down’ of the use of dental amalgam and actively supports the introduction of dental materials alternative to amalgam. A global ‘phasing‐down’ of dental amalgam will contribute considerably to reduction of mercury use and release; meanwhile, a complete ban is not yet appropriate. The issue of equity in dental health care needs to be carefully considered. The Committee concluded that dental amalgams are effective and noted that none of the dental materials – amalgam and alternatives – was without clinical limitations and toxicological hazards.

Unfortunately many patients have been advised, or have requested, to have existing amalgam restorations replaced in the mistaken belief that this will improve their general health or result in the cure of chronic diseases and afflictions. Health authorities have consistently rejected these reasons for the removal, or non‐placement, of amalgam restorations.
The US National Institutes of Health in 1991, the Swedish
Medical Research Council in 1992, the US Public Health Service in 1993, and the Swedish Board of Health and Welfare in 1994 all reported that there was no evidence of ill health resulting from the use of dental amalgam, with the exception of relatively rare and well‐localized allergic‐type reactions on adjacent mucosa.

Potentially available forms of mercury
Mercury is ubiquitous in nature, and is present in many foods, drugs, cosmetics and in the air we breathe. At low concentrations it is well tolerated by the body, being poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract and readily excreted by the kidneys.
However, at higher concentrations, it can pose risks to health.
The risks differ according to the physical and chemical form in which exposure occurs.
From the toxicological viewpoint, there are four available forms of mercury: liquid, vapour, inorganic compound, and organic compound.

### Liquid mercury (elemental)

Liquid mercury has no toxic effect when swallowed. Droplets of mercury are very poorly absorbed and most mercury in that form passes harmlessly through the gastrointestinal tract. Any that is absorbed is in inorganic form and is readily excreted by the kidneys.

### Mercury vapour (elemental)

Significant toxicity can occur when large amounts of mercury vapour are inhaled. This accounts for most of the occupational or accidental exposure risk to individuals. Mercury vapour is

rapidly absorbed into the blood via the lungs. A proportion of the mercury vapour that enters the blood remains un‐ionized or uncharged for several minutes. While uncharged, it is lipid soluble and can therefore readily cross the blood–brain barrier. After several minutes it becomes charged, and can then leave the brain only extremely slowly. Chronic inhalation of mercury vapour therefore leads to increasing levels of mercury in the brain.
Mercury is a non‐specific enzyme inhibitor and interferes with cellular function. Chronic exposure to mercury vapour can therefore result in mild to moderate central nervous system effects that include, with increasing concentrations, insomnia, irritability, memory loss, headaches, depression and muscle tremors. Conditions that can mimic this mercury toxicity include alcoholism, lead poisoning, Parkinson’s and senile dementia.
Diagnosis of mercury intoxication requires testing the mercury level of urine (μg mercury/litre of urine) [14]:
• 10μg is normal, reflecting clearance of mercury ingested in food;
• 100μg is a significant exposure;
• 300μg is the level at which symptoms/signs appear.
Although dental staff may be at risk if very poor mercury hygiene is practised, the levels of mercury vapour to which patients, who receive silver amalgam restorations, are exposed is well below the level known to pose risks to health [15].

### Inorganic mercury compounds

Although dental amalgam contains several inorganic mercury compounds (e.g. Ag‐Hg), these are of very low or no toxicity.
They are harmless when swallowed and are rapidly and effectively excreted.

### Organic mercury compounds

Some organic compounds of mercury, especially methyl and ethyl compounds, are highly toxic at low concentrations and are a significant environmental concern. However, no organic mercury compounds are known to form in the oral environment through the use of dental amalgam.

WHO statement on the future use of amalgam
Because the release of very small quantities of mercury from amalgam has been demonstrated, there have been anecdotal reports of cases of mercury toxicity resulting from dental amalgams. The evidence has been subjected to scrutiny from multidisciplinary panels of experts, who report:

although it is not possible to completely rule out adverse effects in a minority of susceptible patients, it is concluded that there is insufficient evidence to justify the claims that mercury from dental amalgam restorations has an adverse effect on the health of the vast majority of patients.

The WHO meeting in Geneva offered the following statement:

Dental amalgam has a well‐documented history of safety and efficacy in dentistry. Its advantages include ease of handling, durability, and relatively low cost. Dental amalgam has numerous indications for use, especially for restorations in stress‐bearing areas. Its main disadvantages are poor aesthetics and the necessity for sound tooth structures to be removed in order for retention to be obtained.

Allergy to amalgam
A true allergic response to the mercury associated with dental amalgam is rare but may be manifested in one of two ways. An immediate sensitivity may follow placement of an amalgam restoration with external skin lesions being more common than intraoral lesions. An urticarial rash may appear on the face and limbs and this may be followed by dermatitis. Once the amalgam has set and the level of free mercury is greatly reduced, the skin lesions resolve [18].

Be Aware

Any one of three factors may cause mucosal lesions:
• Allergy to mercury
• Cytotoxicity related to the corrosion products
• Trauma from rough restoration

Patients with lesions related spatially to amalgam restorations should be patch tested for sensitivity to the constituents of dental amalgam by a dermatologist experienced in this field.

Concerns for dental staff
In the 1970s and 1980s, the findings of several large studies on urine mercury levels in dentists resulted in the establishment of firm ‘Mercury Hygiene protocols’ – refer to ‘Recommendations of the World Dental Federation’ [14].
As a result, in less than 10 years, the average urine mercury level in dentists in the USA declined from 14.2μg/l to less than 5μg/l compared with the average for the general population of 1–3μg/l.
The first neuro‐behavioural effects of a raised mercury level are said to appear at a urine level of approximately 100μg/l [19].

Environmental concerns

If environmental contamination by mercury‐containing waste from dental practices is not cut down to very low levels, then it is likely to be the main reason for government action against the use of amalgam in the future [20].

Particles of waste amalgam are produced when restorations are removed.
