A proper history and evaluation of a dental patient should address not only their medical status and their use of prescribed drugs, but should also include lifestyle issues such as the intake of water, various sugars, alcohol and caffeine, as well as tobacco smoking and the use of other ‘recreational’ or mood‐altering drugs. ### Introduction

Each of the diseases that causes tooth structure damage or loss is dependent on several factors that can change in response to choices that patients make about their own personal lifestyle, and that can be affected by changes in general health status.
Clearly evident are those choices related to food and drink, both in content and in frequency. Both dental caries and most tooth corrosion by acid are linked to such choices, as is acid‐accelerated wear. The timing and nature of oral hygiene practices affect dental caries very directly in the negative sense, and contribute with acid foods or drinks in the positive sense to the wedge‐shaped defects of ‘cervical abrasion’, and to ‘cervical hypersensitivity’.
Other choices can affect tooth structure diseases as well.
Many medications and drugs, both prescribed and non‐prescribed, can affect salivary flow. The intake of alcohol, caffeine, nicotine and many other ‘recreational’ drugs can all influence the integrity of tooth structure through their effect on saliva flow. Some disabilities, and many systemic illnesses, will also affect tooth structure and oral health status through the effects that they have on food choice, hygiene practices and, in some cases, salivary flow as well.

### Salivary Dysfunction

Changes in salivary flow are a common factor in many of the circumstances described above. It is therefore practical and helpful to first review the role of saliva in relation to general oral health and to tooth structure in particular. The focus will be on salivary impairment or dysfunction. The basic role of saliva in oral health and its relation to the fundamentals of demineralization and remineralization are described in Chapter 1.
Saliva performs multiple roles within the oral cavity (Figure 16.1). Regrettably, its importance is usually not appreciated until the usual healthy flow is severely reduced, or absent.
Salivary dysfunction is a common problem and is frequently undiagnosed, at least in part, because the patient’s symptoms are not a reliable early indicator of salivary gland dysfunction.
Patients will rarely be aware of diminished output until the resting flow rate is less than half the normal rate [1].
The functions of saliva include:
• lubricating the oral tissues for swallowing and speech;
• assisting the sense of taste by acting as a solvent;
• maintaining health of the oral mucosa;
• assisting in digestion through the actions of amylase and lipase;
• dilution and clearing of soluble material from the oral cavity;
• buffering bacterial acids in biofilm;
• buffering acids from food and drink;
• buffering short‐term exposure to gastric acids in reflux and vomiting;
• serving as a reservoir for the ions that allow remineralization;
• supporting and controlling the growth of oral microflora.

Salivary flow is stimulated both by the taste of food and by the action of chewing and a rise in flow rate increases both the pH and the buffering capacity. Phosphate levels will also rise, and this will influence the balance between demineralization and remineralization of the tooth structure.
Reductions in the quantity of salivary secretions or changes in the properties of saliva are responsible for a host of related intra‐oral and digestive problems that may impact directly upon the quality of life. These include:
• difficulties in chewing and swallowing food;
• difficulties in speech (dysphonia);
• alterations in taste (dysgeusia);
• increased plaque formation;
• increased risk of caries;
• increased risk of corrosion and tooth wear;
• mucosal abrasions and mucosal irritation;
• increased halitosis;
• increased risk of oral fungal infections.
These problems may in turn influence medical status because with oral discomfort, individuals may lose interest in eating and therefore suffer from poor nutrition.
Because salivary dysfunction can have numerous implications for general health, as well as oral health, screening for salivary parameters should be an essential component of routine patient assessment (Figures 16.2 (a)–(d)). In cases of unexpected, or otherwise unexplained loss of tooth structure, an assessment of salivary function is an essential part of diagnosis.

Salivary secretion and flow
Several mechanisms work in partnership to control salivary flow. Flow rate is under autonomic nervous control. This means that stimulation of parasympathetic nerves causes the release of water and ions, but not proteins. However, sympathetic stimulation causes the release of proteins packaged within acinar cells.
Proprioceptive stimuli from masticatory muscles and the periodontal ligament will, by reflex action, excite the salivary nuclei within the brain. These are also influenced by the cerebral cortex to the extent that the act of chewing will increase the salivary flow rate. The smell, sight and thought of various foods will stimulate a similar reaction.
The resting flow rate of saliva will vary throughout the day. It decreases during sleep and increases during the waking hours reaching the maximum flow during mid‐afternoon. This daily variation has relevance when assessing the resting flow rate in the clinical setting.

The typical resting flow rate is 0.03 mL/minute so the total amount of saliva secreted during eight hours of sleep will be only 15 mL. There will then be about two hours of stimulated flow per day during eating and that will be in the order of 1 mL/ minute. During the remaining 14 waking hours the resting flow will resume at about 0.3 mL/minute, leading to a total daily production of 700–1000 mL.

Clinical salivary parameters
Resting saliva flow and quality can be examined by visual means, including:
• visual assessment of lower lip labial gland secretion;
• visual assessment of resting salivary volume in the oral cavity (pooling);
• visual scoring of salivary viscosity (watery, bubbly, or frothy);
• measurement of resting salivary pH using pH paper or a pH meter.

Impact of medications
Many major medical disorders are associated with reduced salivary flow, and these must be recognized. However, other common factors in the aetiology are often overlooked, particularly the effect of medications.
Several hundred medications will induce salivary dysfunction, and this important side effect is not always listed as an adverse effect in prescribing guides. Over‐the‐counter medications such as expectorants and decongestants are particularly important.
‘Poly‐pharmacy’, the use of many drugs on a daily basis, is common among elderly and medically compromised patients.

All patients in this category should be tested for salivary dysfunction, even in the absence of overt caries or other pathology.
Salivary dysfunction can also be a side effect because of delayed metabolism and clearance of drugs by the liver and/or kidneys.
Because of the circadian rhythm, which prompts the saliva flow to be highest in mid‐afternoon, it is wise to take repeated measurements of resting salivary flow at different times of the day in patients for whom such thorough investigation may be justified. For example, an incorrect assessment can occur when patients are seen only in mid‐afternoon.
Salivary dysfunction is not the only oral side‐effect of medications. The patient must be carefully assessed for others such as taste dysfunction, gingival overgrowth, mucosal ulcerations or lichenoid lesions, mucosal pigmentation, tooth discoloration, involuntary facial movements, facial pain and dysaesthesia (Figures 16.4 (a)–(d)).

Caffeine intake, hydration, and salivary flow
A negative fluid balance and systemic dehydration may decrease both resting saliva flow and pH and can lead to corrosion, accelerated tooth wear [2] and an increased caries risk. As the body moves into negative fluid balance, the resting saliva volume will decrease and viscosity will increase in the anterior floor of mouth contributing to a sensation of thirst. However, thirst itself is an imperfect indicator of body fluid balance. Severe dehydration affects both resting and stimulated flow rates, whereas in mild dehydration, only the resting flow is abnormally low [3].
Water constitutes approximately 70% of the mass of body cells and approximately 60% of the total mass of the human body. Regulation of thirst through the hypothalamus is a relatively poorly developed reflex and lags behind the body’s need for water. There is a level of obligatory daily water excretion related to the disposal of waste through the kidneys, faeces, lungs and skin. Typical daily water loss from the kidneys is in the order of 500 mL to 1.5 litres; from insensible perspiration through the skin 450–900 mL; from the lungs 350 mL; and from faeces 150 mL. To compensate for this, the normal total water intake should be in the vicinity of 2.3 litres and this is generally obtained as follows:
• from the metabolic production of water, approximately 200–
300 mL;
• from foods 700–1000 mL;
• from liquids 550–1550 mL.
These figures explain the rationale behind the advice to drink five to six glasses of water per day. During hot weather, water loss increases in two ways: sensible perspiration, where the skin and clothing is visibly wet (1400 mL per day) and insensible perspiration, together with loss through the lungs (700 mL per day). Thus, total fluid intake in hot weather, assuming no significant physical activity, should be 3.3 litres, an increase of 1 litre.
During prolonged exercise large amounts of fluid can be lost through sensible perspiration. Amounts up to 5 litres are not uncommon, thereby increasing the daily water requirements to well over 6.5 litres per day.
A reduction of 2% in body weight from water loss will generally trigger the thirst reflex. Once body weight loss is in the order of 3–4%, complete cessation of resting salivary output will occur because of the release of an anti‐diuretic hormone. At the same time there will be a dramatic reduction in urinary output as the kidneys attempt to save water.
The diuretic effects of both alcohol and caffeine are significant so patients with a high intake should be aware of the need to take in additional water. The net effect of a high caffeine intake will be loss of fluid and this can precipitate adverse changes in the salivary environment, particularly the resting saliva. When assessing a patient’s intake of caffeine, it is recommended that coffee, tea, cola‐type soft drinks and energy drinks all be carefully noted, since each is a major source.
Caffeine is addictive so acute cessation of caffeine intake may be associated with a characteristic withdrawal syndrome [4].
Reductions should therefore be achieved gradually. A typical

mean daily caffeine intake is 4 mg/kg, which equates to approximately 280–300 mg/day in an adult with normal body weight of 70–75 kg. Demonstrable physiological and psychological effects from caffeine may occur at doses of 60–70 mg in most individuals.

Caffeine is the most popular, widely consumed, legal, and socially acceptable psychoactive drug in the world. Hollingworth’s 1911 investigation was among the first to systematically study the behavioural effects of caffeine. His research was undertaken following a United States federal government suit against the Coca‐Cola Company for marketing a beverage with an addictive ingredient, namely, caffeine [5].
Caffeine is a psycho‐stimulant and at dose levels of 50–200 mg will raise mental alertness, induce a faster and clearer flow of thought, wakefulness, and restlessness. Higher doses will stimulate the vasomotor and respiratory centres. It also has a slight stimulant action on the heart, increasing cardiac contractility and output, and dilating the coronary arteries. It constricts cerebral blood vessels but causes bronchial relaxation, increases urine output and increases secretion of gastric acid. This latter effect explains why a high caffeine intake exacerbates gastro‐ oesophageal reflux. Excessive intake of caffeine causes insomnia, anxiety, tachycardia, hypertension, cardiac arrhythmias and gastrointestinal disturbances. Chronic use is associated with habituation and tolerance, and, as noted above, discontinuation may produce a withdrawal syndrome [6]. There are well‐known psychological and physiological effects from modification to caffeine intake, and these need to be borne in mind when advising patients [4, 7].
Very high levels of caffeine have deleterious effects on the performance of the heart and the cardiovascular system. Intakes above 680 mg per day, equivalent to five cups of drip filter coffee, can be associated with an elevated risk of cardiac arrest in patients who are non‐smokers [8].
The medical literature has drawn attention to links between high caffeine beverages and osteoporosis. As well, consumption of soft drinks containing phosphoric acid has been shown to be an independent risk factor for hypocalcaemia in post‐ menopausal women. Consumption of one bottle per day of cola soft drinks has been associated with depressed blood calcium levels [9] in elderly women, while black cola beverages have been associated with an elevated risk for fractures in teenage children, particularly in females [10, 11]. Alcohol intake, saliva and dental disease
Regular intake of large quantities of alcohol can exert a range of effects on oral health, including salivary dysfunction, as a consequence of the diuretic effects of ethanol leading to a negative fluid balance. This can contribute to both caries and corrosion, and the latter can be exacerbated by the reflux of gastric acid contents following binge drinking. Mixing alcohol with a range of carbonated drinks containing high levels of sucrose, combined with depressed saliva flow rate from dehydration, can result in increased caries activity.
Chronic alcoholism will lead to cirrhosis of the liver and the resultant scarring will influence the production of a range of water‐binding proteins such as albumin, leading to disturbances in fluid balance. These changes, in turn, reflect back into the production rate of saliva. Reductions in resting saliva flow and pH can occur following a range of liver conditions including both chronic Hepatitis B and chronic Hepatitis C infections, as well as in alcoholic cirrhosis.
Other oral health impacts of alcohol abuse include neglect of home oral health care, interactions with numerous drugs including the antibiotic metronidazole, erratic attendance at dental appointments and a tendency to bleeding. Impaired wound healing will be a secondary effect from liver dysfunction if alcoholic liver cirrhosis has developed. Trauma to the teeth and face from falls and other accidents will heal slowly. High levels of alcohol ingestion have been linked with an increased risk of oral malignancy in smokers via a co‐factor effect, however, this association does not occur with mouthrinses containing alcohol.

Other drug use and salivary dysfunction
Numerous ‘recreational’ drugs cause salivary dysfunction, either through direct pharmacological effects on the central nervous system, or through secondary pathways such as release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH). Examples of illicit substances linked to dry mouth and extensive dental caries include cannabis (marijuana), LSD, cocaine, amphetamines, and opiates such as heroin, morphine and methadone.

Depressed resting saliva flow rate will contribute to an increased risk of caries, for reasons already outlined. Addicts may neglect regular oral hygiene practices such as toothbrushing and generally have a high intake of sweets, soft drinks, and foods with a high sucrose level. The salivary dysfunction combined with this cariogenic diet explains why there is a higher caries rate and greater tooth loss among drug users compared with others in the same age group. There are other factors that contribute to dental problems. Opiate‐injecting drug users, for example, attend for dental care on an irregular basis, and usually only when there is a need for relief of acute dental pain [12].
Methadone may be used as treatment for some drug users, as part of their management, and this is essentially the replacement of one opiate with another. It is delivered orally in a syrup of sorbitol and glycerol and has erroneously been identified as being cariogenic because of the pharmacological effects rather than personal neglect [13–17]. There is a misconception that the increased caries risk is due to the syrup, in the mistaken belief that this is formulated with sucrose. In fact, the base of methadone is sorbitol, a naturally occurring polyol that yields little in the way of fermentable products from the pathway of glycolysis. It has a cariogenicity approximately 10% of that of sucrose on a per‐weight basis, and cannot be fermented effectively by
S. mutans, even though other oral organisms can ferment it (Table 16.2).
In reality, dietary alterations are of greater significance due to pharmacological effects of methadone on those regions of the brain that influence food preference. Methadone causes a desire and craving for sweetness [18–20], and this in turn leads to a high sugar intake in the form of refined sugars with high cariogenicity [21].

Diabetes mellitus and saliva
In poorly controlled diabetes mellitus there will be a reduction in both resting and stimulated salivary flow rates and a reduced pH. Poor blood glucose control can increase the polyuria associated with diabetes, leading to negative fluid balance and dehydration. With long‐standing disease, lymphocytic infiltration of the major salivary glands may lead to reductions in both resting and stimulated salivary flow. In some patients, bilateral enlargements of the parotid salivary glands may occur.

Diabetics, like patients with bulimia, may collapse in the dental surgery from hypoglycaemia. Loss of consciousness may be preceded by a range of symptoms including heart palpitations, sweating, confusion, irritability, headache, and seizures.
To assist in the rapid and effective management of hypoglycaemia, glucose syrups or other readily available sources of glucose should be available in all dental practices. Susceptible patients should be advised to eat a light meal or snack prior to dental appointments, to reduce the risk.
Strong linkages have been reported between diabetes mellitus and a range of oral health conditions particularly periodontitis, oral fungal infections and dental caries.. An underlying dehydration in the poorly controlled patient can induce salivary dysfunction with depressed resting flow and pH resulting in an aciduric oral flora. This may lead to the development of caries and oral fungal infections with Candida albicans. In addition, lymphocytes may infiltrate the salivary glands resulting in a reduced stimulated salivary flow as glandular capacity reduces over time.
Patients with Type I diabetes and severe periodontal disease may release potent pro‐inflammatory mediators into the circulation, leading to a range of diabetic complications. Side effects can include renal disease, stroke, transient ischaemic attacks, angina, myocardial infarcts and heart failure [22].
Non‐insulin‐dependent Type II diabetes mellitus has been linked with a high carbohydrate lifestyle, and this, of course, has implications for coronal and root surface caries. Poorly controlled diabetes results in polyuria and this, in turn, may impact on resting salivary parameters. Periodontitis in these patients may exacerbate the severity of the diabetic state by decreasing glycaemic control [23].

Psychological stress and salivary flow
A patient’s psychological state can influence their resting salivary output to a reasonable extent [24]. Individuals with a high level of stress in their workplace or home environment can show prolonged reductions in resting salivary flow rate. This can arise because of alterations in sympathetic and parasympathetic output that occur with emotional and psychological stress.

Hormonal variations and salivary flow
Hormones can stimulate water‐saving mechanisms thus influencing salivary flow and composition through direct action on acinar or ductal elements within the salivary glands. The anti‐ diuretic hormone, which increases water reabsorption in the distal tubule in the kidney, has the same action on salivary gland ducts. As a consequence, a reduced volume and increased viscosity of saliva in the anterior floor of the mouth may contribute to a sensation of thirst. However, thirst itself is an imperfect indicator of body fluid balance.
There are several known effects of hormonal changes on salivary parameters in women. Oral contraceptive use causes a higher buffer capacity. In pregnancy, there is reduced buffer capacity. This effect declines, and then reverses in very late pregnancy, just before delivery, when salivary buffer capacity rises.

Menopause can cause lowered resting flow rates, but this effect is not seen in all women [25–27].

Gum chewing and salivary function
Chewing sugar‐free gum can be an effective preventive oral health behaviour because it leads to a prolonged increase in both the stimulated and unstimulated salivary flow. It augments the protective properties of saliva such as oral clearance of dissolved sugars, buffering, pH, and super‐saturation with minerals. It will elevate plaque pH and this can be enhanced by including phosphopeptides in the gum. This has been demonstrated by Reynolds et al., who assessed demineralized slices of enamel mounted in intra‐oral appliances
[28,29,30,31]. A similar elevation in pH can be achieved by using alkalinizing agents such as urea within the gum formulation [31].
Long‐term chewing of xylitol or xylitol‐sorbitol gums has been shown to lead to a reduction in caries risk over five years
[32, 33]. During gum chewing, the saliva flow rate peaks during the first minute and can be maintained by continual chewing. It will not increase dramatically by chewing faster, with flow rate being maintained over a range of chewing frequencies between
35 and 130 chewing actions per minute.
It is important to stress that gums containing sucrose must be avoided. They are less stimulatory to salivary flow than sugar‐ free gums, do not promote mineralization, but are directly cariogenic through sustained release of sucrose [34].

### Diseases Causing Loss of Tooth Structure

This section examines strategies for the prevention and treatment of caries and related tooth structure loss, from the perspective of unusual behavioural, lifestyle and health issues.
The co‐existence of several factors, such as bacterial plaque, fermentable carbohydrates, a susceptible tooth surface and time, is necessary for caries to occur. It is sensible, therefore, to base caries preventive strategies on several independent elements, rather than one element alone. For example, lifestyle factors that may suppress salivary flow include smoking, caffeine and a low water intake. On the other hand, actions that elevate salivary pH and buffer capacity include regular use of sugar‐free chewing gums, and sodium bicarbonate mouthrinses.
Bacterial species, both acidogenic and aciduric, flourish in low pH environments. Refined carbohydrates and acidic foodstuffs therefore encourage growth of bacteria such as Streptococcus sobrinus and Streptococcus mutans. The biofilm‐tooth interface is the medium in which the disease progresses, and the biochemistry of that medium is strongly influenced by saliva.
It is therefore not surprising that salivary parameters such as flow rate, ionic composition, buffer capacity, and fluoride concentration exert a major influence on the equilibrium between demineralization and remineralization.

Exposure to fluoride
Frequent exposure to fluoride achieves optimal low‐level loading of the salivary fluoride reservoir sufficient to prevent the initiation and progression of caries. At low concentrations, fluoride inhibits demineralization and enhances remineralization through precipitation of calcium phosphates, and the formation of fluorapatite (see Chapter 2).
When present in high concentrations, fluoride can inhibit bacterial enzymes and reduce the utilization of fermentable substrates by S. mutans. At levels of 0.5 mmol fluoride/L within plaque, the cell cytoplasm of S. mutans is acidified, and glycolysis is inhibited once the intracellular pH reaches pH 5.2. This accounts for 75% of the caries inhibition effect of high concentrations of fluoride, with the remaining 25% due to the combined effect of fluoride on enamel solubility. However, because this does not reduce the number of viable bacteria, some form of antibacterial agent is essential for caries control in the high‐risk patient.
There is evidence that glass‐ionomer cement can suppress cariogenic micro‐organisms through fluoride release (see
Chapter 9). Clinical studies have examined bacterial counts in carious dentine under resin‐modified glass‐ionomer and amalgam restorations over a six‐month or two‐year period.

Fermentable substrates in the diet
Lifestyles with a high frequency of intake of fermentable substrates will increase caries rates so assessment must include questioning regarding patterns of snacking. Snacks include carbonated drinks or energy drinks, particularly those containing caffeine (Figure 16.5), as well as tea and coffee.
Sucrose, glucose and other fermentable substrates in the diet can be replaced with non‐fermentable sweeteners such as xylitol, sorbitol or isomalt (a chemically modified carbohydrate), or an artificial sweetener with no chemical similarity to carbohydrates, such as aspartame or saccharin.
Xylitol is taken up by S. mutans, requiring the organisms to expend energy, but is not fermentable, so no acid fermentation by‐products will be released. This means it is not cariogenic, however, recently attention has been directed to the effects of prolonged consumption of xylitol. Chewing a xylitol gum regularly for extended periods can suppress salivary levels of S. mutans, and appears to select for S. mutans with impaired adhesion properties, that is, these bacteria bind poorly to teeth and shed easily from plaque to saliva. This means that frequent chewing of xylitol gum by pregnant women may be associated with reduced mother‐to‐child transmission of these bacteria.
This conclusion is supported by clinical trials.
Soft drinks are often a major contributor to total sucrose intake because they contain typically 12–13% sucrose by weight.
This means a 375 mL can of soft drink contains 50 grams of sucrose, and a 600 mL bottle about 80 grams. A high total daily intake of sucrose can lead to insulin resistance and Type 2 diabetes and this is a leading cause of kidney failure and blindness, and a major risk factor for death from cardiovascular disease and stroke. It can also lead to obesity and it must be noted that there is an epidemic of this in Western countries.

Dental caries is a true infectious disease that fulfils Koch’s postulates. The transmission of S. mutans from mother to child via saliva has been demonstrated in numerous studies. Behaviour patterns include kissing, sampling of food, and sharing spoons or feeding utensils. The child’s own saliva provides a level of protection from early childhood caries but this is limited to the mandibular incisor teeth.

Frequent intake of acid in food or drinks
A high frequency of exposure to dietary acids in the form of soft drinks, sports drinks, and fruit juice can shift the supra‐gingival oral flora toward aciduric micro‐organisms. As the intra‐oral pH falls, the numbers and proportions of S. mutans and lactobacilli increase, and the proportions of acid‐sensitive species such as Fusobacterium nucleatum, Streptococcus gordonii and Streptococcus oralis will fall. Thus, the fall in pH not only enhances the competitiveness of cariogenic organisms but inhibits the growth and metabolism of species not associated with caries.
Chemostat studies of plaque bacteria have shown that certain

species, other than S. mutans or lactobacilli, are competitive at pH values low enough to demineralize enamel, e.g. Veillonella dispar. They may be found within the plaque involved in the initiation of dental caries, but are likely to be secondary colonizers rather than primary pathogens.

The challenge with this strategy, if used in isolation, is that mature plaque is a highly ordered structure with numerous metabolic interactions between bacteria. The microbial community has already achieved a high level of homeostasis that will make it somewhat resistant to deliberate external manipulation.

Endogenous acids
Acidic conditions can be created by eating disorders or gastrointestinal conditions, such as chronic gastric reflux, in which the source of the acid will be hydrochloric acid from the stomach. Stronger acids can degrade pellicle by proteolysis, and thus predispose the tooth surface to mineral loss from corrosion (Figure 16.6). However, these conditions predispose to caries as well because of the likelihood of greatly increased counts of S. mutans through their preference for highly acid conditions. A preventive programme for these patients must address the microbial risk factors for caries as well as the corrosion potential.

Eating disorders
Eating disorders such as anorexia nervosa, bulimia, and binge eating are most prevalent in adolescent girls and young adult women. They can have dramatic effects on oral health, particularly non‐carious loss of tooth structure, through corrosion.
Typically there will be adverse physiological changes beyond the oral impact.
They are psychiatric disorders characterized by abnormal eating patterns that result in both dramatic weight loss and serious general health consequences. Bulimia is more common in late teenage and early adult years. There will be refusal to maintain body weight over a minimal normal weight for age and height, an intense fear of gaining weight, and a distorted body image.
There are two subtypes:
• those who drastically limit their food intake and avoid eating;
• those who purge after eating.

Gastric purging can cause dramatic loss of tooth structure from the corrosive effects of gastric hydrochloric acid. Proteolysis of pellicle removes this important protective layer of salivary glycoproteins, exposing the tooth surface directly to the gastric contents. Patients typically will clean their teeth after purging, resulting in abrasion of the softened enamel. Purging can also induce oesophageal inflammation, protein malnutrition, dehydration, and electrolyte imbalances. Bruises, calluses, scarring, fungal infections of the nail beds and abrasions of the fingers may be present from repeated episodes of self‐induced vomiting.
Patients presenting with sensitive exposed dentine and palatal erosion of the maxillary anterior teeth are likely candidates for a disorder involving regurgitation of gastric contents. However, consideration should be given first to the more common condition of gastric reflux, which can be attributed to the side effects of several medications, as well as to pregnancy and hiatus hernia. Caffeine will increase gastric acid production and may increase the severity of corrosive and abrasive tooth wear on palatal surfaces of maxillary incisor teeth.

Cigarette smoking
There is an extensive literature addressing the impacts of smoking on oral health. The vaso‐constrictive effects of nicotine can result in suppressed resting saliva flow, with consequential decreased pH and buffer capacity. In addition, smoking alters the ecology of the oral flora through enhanced growth of facultative anaerobes in supragingival and subgingival plaque. Smokers have higher levels of dental plaque and higher caries rates than non‐smokers.
The saliva film in smokers is thinner and slower moving. Its poorer defensive capabilities explain why smokers are more likely to develop carious lesions on the proximal surfaces of posterior teeth and the cervical aspects of maxillary anterior teeth and mandibular premolars (Figure 16.7).
Smoking also suppresses the healing of oral wounds, and this contributes to an increased severity of destructive periodontitis.
There is an increased risk of malignancies in the oral cavity and in other sites such as the larynx, bladder, and pancreas. Acute necrotizing ulcerative gingivitis, mucosal pigmentation, and halitosis are also strongly linked to smoking.

Unpleasant withdrawal symptoms (irritability, anxiety, difficulty concentrating and mood disturbances) appear as soon as blood levels of nicotine drop after a cigarette (within 1–2 hours) and smokers crave a cigarette to relieve these symptoms. Sensory stimuli such as the taste and feel of tobacco become associated with the relief of symptoms and so act as a cue to want to smoke [9].

Dental caries and general health
Severe dental caries can impact on general health through a range of mechanisms. Loss of teeth can affect masticatory function, leading to changes in food selection and nutrition. Dental pain can cause significant morbidity and alterations in lifestyle as particular types of foods or mandibular movements may be avoided. It will also disrupt sleep and affect work performance.
When endodontic lesions develop as a consequence of untreated caries, a range of acute phase proteins and cytokines are released into the circulation with possible systemic effects including fever and malaise. Bacterial infection may arise from an endodontic focus in patients with severely compromised immune systems [43].
Masticatory function may decline with tooth loss, leading to modification to the nutritional status. Food may be overcooked to soften it, leading to reduced levels of vitamins. Masticatory efficiency will reduce as opposing tooth units are lost or become painful and unstable dentures can lead to a reduction in resting salivary flow. Several studies have shown an important link between masticatory function and the loss of teeth. One study [44] showed that 38% of 1,160 elderly patients reported difficulties in chewing at least one of six common foods, despite the fact that 57% of the dentate patients and virtually all of the edentulous patients wore dentures. There was strong link between difficulty in chewing and the loss of teeth. Nearly 60% of edentulous patients reported difficulty in chewing, whereas only 6% of patients with fewer than nine missing teeth reported problems chewing common foods.

Anti‐bacterial therapy
Chlorhexidine (CHX) is regarded as the ‘gold standard’ as a mouthrinse or topical treatment for suppressing S. mutans.
Unlike fluoride, chlorhexidine does not directly affect the ionic reactions of remineralization, but is effective solely on the cariogenic micro‐flora. This means that, because CHX is highly effective against S. mutans, it is particularly useful as part of caries prevention programme, as distinct from a caries control programme. It can cause a 1000‐fold reduction in salivary levels with a single treatment. However, the levels will re‐establish over time so intermittent use is required [1, 45].

CHX can be delivered in a variety of ways including varnishes, mouthrinses, gels, dentifrices, and chewing gums. The gel form can be applied using a toothbrush or in a custom‐built tray. Delivery using a chewing gum vehicle has the advantage of simplicity and the possibility of combining chlorhexidene with xylitol to reduce salivary levels of S. mutans, lactobacilli and yeasts.
Problems with CHX mouthrinse include:
• taste alterations;
• staining of the teeth, tongue, and restoration margins;
• increased calculus formation;
• mucosal irritation from alcohol‐based mouthrinses.
Advantages of CHX gel products over rinses are:
• no mucosal irritation;
• reduced influence on taste perception;
• reduced staining if used intermittently;
• lower cost;
• excellent patient compliance.

Home care programmes for patients with salivary dysfunction
Frequent use of bland rinses containing saline or sodium bicarbonate and the application of rehydrating or mucosal protective products, such as Laclede Oral Balance gel, can provide relief from symptoms of oral dryness. Patients with profound xerostomia suffer from mucosal irritation and many are unable to tolerate the use of some commercial rinses that contain alcohol or flavouring agents. Salivary stimulants, such as pilocarpine, may be useful but may lead to side effects. It is essential that sugar and acid‐containing sweets be discouraged as salivary stimulants because they accelerate caries in the presence of low saliva flow. Patients should be encouraged to apply a lip lubricant at regular intervals.
Use of sugarless chewing gums is of major importance for promoting salivary function. The antibacterial, buffering, and lubricant qualities of saliva improve with increasing flow rates, so that stimulating the flow with gum chewing will increase the protection from the salivary secretions. Other ingredients in chewing gums, such as xylitol and phosphopeptides, provide additional preventive benefits and assist in maintaining oral health.
Home use of a neutral sodium fluoride gel or high fluoride dentifrice on a daily basis will maintain salivary fluoride concentrations and inhibit mineral loss. Neutral fluoride gel may be applied on a toothbrush but acidulated fluoride products are contraindicated for patients with severe salivary dysfunction because they may cause erosion, dentine hypersensitivity, or mucosal irritation.
Xylitol and phosphopeptides exert modest suppressive effects on S. mutans, however, additional antimicrobial targeting is worthwhile in patients with very high caries activity. A water‐based
0.2% chlorhexidine gluconate gel, applied with a tooth‐brush, is

the agent of choice for chemical plaque control in patients with salivary dysfunction, since it possesses proven anti‐plaque, anti‐ gingivitis, and anti‐caries activity. The alcohol content of some of the other chlorhexidine rinses is not recommended because it may cause mucosal burning and may reduce saliva flow.
Topical CPP‐ACP preparations are available as topical thick pastes for clinical use in tray carriers or brushed directly onto the teeth. In addition to their value in preventing caries or erosion, they control cervical hypersensitivity, a condition often associated with reduced salivary flow or low pH conditions. The thick creamy consistency of CPP‐ACP products is well suited for self‐application by patients and is completely safe if ingested and this is an important consideration for products that will be used by patients in their own home.
Antifungal therapy may be required in xerostomic patients with a history of recurring oral candidosis. Chlorhexidine gel exerts mild antifungal effects and can be used together with alkalinizing mouthrinses, such as sodium bicarbonate, to suppress levels of Candida species. If dedicated topical antifungal agents are required, care must be taken that the preparation is free of sucrose or other fermentable substrates. Suspensions or lozenges of nystatin or amphotericin are used commonly for treatment of candidoses. Tissue surfaces beneath dentures and the denture surface itself are typically heavily contaminated with fungal organisms. The denture surfaces can be cleaned and then the denture immersed for a short period in dilute sodium hypochlorite to reduce the fungal load. In addition, antifungal creams or suspensions such as miconazole can be directly applied to the fitting surface of a denture, and the denture returned to the mouth to give a sustained effect. Severe denture‐related fungal infections require expert attention.
Regular dental review (at least three‐monthly recall) is important for ensuring that oral hygiene is maintained at an adequate standard, and caries and other conditions are controlled. Maintenance dental sessions should include oral hygiene reinforcement, removal of plaque, and surface treatments with fluoride varnish or phosphopeptides. Adequate monitoring will ensure that the oral health status is maintained, and the need for extensive restorative work is minimized.
