### 9.1

### Introduction

The European Society of Endodontology (ESE) defines cracked teeth as those having a crack extending into the dentine, of unknown depth or size, which may extend subcrestal into the root. There may be pulpal involvement [1]. Cracked teeth are uncommon in children, adolescents, and younger adults and are more often seen in those over 40 years of age. There is a predilection for mandibular molars, followed by maxillary molars and premolars, without a clear gender disposition [2–4].

The prevalence of cracked teeth (10–70%) varies depending on the time period, geographic locations, and whether it is reported from general or specialist practice [5,
6], with at least one review reporting that cracked teeth are the third most common reason for tooth loss in industrialized countries [7]. The scale of the problem has reportedly increased in the last decade [8, 9]. This was recently highlighted by the American

### 9.2.1

### Assessing the Risk Profile

There are three main components to consider when evaluating a patient with a potentially cracked tooth.

Q1: Is there an environmental issue in this dentition?
Q2: Is there a loading issue in this dentition?
Q3: Is there a structural issue in this dentition?

These three factors often overlap and coexist, increasing the likelihood of crack initiation and propagation.

Habits and Parafunction
Chewing on ice, chewing on hard objects, eating coarse food, consuming hot and cold food in quick succession, and unilateral mastication are some of the habits associated with cracked teeth [15]. The final element in the history should be ascertaining the possibilities of sleep bruxism and or daytime clenching. In this context, knowing some fundamental details of bruxism (sleep and awake) is important.

The global prevalence of bruxism (sleep and awake) is around 22% [16]. Sleep bruxism is associated with increased duration and frequency of masseter contractions, and greater tooth–tooth contact time [17, 18]. As a general rule, the proportion of occlusal forces on molar regions increases as clenching strength increases, and the proportion of force on premolars and anterior teeth decreases with increased clenching strength [19]. However, daytime clenching (awake bruxism) is associated with non-functional tooth contact, increased masseter muscle activity, and temporomandibular disorder-related symptoms and is significantly influenced by psychological stress [20, 21]. Daytime clenching is often associated with myogenic facial pain, tension-type headache, and submaximal clenching [20, 22, 23]. The pertinent relation between these sleep disorders and cracked teeth is not limited to the possibility of cyclic fatigue-induced crack initiation. Individuals with bruxism may present with myofascial pain and a need to differentiate referral pain from the masseter (and or temporalis) to the molars, from symptoms associated with cracked teeth
[24]. These individuals are particularly susceptible to cracking their virgin molars.

Medication and Medical Conditions
The presence of certain medical conditions and the relationship between antipsychotic medications and bruxism are inconclusive [23]. However, there seems to be a possible association between selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and bruxism [25, 26]. Hence, the potential for both myogenic pain and cracked teeth should be considered in subjects on SSRIs presenting with an elusive toothache.

### 9.2.2

### Listening to the Patient

Cracked molars can present with solitary or a combination of symptoms. These range from sensitivity to cold, sensitivity to heat, pain on biting, and spontaneous pain and various combinations of symptoms [11, 27]. However, nearly half of the cracked molars presenting to general dental practices are asymptomatic [11, 28].

Sensitivity to Cold
Sensitivity to cold can often be an early sign and may be mistaken for dentin hypersensitivity. Of interest is the fact that many of the symptomatic cases in an extensive general dental practice-based research network study had sensitivity to cold as the sole symptom [11]. Unfortunately, sensitivity to cold seems to be a symptom that clinicians often do not associate with cracked teeth [27].

On the other end of the spectrum, the pain intensity can be severe, diffuse, and poorly localized with radiation to the opposing jaw, anterior teeth, and extraoral sites such as the ear and the temple region. When confronted with such a presentation, it is essential to remember that referred pain often follows a cephalic direction, and hence, it is more likely for pain from mandibular molar pulpitis to be referred to maxillary teeth than the contrary [29]. However, referral pain in a posterior-to-­ anterior direction should also be considered [29].

Pain on Biting
The emphasis on pain on biting as a predominant symptom in cracked tooth is based on presentations at specialist practice and denotes a more advanced state of crack propagation [12, 13]. It is not an exclusive symptom of a cracked tooth, nor a requirement to be categorized as a cracked tooth, but pain on biting (particularly in a specific direction) should raise a high index of suspicion for a cracked tooth. More importantly, the absence of pain on biting should not be used to exclude a cracked tooth, as they form only 33% of the symptomatic cracked teeth presentations to general dental practice [28].

Loss of Function
An important gauge in triaging patients in pain is ascertaining the extent of loss of function. This can vary from avoiding chewing hard food on a particular side to protecting a region with the tongue while having beverages at certain temperatures, or it may involve completely guarding a region due to extreme pain (Fig. 9.1).

Location and Size of Occlusal Contact and Preferential Wear
Assessing the size of the occlusal contact is critical, as very high stress can be reached when the contact area is smaller, even in those with low biting force [37].
The location of occlusal contact in centric and lateral sliding positions helps to evaluate the length of the moment arm from the pivot point (which is usually the base of the fissure or restoration–tooth interface). A clinical tip for ensuring the occlusal contact points are not washed off while assessing multiple centric and translational contacts is to coat the Shimshock with Vaseline. This helps in documentation and patient education on the reasons for cracking. As a general rule, areas of preferential wear should be noted, as they often indicate areas of eccentric loading (Fig. 9.5).

Steepness of Cuspal Inclines and Integrity of Marginal Ridge and
Proximal Contact
The location of the occlusal contact should be evaluated along with the steepness of the cuspal inclines (Fig. 9.5). Cusps with steeper cuspal inclines create significant wedging force at the base of occlusal fissures [38]. Similarly, loss of integrity of the marginal ridge results in crack initiation at significantly lower occlusal load compared to those with an intact marginal ridge [39].

### 9.3

### Tools and Methods to Detect Cracks

A recent study evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of transillumination, DIAGNOcam, microscope, and macrophotography showed that none of the tests have high accuracy when micro-CT was used as the gold standard [40]. Of concern is that 20% of the teeth extracted, suspecting cracked teeth, were found not to have any cracks extending into the dentin [40]. Hence, these tools should not be used in isolation to diagnose cracked teeth.

Bite test and transillumination are popular tests for detecting cracked teeth
[41–43]. Bite tests are performed using Tooth Slooth or Frac Finder to elicit pain on release of bite by selectively loading individual cusps [42, 43]. However, the repeatability of this test is unpredictable, as the angle at which the load is applied must align with the crack to elicit a positive response. Transillumination highlights a range of surface flaws, such as enamel infractions and anatomical defects, such as fissures and grooves, and will often yield a positive finding in most posterior teeth (without the presence of a crack that involves dentin). The large number of false positives this test could detect is highlighted in Fig. 9.6. A microscope with high magnification (20–25×) or macro-photography using ring flash is optimal for confirming the absence of cracks, as indicated by the reported high specificity of 92.9%
[40]. Staining with methylene blue may help visualize cracks across the marginal ridges, but it is often difficult to discern between a deep fissure and a crack. In any case, examination under a dry field with illumination and magnification is essential.

Advanced Imaging and Other Technologies for Crack Detection
Magnetic resonance imaging [44], optical coherence tomography [45], lasers [46], quantitative light fluorescence [47], and quantitative percussion diagnostics [48] are some of the innovative tools that seem promising. However, none of them were tested following the STARD guidelines.

Pulp Sensibility Tests
The assessment of pulpal status using pulp sensibility tests should be carried out, and a range of responses should be expected from those suggestive of pulpitis to pulp necrosis [14, 41]. Pulpal necrosis in the absence of other obvious causes such as caries, defective restoration, or traumatic dental injury may often be due to a longitudinal crack extending from the occlusal surface into the pulp [14].

Radiographs
Periapical and bitewing radiographs should be part of the standard examination, and subtle loss of crestal lamina dura, angular defects, posterior teeth with shallow restoration, signs of relative difference in pulp chamber calcifications, and cuspal profiles should be specifically assessed. A high-resolution, small field-of-view cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) may be helpful in cases where radicular crack extension is suspected [49]. Though cracks are usually challenging to detect in the
CBCT scans, localized loss of crestal bone (angular defect, see Fig. 9.7) often serves as a surrogate marker for cracked teeth with radicular extension [50].

Removal of Restoration
In teeth suspected of having cracks, particularly those with amalgam restorations, removal of the restoration and assessment of the pulpal floor of the cavity and base of the cusps are imperative. Once the presence of a crack is confirmed, a structured assessment of the crack should follow.

### 9.4

### Management Options for Cracked Molars

Unfortunately, there does not seem to be a consensus on how best to manage cracked teeth [27, 51]. This variability is highlighted in several surveys on treatment recommendations for cracked teeth across different nations [27, 51–53].

A common misconception is that all cracked teeth are symptomatic. However, around 55% of the teeth with cracks in a National (general) Dental Practice-Based
Research Network (NDPBRN) study were asymptomatic [4]. Interestingly, studies conducted at specialist endodontic practices or endodontic departments of academic institutions report that cracked teeth are mostly symptomatic [12, 41]. This key difference in practice settings should be considered when evaluating the literature in this space [53]. Other reasons for differences in recommendations are the variability in the definition of a cracked tooth, the lack of accurate diagnostic tools for three-­dimensional mapping of the crack, and the lack of well-controlled intervention studies.

### 9.4.1

### Can Cracked Teeth Be Monitored Without Intervention?

In the NDPBRN study, 2858 cracked teeth with vital pulp were seen by 209 general dental practitioners, of which 65% were advised monitoring without intervention
[54]. Of these recommended for monitoring, only 20% had a change of scenario that required treatment during the 3-year follow-up period. It should be considered that the key criterion for enrollment was a vital posterior tooth that had at least one observable external crack [4]. In other words, this was a loose definition, (unlike the one used in this chapter and by both the AAE and the ESE) where an extension of the defect into dentin was not required for enrolment in the NDPBRN study. In addition, around half of the 2858 cracked teeth were asymptomatic. In the absence of clinically applicable tools that can assess the depth of a surface defect, it is important to assess whether the structural, environmental, and loading factors discussed earlier are favorable for crack initiation and propagation (see Box 9.1). A decision to monitor should only be made after systematically assessing the context of each case (Fig. 9.8).

### 9.4.2

### Cracked Teeth with Reversible Pulpitis

The main objectives of management are preventing further crack progression and providing an environment for the pulp to recover and resolve symptoms. A key determinant is ensuring that the crack is limited to the coronal aspect with no radicular extension. These cases usually have an existing shallow restoration or are unrestored. In cases with amalgam restorations in particular, removal of the restoration and assessment of the crack extension is essential [12, 55]. Direct composite resin restoration with a resin-modified glass ionomer liner with or without cuspal coverage should be performed. The tooth should then be secured using a stainless-steel band luted with resin-modified glass ionomer cement in the initial visit [56]. Clinical

### 9.4.4

### Should Cracks Be Removed?

There are no randomized control trials that have tested tooth and pulp survival with or without crack removal. Though cracks often have the presence of bacteria, complete removal of bacteria from dentin is not required for the pulp to remain vital and asymptomatic [61–63]. However, inflammatory cell infiltration of the pulp should be expected in such situations, and the long-term survival of the pulp would depend on the integrity of the seal of the restoration [62]. A minimally invasive approach without removal of the crack preserves the tooth structure and delays the restorative cycle [64]. In addition, the pulp survival with or without crack removal ranges from
76% to 80%, showing that there is no inherent advantage in pursuing removal of cracks, and individual circumstances should be carefully evaluated to improve the outcome [12, 57].

### 9.4.5

### Cracked Teeth with Irreversible Pulpitis/Pulp Necrosis

Cracked teeth with irreversible pulpitis can sometimes present significant challenges in locating the offending tooth, mainly when associated with diffuse pain.
They often continue undiagnosed for a significant period of time [65]. This could be due to persistent bilateral activation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase in the trigeminal subnucleus caudalis and central sensitization [66, 67]. The convergence of afferent neural inputs from regions outside the orofacial receptive field onto the trigeminal subnucleus caudalis might also explain the referred pain associated with this condition [66, 68]. Hence, a careful assessment considering the factors highlighted in Boxes 9.1 and 9.2 is essential to identify the offending tooth with severe pulpitis. Strategies for achieving successful local anesthesia in such situations are described in Chap. 4 of this book.

Once the restoration, caries, and pulp are removed and a clean field is obtained, further systematic crack assessment should be carried out, as suggested in Box 9.4.
This is often a good time to consider strategically redoing the periodontal probing, particularly if new cracks or branching of cracks are identified. Cracks crossing the pulp chamber, those that can be detected with explorer, more than 5 mm of periodontal probing, terminal tooth, and multiple cracks are often associated with reduced survival [1, 69, 70].

A cracked tooth in an unrestored or minimally restored tooth with pulp necrosis is often called “fracture necrosis,” which implies radicular extension in a corono-­ apical direction [14]. These are often associated with deep isolated periodontal probing defects, and the prognosis is poor if the probing depth is more than 5 mm
[1]. The key factor here is that the presence of necrotic pulp is not an indicator of poor prognosis, and careful assessment of the above-mentioned factors (Box 9.4) is essential [71]. Deep periodontal probing associated with a corono-apical crack should be distinguished from vertical root fracture, which is more commonly seen in endodontically treated teeth, and the crack originates from the apex of the tooth.
Another differential diagnosis is a split tooth, which is a longitudinal crack in a mesio-distal direction with complete visible separation of an entire tooth into two parts.

There are two other critical elements to consider in cracked teeth following endodontic treatment. Irrespective of whether endodontic treatment was initiated due to pulpitis or pulp necrosis, endodontically treated teeth associated with cracks should receive a full crown as early as possible [13]. The relative risk of extraction is 11 times more if a full crown is not provided [60]. The second factor is the management of teeth with cracks extending beyond the root canal orifice. In these situations, there is merit in placing the obturation material 2–3 mm apical to the deepest extension of the crack [72]. The coronal aspect of the root canal involving the crack may be restored with a fluoride-releasing resin as an extended orifice barrier [72].
This is followed by core build-up and full crown at the earliest. This modality of managing cracks with intracanal extension has been shown to have a survival rate of
96% in a 4-year follow-up study [72]. Overall, the survival rate of cracked teeth requiring endodontic treatment ranges from 84% to 88% over a 1–5-year follow-up period [1].

### 9.5

### Conclusions

A systematic approach, including careful observation of high-risk extraoral features, listening carefully to patient symptoms, specifically looking for hard tissue risk factors, and understanding the limitations of diagnostic tools, is imperative for assessing potentially cracked teeth. A global outlook with an emphasis on environmental, loading, and structural issues leading to cracked teeth should form part of the examination process. The triad of these elements, along with pulpal and periradicular involvement, will dictate the management options. In most cases, cracked teeth can be successfully treated, and the emphasis should be on formulating a plan
