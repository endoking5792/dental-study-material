Terminology in dentistry, particularly in endodontics, is crucial for effective communication among researchers, clinicians and students. However, many anatomical terms related to root and canal morphology are inconsistent and controversial. This review provides a critical analysis of these terminological inconsistencies and their impact on research, education and clinical practice.

Inconsistent use is present in terminology in root and canal anatomy, and this creates confusion in research, education and clinical practice. Key findings were

1.	 There are controversial terms describing root anatomy.
2.	 Terminology in pulp chamber anatomy is inconsistent.
3.	 Areas with controversies in root canal anatomy:

•	 Canal isthmuses and transverse canal anastomoses
•	 Apical canal bifurcations
•	 Accessory canals and foramina

The authors emphasise the need for a globally accepted, standardised terminology to enhance accuracy, improve study comparability and ensure better communication in dentistry.

### 1.1

### Introduction

A central goal of cleaning and shaping procedures in endodontics is to obtain a debrided root canal system that is in its entirety free of microbiota and debris.
Therefore, detailed knowledge about root canal anatomy prior to any access to the root canal system is absolutely mandatory [1]. Moreover, complex root canal anatomy in molar roots should be expected in every single case. Root and root canal anatomy directly impacts practice and procedures for access cavity preparation, canal shaping and obturation and, in fact, most procedures in molar endodontics.

It is well established that intra-radicular microflora is the main cause for developing, or persisting, apical periodontitis [2]. Unfortunately, the intra-radicular infection mainly consists not of planktonic bacteria but of well-organised biofilms. The bacteria in biofilms show a higher pathogenicity compared to their planktonic counterparts [3]. More than 400 different bacterial species were found in root canal systems of teeth with necrotic pulps [4]. Interactions between different species within endodontic biofilms lead to enhanced stress resistance [5]. Their location within complex molar root canal configurations makes complete eradication of endodontic biofilms virtually impossible; even reducing the microbial burden below a biologically acceptable threshold demands careful canal debridement. It is safe to say that an in-depth understanding of root canal anatomy is of utmost importance for successful molar endodontics.

### 1.1.1

### Components of the Root Canal System and Classifications

In roots with round cross-sectional shapes, the number of root canals corresponds in most cases to the number of roots. However, an oval-shaped root may have more than one canal [1]. The immense complexity of molar canal configurations is based on a wide range of root canal curvatures, different root canal sections, different accessory canals, fins and isthmuses. Different attempts of classification contributed to a deeper understanding of root canal anatomy. There are numerous classifications for anatomical variations in root canals. Weine and co-workers [6] examined in a laboratory study the mesio-buccal roots of maxillary molars and classified these into four and later in five types as shown in Fig. 1.1.

A more detailed classification is recommended to more accurately describe the internal root canal configurations of individual molar roots. One of the most commonly used classifications is the one by Vertucci [7] with eight different canal morphologies (Fig. 1.2a).

However, if there are more than two canals within one root, this classification is again limited. Gulabivala et al. [8] further developed this classification into additional nine morphology types. Especially for describing the root canal formation of the mesial root in mandibular molars, types 1, 2 and 3 of this classification are meaningful (Fig. 1.2b).

Another research group [9] extended Vertucci’s classification with additional 14 configurations examining 2800 extracted human teeth. These further developments

### 1.2

### The Anatomy of Maxillary Molars

A sufficient root canal treatment in maxillary molars is based on an optimal access to and preparation of all existing root canals. The goal of treatment is to present the existing anatomy as comprehensively as possible and widen the root canal system to enable a sufficient disinfection and filling. The anatomy of maxillary molars is very complex, and the root canal treatment of this particular group of teeth represents a major challenge for dentists [1]. Carabelli documented the particular anatomy of maxillary molars as early as 1844 [14]. Numerous subsequent publications discussed the complexity of maxillary molar anatomy; most often the mesio-buccal

In 1917, Walter Hess [15] presented the anatomical complexities with a multitude of branches and accessory canals by illustrating in detail the number and formation of root canals. He was the first to relate age and gender of the patients to root canal complexity.

Many studies have shown the anatomy of the upper first molar and especially the presence of an MB2 canal using different techniques [16]. Failures in root canal treatment of this tooth type are often based on untreated MB2 canals [6, 17]. The clinical prevalence of the MB2 canal in maxillary first molars and second molars is reported in up to 93% and 60%, respectively [18].

Results from laboratory micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) studies are of special interest for molar anatomy because this technique allows a three-­dimensional presentation and analysis of the root canal system without damaging the tooth structures [19]. Due to modern treatment methods like the use of dental microscopes, options for a successful therapy of difficult root canal anatomies have significantly improved [20]. The location of MB2 canals during root canal treatment of maxillary molars is much more likely by applying the dental microscope and through the use of specialised tools than without magnification [18].

The first maxillary molar is the most voluminous of all teeth; it has four pulp horns, and the pulp chamber usually has a rhomboid cross-sectional shape [1]. The second maxillary molar in principle is of similar shape (Fig. 1.6a). However, the pulp chamber is often more long-oval, sometimes it is ribbon-shaped (Fig. 1.6b).

A maxillary first molar has typically three separate roots, and in only about 4% of the cases just two roots are found. Two or more merged roots occur in about 5% of all cases. The presence of four roots is extremely rare [16]. In the second maxillary molars, merging of roots is much more common. Interestingly, the disto-buccal (DB) root canal in the second maxillary molars is often difficult to negotiate because an s-shaped DB root is quite a common finding.

In the literature, three mesio-buccal root canals have been described to occur up to 7% in first maxillary molars [23].

Below this ledge, the canal path leads towards mesio-palatal before curving back to the centre and apically.

This makes the insertion or the negotiation of these canals very challenging. By removing these obstructions and carefully tracking the canal structure towards mesial and apical using ultrasonic tips or long-shank round burs (‘troughing or countersinking’), these obstacles can be removed until a straight access to the MB2 canal is possible. It could be necessary to extend troughing 0.5–3 mm deep, which risks perforating the pulpal floor towards the furcation area [24]. During initial instrumentation and shaping of MB2 canals, there is the risk of a strip perforation because of the cross-sectional shape of the MB root (Fig. 1.9). A brushing movement towards the mesial aspect of the root is recommended [25].

Once we successfully managed to initially instrument the canal, a further challenge could be to reach the apical foramen in MB2 canals because of two reasons.
First, these canals are often very narrow and curved. Second, the chance to encounter a Vertucci class V, VI or VII in MB root systems is not uncommon (Fig. 1.2a). It remains a challenge for the clinician to recognise a deviation of the MB2 canal in the middle or apical third to the palatal aspect and successfully prepare such deviations.

According to Vertucci [7], disto-buccal roots of maxillary molars show one canal in 100% of the cases. Other authors described the occurrence of a second disto-­ buccal canal in first maxillary molars with an incidence of 1.6–9.5%. However, in up to 98% of these cases, the two canals merge in one apical foramen and only 2% show two or more canals at the apex of the disto-buccal root [16].

A rather rare variation of first maxillary molars is the C-shaped canal system, which is formed from merged disto-buccal and palatal roots with a prevalence of
0.1% [26, 27].

### 1.3

### The Anatomy of Mandibular Molars

The mandibular molars, particularly the mandibular first molar, are the most frequently endodontically treated teeth. Their treatment offers a variety of anatomical challenges. That complexity includes multiple canals, isthmuses, lateral canals and apical ramifications. Additionally, the distal dentinal wall of the mesial root is thin and known as the so-called ‘danger zone’ [28, 29] (Fig. 1.10).

Harris et al. [29] scanned 22 first molars using micro-CT and showed that 1.5 mm below the furcation in the mesial root radicular wall thickness towards the furcation was the smallest, with values of 0.81–1.22 mm. Comparable results were obtained by others [30], showing an average dentin thickness of 1.2–1.3 mm measured also
1.5 mm beneath the furcation area.

Using the data from Harris et al. [29] regarding the pulp chamber floor, the shortest and longest distance between the orifices of the mesial canals averaged between about 1.4 and 3 mm. All scanned teeth in that study showed a single distal canal opening at the measured level 1.5 mm coronal to the furcation area, while there are two canals more apical more frequently [31]. The buccolingual dimension of the distal canal was about 2 mm. The average tangential distance from the mesial to the distal canals beneath the pulpal floor was about 4.5 mm.

### 1.3.2

### Additional Root Canals

In a systematic review [37] as well as reported earlier by Vertucci [7], the type IV configuration is the most frequently encountered canal configuration in the mesial root of first mandibular molars followed by type II. Three canals were reported in about 61%, four canals in 36% and more than five canals only in approximately 1%
[33]. However, newer data were generated by micro-CT in more complicated canal anatomy in mesial roots of mandibular first molars [29]. In about 9% of the studied teeth, even four canals were observed in some root sections of the mesial root.

In the distal root of mandibular first molars, type I configuration dominated (3%) followed by types II (15%) and IV (12%) [37]; again, micro-CT studies showed a more complex image [31] (Fig. 1.12).

### 1.3.2.1	 Middle Mesial Root Canals

A so-called ‘middle mesial’ root canal in mesial roots of mandibular molars has been described as an additional canal between the mesio-buccal and the mesio-­ lingual canal, which occurs in the development root [11]. Middle mesial canals were found with a frequency of 1–15% [1]. With the enhancement of clinically available detection methods (i.e. the use of the dental microscope) [38] and troughing the dentin between the two main mesial root canals up to 2 mm [39] in a recent clinical study, a prevalence of up to 22% was found (Fig. 1.13).

However, the age of the patients at the time of extraction of that in vitro study
[39] is not known; thus, the results may not be representative of the adult population at various stages of life. An age-related incidence of negotiable middle mesial root canals was indeed described by Nosrat et al. [40]. They found about 33% middle mesial canals in patients ≤20 years old, 24% in patients 21–40 years old and 4% in patients >40  years old. ### 1.3.2.3	 Accessory, Lateral Canals and Apical Ramifications

Accessory and lateral canals connect the pulp space to the periodontium. An accessory canal represents a junction of the main pulp canals or the pulp chamber, which communicates with the root surface. A lateral canal is an accessory canal located in the coronal or middle third of the root, usually branching off from the main canal (Fig. 1.15) [45]. In total, 73.5% of accessory canals were found in the apical third, 11.4% in the middle third and 6.3% in the coronal third of the root [7]. Accessory canals were also found in intraradicular regions at bior trifurcations of teeth with more than one root and were then called furcation canals [46].

### 1.3.2.4	 C-Shaped Canal Systems

A so-called C-shaped canal system is described as an anatomical variation [26].
Mostly the distal root canal is connected to one of the mesial root canals. A high prevalence of roots with C-shaped canals in mandibular second molars was found in an Asian population [45], with a range from about 14% to 50%. Cooke and Cox
[48] were the first to report on C-shaped canal configurations [27]. Although most
C-shaped canals occur in the second mandibular molars, C-shaped canals were also occasionally observed in first molars. Instead of various individual openings, pulp chambers of C-shaped molars showed a curved opening of a 180° circle or more into the canal system; instead of the full arc, semi-colon type shapes are also seen.

Coronoapically, C-shaped molars showed extensive anatomical variations, which can be divided into two groups:

•	 A single curved C-shaped canal from the canal entrance to the apex
•	 Three or more distinct canals below the usual C-shaped opening

C-shaped molars with a single, wide canal are the exception. According to
Vertucci [45], most commonly presented is type 2 of the C-shaped canals with individual orifices but C-shaped connections.

A more detailed description of C-shaped roots in mandibular second molars has been published using micro-CT on extracted teeth from China [26, 27, 49, 50]. The cross-sectional canal shape was analysed using 0.5 mm slices towards the apex and divided on each level into the following five categories: (C1) a continuous C-shaped contour without separation or division; (C2) a semicolon-shaped canal as a result of an interruption of the C-shape; (C3) two or three separate canals, which are connected by an isthmus; (C4), a single round or oval canal; and (C5) no canal lumen could be observed [25] (Fig. 1.16). ### 1.4

### Conclusions

Molar anatomy is complex, with defined basic morphologies and multiple variations. Third molars are not typically root canal-treated; their anatomy may add another layer of complexity to the details provided earlier. Therefore, in planning molar root canal treatment clinicians should

•	 Have an understanding of possible anatomic variations of the tooth in question
•	 Obtain all relevant clinical impressions and images, including periapical radio-

graphs, bitewings and cone beam CT scans
•	 Use magnification during the procedure
•	 Be ready to expect the unexpected in molar anatomy, both for the number of

canals and their individual configuration
