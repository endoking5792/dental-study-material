### 8.1

### Introduction

Root canal treatment should not be initiated and is not to be considered finished until a coronal seal is put in place, with proper restoration of tooth functional integrity. It is evident from the literature that any delay between endodontic treatment and tooth restoration should be as brief as possible, since significantly reduced chance of survival after endodontic treatment was reported for teeth restored with temporary restorations, compared to those receiving rapidly a permanent restoration after completion of the root canal treatment [1, 2]. However, what is less clear is the way endodontically treated teeth in general—and molars in particular—should be restored. In the recent S3-level guidelines by the European Society of Endodontology (ESE), it is mentioned that “for the management of restorable teeth with apical periodontitis, we recommend root canal treatment, appropriate restoration and

Best clinical practice should be guided by the highest level of evidence, with greater emphasis on clinical data—ideally randomized clinical data—rather than in vitro studies. Nevertheless, the opposite is often reported when it comes to tooth restoration, as reflected in the conclusions of several papers addressing the restoration of endodontically treated teeth. From the few clinical studies available, it is difficult to extract clear clinical guidelines. For example, it is stated in a practice-based study that the influence of tooth type on the survival of endodontically treated teeth is not well established in the literature [8]. The authors found that restorations placed on premolars and anterior teeth were more successful compared with restorations in molars, but this is not universally accepted [9, 10].

This lack of consensus suggests that the failure to consider certain factors hinders the development of clear, evidence-based guidelines for the restoration of endodontically treated teeth. To give a specific example, in a meta-analysis (high level of evidence) evaluating the prognostic factors for tooth survival after endodontic treatment, the placement of crowns or cast restorations was reported to improve tooth survival compared to direct restorations [11]. Nevertheless, some important parameters were not considered in the analysis in that study, for example, tooth type, the amount and distribution of residual tooth structure, or the type of final restoration.

Confounding variables further compromise these results. For example, it has been argued that teeth with preoperative periapical lesions are less likely to receive a crown compared to those without lesions and anterior teeth are more likely to be restored with a filling and sooner than posterior teeth [12]. This indicates the existence of treatment selection bias, which will be further developed in this chapter. It also highlights that decision-making for restorations of endodontically treated teeth should be specific to the amount and distribution of remaining tissue. Molars indeed represent the majority of teeth treated in specialized endodontic practice, accounting for nearly half of all treated teeth, as identified in a 25-year prospective cohort study including 2500 treatments (Fig. 8.1) [13]. This may be explained by the fact that they are the most susceptible to caries development [14, 15] and to cracks [16].

### 8.2

### Longevity of Root Canal-Treated Molars

There is a growing and justified tendency to focus more on patient-centered outcomes. In this context, it has been reported that what matters most to patients undergoing root canal treatment is the preservation of the treated teeth in function—second only to effective communication and trust in the dentist—and ahead of other important aspects such as treatment cost or pain level [17]. Hence, the ultimate goal of the root canal treatment is to maximize the longevity of root canal-treated teeth. This is in line with the recent ESE S3-level guidelines, which describe tooth preservation as the “most critical” clinical outcome [3, 18].

In a recent literature review on the survival of root canal-treated teeth, an estimated annual loss rate of 2% was reported [7], which, as previously stated, supports the view that conservatively treated teeth have a longevity relatively comparable to that of implants [19]. However, a closer look reveals that molars are consistently reported to be at higher risk of tooth loss, as summarized in a meta-analysis on the subject [11], and confirmed by several more recent studies [8, 13, 20–22], as illustrated in Fig. 8.2.

### 8.4

### Fluid-Tight Seal of the Root Canal System

Before 30 years, a pivotal study by Ray and Trope sparked significant debate in the field of endodontics when it demonstrated that the quality of the coronal restoration was more important for periapical health following root canal treatment than the quality of root canal obturation itself [27]. Although this conclusion has since been reconsidered, the study undeniably pointed out the critical role that coronal restoration plays in maintaining long-term periapical health. Preventing infection or reinfection of the root canal system is undoubtedly paramount to the success of endodontic treatment. The latter, which is defined based on a combination of clinical and radiological criteria, is categorized in the recent ESE S3-level guidelines as
“critical outcome” besides the “most critical” that is tooth survival [3, 18]. Preventing and treating root canal infection is clearly at the core of root canal treatment success
[28, 29] and therefore requires restorative strategies that allow for reaching this goal both during and after the root canal treatment.

Before initiating the root canal treatment itself, pre-endodontic restorations are often required. They serve not only to establish optimal conditions for disinfection of the root canal system but also to assess whether the tooth is restorable (Fig. 8.5) or if additional interventions, such as crown lengthening, are needed (Fig. 8.6). Molars endure the majority of the vertical masticatory forces, while most of the time, they are protected from lateral forces by anterior teeth through anterolateral guidance [39]. Occlusal loads sustained by molars during normal function range roughly between 100 and 300 N, while they do not exceed 50 N for premolars, and are lower yet for anteriors [40]. Higher values for maximum voluntary bite force have also been reported (500–800 N) [41] and can potentially reach even higher levels in cases of parafunction [39]. The variability is due to factors such as the type of food [42], age [41], or gender [43].

This underlines that restorative concepts should be specific to each tooth type, since each is submitted to very different challenges. Molars are mostly submitted to axial forces of high intensity. Since root canal treatment weakens teeth due to loss of structure, in addition to the loss of marginal ridges that often accompanies the loss of vitality, there is a particular need to adequately protect endodontically treated molars against tooth fracture. In this regard, it is to some extent surprising that most reviews and guidelines dealing with restoration after endodontic treatment propose general concepts of restoration for all teeth, without specifically and explicitly differentiating recommendations for each tooth type [44, 45]. We therefore suggest that results based on one tooth type should no longer be generalized to all endodontically treated teeth without any distinction, as is often the case in the literature.

Tooth weakening following endodontic treatment seems to have little [39] or nothing to do [46] with local changes of dentin characteristics following endodontic treatment (e.g., loss of moisture, use of chelators, irrigants, or medicaments). The major cause for increased susceptibility to fracture of endodontically treated teeth appears to be the loss of hard tissue [39]. Endodontically treated teeth indeed very often undergo additional dentin removal in the process of the restoration, for example, creating post space, performing mutilating preparation as in the case of full crowns, or reducing occlusal thin dentin walls, among other procedures. In this regard, it is quite sobering that a major cause of further tissue damage is iatrogenic, especially given efforts that are currently invested in developing strategies to regenerate dental tissues in order to strengthen tooth structure.

In light of this paradox, it is important to weigh the necessity or rationale of additional tissue sacrifice. The ultimate goal of dentistry is to retain a healthy complement of teeth for a lifetime; therefore, appropriate strategies should be “conservative” in order to control, rather than eliminate a condition, with existing tissues preserved as much as possible [47]. Indeed, it appears that habits, heritage, and/or traditions sometimes lead to a “conservative” dentistry in the sense of holding to traditional attitudes and values and being cautious about change or innovation. Adhesive dentistry started in 1955 with Buonocore, and bonding materials and strategies have since considerably evolved. Dental adhesives are now more and more reliable and predictable [48]. Specifically, regarding restoration of endodontically treated molars, composite restorations enable us to do more and more without mechanical, non-adhesive, retention features such as cavity taper, groves, or posts.
Retention elements necessary in the era of silver amalgam are no longer needed, and this allows for more tissue preservation.

Another major breakthrough that is affecting tooth restoration is the advent of digital technology, specifically the CAD/CAM technology. Clearly, digital technology is changing dentistry, and it is likely that the use of computers will replace most manual tasks with the hope of getting results faster and with fewer errors [49]. For endodontically treated teeth, with a likely further spread of CAD/CAM devices, clinicians may have the option of placing a permanent indirect restoration immediately after completion of endodontic treatment. As mentioned above, this approach is highly desirable in terms of endodontic success, as it limits or avoids temporary restoration failure.

Finally, another interesting evolution is the advent of “bio”-materials. Far more than just a buzzword, the addition of these three letters in front of the word “material” has a clear meaning, in that materials should not only be considered for their intrinsic qualities, but in the context of their integration in a living organism.
Biomaterials are expected to behave and perform in a similar fashion as the tissue they replace. Therefore, their characteristics should be as close as possible to the tissues replaced. This should be kept in mind by clinicians when choosing the best strategy and most adapted material for tooth restoration. The current trend is to use materials whose mechanical properties are as close as possible to the dental tissues they replace [50], which is sometimes implied by the expression “biomimetics.”

For example, an important property to consider is the elastic modulus, which is the tendency of a material to be reversibly deformed when a force is applied to it. A significant difference in elastic modulus between the replacement material and the tissue replaced might result in inappropriate interfacial stress generation. The elastic modulus was determined to be about 19 GPa for dentin and 80–94 GPa for enamel
[51]. In comparison, it is much higher in metals and other dental materials, for example, about 110 GPa for titanium, 200 GPa for stainless steel, 200 GPa for zirconium, and 300 GPa for aluminum oxide. This major difference in modulus suggests that metal posts in roots should be used with caution. In contrast, the elastic modulus of fiber posts ranges between 10 and 25 GPa, which is much closer to that of dentin [52].

The elastic moduli of currently available materials for coronal restoration are as follows: 10–15 GPa for hybrid resin-based composites [53], 10–17 GPa for glass ionomer [54], about 20 GPa for amalgam [55], 71 GPa for feldspathic porcelain, and 224 GPa for zirconia [56]. Interestingly, it has been shown that an increase in elastic modulus of the restoration from 5 to 20 GPa actually results in a 30% reduction of stress at the tooth–restoration interface under occlusal load [57]. To address this, there is a need for effective methods to quantify and evaluate the quality of the remaining coronal tissue. Some forward-looking suggestions have been proposed a few years ago, while some of these may have seemed a bit ambitious at the time. For instance, it has been suggested that, ideally, a rapid finite element analysis (FEA) should be conducted for each clinical case. This could be integrated into computer-aided design (CAD) software, providing practitioners with insights into structural weaknesses within the cavity and offering guidance on the most appropriate restorative materials and techniques [62]. Additionally, a diagnostic tool to determine the degree of fatigue in the remaining dental hard tissues is lacking, yet such a tool could be crucial for predicting the longevity of restored teeth. The absence of this information represents a major limitation in most in vivo and in vitro studies, which can often be reduced to speculative assessments rather than biomechanical comparisons. ### 8.6.1

### The Ferrule Effect

The dental ferrule refers to a circumferential band of dentin of at least 1–2 mm coronal to the margin of the preparation for a full crown (Fig. 8.8). ### 8.6.2

### Post Placement in Molars

A significant proportion of dentists (57%) appear to expect that post placement reinforces root canal-treated teeth and reduces the risk of fracture, according to a survey conducted in Germany among general practitioners with over 1600 responses

However, based on the available literature, the question of whether post placement is truly indicated in the restoration of endodontically treated teeth remains difficult to answer with a definitive yes or no. There are numerous factors involved, such as the post material or diameter, the quality of the bonding interface between post and dentin, the core material, the crown material, the amount of residual tooth structure, and others. This makes studies difficult to compare and probably accounts to some extent for the contradicting results found in the literature. Importantly, tooth type is a major factor to consider, and most of the studies evaluating post-placement were done on single-rooted teeth. The results obtained for incisors or pre-molars are not directly transferable to molars, and each tooth type has specific characteristics, notably the major differences in occlusal load intensity and directions [76], as well as in root cross-sectional anatomy. ### 8.6.2.1	 Bonding in the Root Canal: The Worst-Case Scenario

In cases where a post is deemed necessary (which, as will be discussed below, is not clearly justified for molars), several factors complicate the bonding procedure.

First, it is not trivial to clean the root canal dentin and prepare it for bonding.
Following endodontic treatment, obturation, and post space preparation, large areas of the root canal walls were indeed shown to be covered with smear layer, debris, and materials (sealer or gutta-percha) [79], which interfere with the post bonding efficiency. Since NaOCl was also demonstrated in some studies to decrease the bond strength [80], there may be an interest in neutralizing this effect by the use of anti-oxidant such as sodium ascorbate [81, 82]. In Lai’s work, control of abundant rinse with water prior to bonding is lacking, while in Vongphan’s paper, there is no significant difference between control and when abundant water rinse is performed after NaOCl. Hence, it is still unclear whether or not this additional step is really beneficial. In any case, as mentioned earlier, optimal bond strength can also be restored through more conventional methods such as dentin cleaning using ultrasonic instruments [35].

Second, the ease of bonding procedure (etching, rinsing, primer application, drying, bonding application), resin-material injection, and photopolymerization is not as straightforward in the root canal space as in the coronal portion, which can result in lower bonding quality, more voids, or sub-optimal resin polymerization [83].

Third, another major limitation of the bonding procedure in the root canal is related to the well-known polymerization shrinkage of resin-based composite, and the resulting stresses generated on the surrounding tooth structure. The estimated Kaplan–Meier survival rate at 10 years was 99%, while the estimated success rate declined to 55%, highlighting the distinction between functional survival and strict success criteria.

Beyond the promising survival rates, this restoration strategy presents other advantages; importantly, it does not require significant tissue removal for the placement of the restoration. It must be kept in mind that, in molar endodontics, each restorative procedure significantly increased the amount of lost hard tissue volume [112]. Endocrowns are clearly a more conservative approach, which reduces the risk of procedural accidents such as root perforations or loss of the apical canal seal associated with post placement [78, 90]. Finally, deep cervical placement of restoration margins is not needed, which enables maximum conservation of enamel, and is crucial to obtain a predictable, stable, and efficient bond [113].

Expanding the focus beyond endocrowns to include other extensive adhesive restorations—such as onlays and overlays—current clinical evidence supports these conservative designs as other reliable alternatives to traditional full-crown approaches. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis on the survival of indirect adhesive restorations in endodontically treated posterior teeth reported overall favorable outcomes [114]. The failure rate was approximately 4% within
2–4 years after placement, increasing to 11% at 7 years and up to 21% beyond
12 years. However, the analysis did not distinguish between molars and premolars, limiting tooth-specific conclusions. Among the included studies, one of the most iconic and pioneering is the prospective study by van Dijken and Hasselrot
[115], with a mean observation period of 13 years. The study evaluated over 250 extensive, purely adhesive posterior ceramic restorations—partial and full coverage—placed under routine clinical conditions, without mechanical retention or post-and-core treatment. Approximately 75% of the restorations were in molars, and the sample included a certain proportion of endodontically treated teeth. The cumulative success rate for molars was 75%, while extrapolated data suggest that fewer than 3% of failures resulted in extraction, yielding an estimated survival rate of up to 97%.

Beyond the high survival rates reported for endocrowns, onlays, and overlays, another noteworthy aspect of these outcome studies is the nature of the failures observed [114]. Depending on the study, reported complications include ceramic fractures, recurrent caries, and restoration debonding [107, 109–111, 115].
Importantly, these types of failures are typically repairable, provided that appropriate clinical protocols are followed, which will be detailed in the next section.
Catastrophic failures—such as vertical root fractures—are rarely reported, supporting the view that extensive bonded restorations may help reduce the risk of tooth loss [114].

### 8.6.4

### Study Biases: The Place of Full Crowns and the Importance

### of Cusp Coverage

Until recently—and still in many countries—it has been widely assumed that placing a full crown is systematically required after endodontic treatment, based on the belief that it improves tooth survival. For example, a large U.S. survey conducted within the National Dental Practice-Based Research Network examined crown decision-making among almost 2000 dentists (83% response rate) [116]. Notably,
94% of respondents recommended crowns for posterior teeth that had undergone root canal treatment, confirming the strong preference for full-coverage restorations in these cases. It is worth considering the possible reasons for that, despite the emergence over the last decades of new strategies with specific advantages, as described above. This is potentially rooted in a combination of long-standing clinical and teaching tradition, insurance data, and reimbursement models that often favor full crowns, and most of all historic literature linking full crowns with improved survival. For example, in a systematic literature review from 2007, an approximately
20% reduction in tooth survival was reported for root canal-treated teeth restored with a direct restoration compared with a crown at 10 years [117]. Likewise, in the systematic review by Ng et al. on tooth survival of root canal-treated teeth, crown restoration is found to significantly improve tooth survival [11]. However, most included studies are either prospective without randomization or retrospective. For example, in a large epidemiological study including almost 800,000 molars [118], it was reported that among the 3% of extracted teeth at 8 years, the number of teeth without crown (large amalgam or composite restoration, or no restoration at all) was more than sixfold higher compared to teeth with crowns. However, such a comparison was not provided among the 97% of retained teeth. The lack of randomization between full crowns and direct restorations in the available studies represents a major bias [12], since the amount of remaining tissue and/or the tooth prognosis play a major and subjective part in the decision-making. For example, teeth presenting a periapical lesion before the restoration were significantly less likely to receive a crown (64%) than teeth without a lesion (76%) [12]. ### 8.6.5

### Restoration of Endodontically Treated Molars as Part

### of Multi-unit Restorations

While most of the considerations discussed so far have focused on the restoration of single-unit, isolated teeth—which represent the majority of clinical cases—it is also important to address how to restore a tooth that will serve as part of a multi-unit restoration, such as a bridge abutment or other prosthetic support, following root canal treatment. In fact, based on the available literature, the more appropriate question is whether an endodontically treated molar should be included in a multi-unit restoration at all. The available data shows that the use of such teeth in plural restorations is subject to caution, with reduced tooth survival being reported [8, 11]. This may be due to various causes, including higher occlusal stresses on the abutment teeth, different distribution of these stresses, with potentially an increased shear force component, or else the removal of a large amount of sound tissue to accommodate for the insertion axis. In light of these aspects, and given the good quality of modern implant strategies, it seems more reasonable to avoid the use of a root canal-­ treated molar as part of a plural restoration whenever possible. In spite of those considerations, if the use of a root-treated molar needs to be used as an abutment, the recommendations made above may need to be reconsidered. Notably, the creation of a ferrule may be particularly justified, and a fiber post may also be considered in that case [6].

### 8.7.2

### Direct or Indirect Restoration: Selection of Material

### and Clinical Procedures

A recent systematic review specifically focused on root canal-treated molars was unable to identify any difference in tooth survival or restoration quality between direct and indirect restorative strategies at short term (2.5–3 years) [126]. Another one compared the clinical longevity of direct and indirect posterior resin composite restorations, but was not restricted to root canal-treated teeth [127]. It showed similar clinical longevity at short (1–3  years), medium (4–7  years), or long term (8–11 years), with low quality of evidence highlighting the need for more long-term high-quality studies to confirm their findings. Finally, an older systematic review underlined that indirect restorations, mainly full crowns, show higher 5- and 10-year survival rates compared to direct restorations in endodontically treated teeth, especially when extensive coronal damage is present [128]. However, like in the other two studies, short-term success rates do not differ significantly, and the overall quality of evidence remains low.

From a practical standpoint, in the absence of more robust evidence, two key factors guide clinicians’ decision-making: the reconstruction of tooth anatomy and the biomimetic properties of the restorative material. Regarding the former, it is evident that the larger the restoration, the more challenging it becomes to recreate through multiple composite increments accurate proximal contact points, occlusal anatomy, and proper occlusal contact with opposing teeth (Fig. 8.16). As a result, it is common clinical practice to favor indirect techniques for larger restorations— particularly when one or more cusps are involved—due to their greater predictability and precision (Fig. 8.16), except if the patient cannot afford the cost of an indirect restoration, in which case the direct composite may become a permanent option.
This clinical reasoning is further supported by evidence from longitudinal studies, which show that the longevity of direct restorations decreases as the number of restored surfaces increases [129, 130].

Biomimetic properties of a restorative material relate to how well the material mimics the properties of the replaced dental tissue, mainly esthetically and mechanically; two material classes are applicable here: resin-based composites and ceramics. Between and within these two material classes, a wide range of biomechanical characteristics can be described. It is not the purpose of the present chapter to review every material available, but to provide the reader with the general important principles.

According to the objectives presented above for the restoration of root canal-­ treated molars, the material chosen should both restore function and prevent any further damage to the tooth. In this regard, one important aspect is then to avoid the generation of stresses within the remaining tooth structure. Finite element analyses have shown that increasing the elastic modulus of the restoration material from 5 to
20 GPa leads to a significant reduction in stresses in the neighboring dental tissues
[57], whereas a further increase above 20 GPa results in a stress increase [131].

### 8.7.3

### Restoration Repair: A Rational Approach

The points discussed above regarding an ideal restoration procedure concern all cases where the placement of a new restoration is considered. However, in many cases, the root canal system must be (re-) accessed, in the presence of a satisfactory permanent restoration, for endodontic reasons (see Fig. 8.17). In such cases, the practitioner must decide whether the existing restoration will be repaired or replaced by a new restoration. Failing restorations or restorations presenting small defects are replaced by most clinicians [140], though this leads to an additional removal of sound tooth structure and, in general, to a higher cost for the patient. Moreover, there is now cumulative evidence demonstrating that repair of restorations is not only a viable alternative to replacement, but one that leads to increased restoration longevity [140].

Some studies have identified that the mean survival period of existing crowns that had access cavity filled with a resin composite after root canal treatment was not different from new crowns, placed after root canal treatment [141]. However, other works have shown the opposite [8], while at the same time acknowledging that despite this reduction of restoration longevity compared to a new restoration, a repair can be considered useful for some patients in some conditions. Postponing the placement of a new restoration does present some advantages, notably a reduction in additional tissue loss, as well as lower treatment costs and duration in the short term.

Other factors may also preclude future surgical interventions, including relative or absolute medical contraindications, such as a radiotherapy, bisphosphonate therapy, or other systemic conditions. This means selecting materials and protocols that facilitate orthograde retreatment, while still meeting the restorative goals outlined in this chapter. For instance, the decision to place a post must be weighed carefully—not only because posts do not improve tooth survival but also because their removal can complicate retreatment and even lead to extraction. As an example, a study on single-rooted teeth comparing surgical and non-­ surgical retreatment showed that vertical root fractures were the leading cause of extraction in the non-surgical group, primarily when post removal was required
[24]. This underlines that facilitating future re-intervention should be considered an integral part of long-term restorative planning, especially in the context of preserving tooth longevity. This consideration is particularly important in molars, where many roots—such as palatal roots, second or third molars, or roots near critical structures like the inferior alveolar nerve—are not suitable candidates for microsurgical retreatment, at least in most hands. If retreatment feasibility is not factored into the initial restorative plan, the only remaining option in case of failure may be extraction. Even though no restorative strategy can completely eliminate the need for re-intervention, some strategies can severely hinder it, thereby increasing the risk of additional irreversible damage.
