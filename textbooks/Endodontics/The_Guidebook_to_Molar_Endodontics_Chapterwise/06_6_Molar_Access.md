Dentin preservation is a key aspect of modern dentistry. Endodontics has paid respect to the concepts of conservative treatment by modifying access cavity design over the years. However, form must follow function, and all factors that caused pulpal and periapical disease must be proficiently addressed during endodontic therapy, so that a favourable long-term prognosis for the endodontically treated tooth can be achieved.

### 6.1

### Introduction

Endodontic access cavity preparation is a key step during root canal therapy, especially in molar teeth. The posterior area typically presents with a limited mouth opening and complicated root canal anatomy. Thus, access to the root canal system is central to avoiding clinical complications and providing long-term success.
Historically, ideal access cavity preparations were recommended for individual tooth types. Clinically, however, ideal situations are rarely encountered. The difficulty level of endodontic access and root canal treatment will depend on various factors, including tooth-specific and patient-specific variables.

•	 Disorders of the temporomandibular joint, the anatomy of the dental arches and

Moreover, with a history of previous root canal treatment, iatrogenic alterations of the root canal system, such as perforations, transportations or separated instruments, may pose challenges. Overall, the ability to clean and/or disinfect the root canal system to either prevent or eliminate apical periodontitis will depend, to a large extent, on the clinician’s ability to gain access to the root canal system.

The present chapter will describe the assessment, preparation and execution of molar access cavities to identify, avoid and overcome potential complications that may arise during treatment and guide to practical access to the root canal system. As discussed in Chaps. 2 and 3, sufficiently diagnostic radiographs using a parallel technique are a prerequisite to arriving at a correct endodontic diagnosis [1].
However, for endodontic treatment planning involving molars, one radiograph may not be enough for proper treatment. Often, it is necessary to take several angled radiographs in mesial/distal angulations to fully appreciate root canal morphology, the extent of decay and existing restorations, the dimensions of the pulp chamber proper and the pulp horns, calcifications in the chamber and root canals or anomalies such as internal or external resorptions [2–5].

Additional bitewing radiographs may be obtained to evaluate the remaining tooth structure and the dimensions and orientation of the pulp chamber. Before accessing a molar, the practitioner should carefully interpret the preoperative radiographs to determine the exact location and size of the access opening, and compare the radiographic image to the clinical situation. Particular care must be taken to appreciate any possible shifting or rotation of the tooth axis, particularly in combination with crown restorations mimicking the original orientation of the former natural crown. For non-surgical retreatment situations, a limited volume cone beam
CT may be indicated if a reason for the failure of the initial treatment cannot be identified by means of periapical radiographs [6].

### 6.2

### General Considerations Prior to Endodontic Access

### in Molars

After careful interpretation of both clinical and radiographic examination and before accessing the tooth, the clinician must have formed a clear mental picture of the steps necessary for proper access, the potential complications and precautions to avoid any iatrogenic errors. This includes the assessment of the size and position of any existing restoration and/or decay, angulation and rotation of the tooth, the orientation of the pulp chamber in relation to crown and roots, calcifications and pulp stones, the position of the furcation and extensions of root trunks, distance from the occlusal surface of the tooth to the cemento-enamel junction, curvatures of roots and the position of canal orifices.
These deliberations will not only maximise the success of the actual access cavity preparation but will also help in making the overall treatment procedure easier (Box 6.1).
After proper anaesthesia (see Chap. 4) and dental dam isolation, the dental dam should be disinfected to prevent incidental contamination with oral bacteria on the dam or the clamp. Suitable disinfecting solutions include chlorhexidine 2%, iodine tincture or sodium hypochlorite. Great caution has to be taken that the dental dam isolation does not leak in either direction to prevent contamination from saliva but also to prevent sodium hypochlorite accidents if the solution penetrates through the dental dam isolation.

Block-out resins or gels may be indicated if proper isolation cannot be achieved with a dental dam and clamp alone. In general, the actual opening of the pulp chamber should only be made after the decay has been removed to avoid initial or further contamination of the root canal system, in particular for teeth with irreversible pulpitis, where the pulpal disease is primarily of inflammatory origin, and the deeper layers of the pulp are not yet infected. Teeth with extensive decay mesially or distally may require the extension of the dental dam to include one or both of the adjacent teeth to allow proper caries or restoration removal without compromising the seal of the dental dam. In general, with certain exceptions for crowns and bridges, as discussed later in this chapter, an existing restoration should be removed together with any decay. This will not only allow the identification of problems such as craze lines or fractures but also provide easier access to the pulp chamber and the root canals [7].

An ideal molar access cavity (Box 6.1) provides good visualisation and facilitates identification of all canal orifices to achieve thorough cleaning and shaping of all coronal pulp spaces and the root canal system, while not excessively weakening the tooth structure. As a general guideline, one should remove as little as possible, but only as much as necessary tooth structure.

Depending on the extent of the decay and the ability to sufficiently isolate a tooth with little remaining tooth structure, it may be necessary to build up the tooth prior to endodontic access [8]. Alternatively, if adequate isolation is feasible without a preoperative build-up, the tooth may receive a complete build-up after the endodontic procedure. However, this is under the condition that a stable, well-sealing temporary restoration can be placed between sessions if a multi-visit procedure is indicated. Upon access, the practitioner should mentally visualise the outline, shape and extent of the pulp chamber. As a rule of thumb, the horizontal outline of the pulp chamber will follow the cross-section of the tooth at the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) [9]. The clinician should align the bur with the long axis of the tooth and aim for the centre of the pulp chamber. A recommendation for access burs depends on the type of coronal restoration present and the extent of the pulp chamber. Carbide burs are indicated for metal restoration. The initial access through a crown (see indications below) should be carried out using a diamond bur for the ceramic portion of the crown to avoid chipping and cracking of the restoration. A carbide bur will then go through the metal portion of the crown.

The actual access to the pulp chamber may be carried out by using a straight conical diamond or small cylindrical carbide bur, depending on the size and configuration of the pulp chamber. These diamond burs may be coated at the tip or non-cutting, which may provide some additional safety from destroying pulpal floor anatomy. A straight bur will allow for easier orientation than a round bur and create fewer undercuts. In an adolescent or adult patient, if no tertiary dentin or pulp chamber calcifications are present, the clinician may feel a sudden drop after reaching the pulp chamber.

Pulp chambers and root canals are generally larger in younger patients [10]. In older patients or where a long-standing low-grade inflammation has been present, the drop into the pulp chamber may not be evident due to mineralisation or deposits of tertiary dentin [11–13]. The pulp chamber may then be significantly smaller and shallower. The clinician should take extra precautions if the radiographic interpretation indicates the presence of hard tissue structures within the pulp chamber to avoid iatrogenic damage to the pulpal floor. In elderly patients, the pulp may have gone through dystrophic changes, and, similar to pulp necrosis, no bleeding may indicate entering the pulp chamber [14]. After the initial opening, one should carefully extend the access cavity by removing the roof of the pulp chamber, allowing for close inspection of the pulp floor and search for the root canal orifices [15]; see also Box 6.2.

Several anatomical landmarks exist that aid in orifice localisation. Developmental lines on the pulp floor connect all canal orifices. Also, colour differences can be observed between bright, opaque dentinal walls and the darker, transparent pulpal floor. While some canal entrances are located easily, these anatomical landmarks are significant in finding calcified canal orifices. One common example is MB2. While removing dentin during the search for MB2, the isthmus itself between MB1 and MB2 appears as one developmental line that terminates at the MB2 orifice.

Established guidelines for access ask for a complete removal of the entire roof of the pulp chamber to allow for straight-line access to all root canals, including the removal of all pulp horns and coronal enlargement (Box 6.3) [16, 17]. The traditional access cavity used to be carried out with stainless steel hand instrumentation or earlier, stiffer nickel–titanium rotary instrument called for excessive unroofing of the pulp chamber to allow for an extensive straight-line access to the root canal system to minimise the risk of iatrogenic errors such as instrument fracture, transportation, zipping or ledging. Modern nickel–titanium rotary instruments are more flexible and allowed for the reduction in the access cavity size, resulting in a modern conservative access cavity design with reduced dentin removal which, however, still allows for the inspection of the entire pulp floor and the thorough removal of any tissue remnants in the pulp chamber. Any healthy hard tissue structures should be protected and preserved;
however, this can be only to the extent of allowing adequate instrumentation and disinfection of the root canal system. An appropriate guideline for access cavity preparation is to remove as little dentin as possible but as much as necessary.

Minimally invasive access cavity preparations were introduced as a strategy for dentin preservation during root canal therapy by Clark and Khademi [18, 19]. The central idea of this concept is to keep the roof of the pulp chamber as intact as possible and minimise dentin removal in the pericervical area to reduce the risks of crown and root fractures with the long-term goal to improve the survival of the endodontically treated tooth. In contrast to the traditional access and conservative access cavity designs, this results in minimised access shapes, often summarised under the umbrella term of contracted cavity design [20]. two canals. However, there is significant variation in morphology [44–49]. The distobuccal or mesiobuccal roots may be fused with the palatal root individually or with each other [50]. Also, many variations exist for the number of roots, root canals and the root canal system, including isthmuses, fins and other communications between main canals [51]. In maxillary first molars, the access cavity should follow a rhomboidal outline and should be located towards the mesial half of the tooth (Fig. 6.1). Ideally, the access cavity should not violate the transversal ridge and should leave approximately 1.5–2.0 mm of the mesial ridge intact if these are not affected by decay. The palatal canal entrance is the largest and most often the easiest to locate upon access. Its location can be found approximately in the centre of the palatal half of the tooth, between one-third to one-half the distance from the mesial towards the distal end of the tooth. After locating the palatal orifice, it will be easier to find the mesiobuccal and distobuccal canal entrances. In many situations, developmental grooves on the pulpal floor may guide the clinician. After identifying the orifices and creating straight-line access, the clinician has to be aware that not only the buccal roots are curved, but also the palatal root most often has a buccal curvature that will not or hardly be visible on ortho-radial or excentric radiographs. The distobuccal canal usually has a more tear-drop shape orifice and is located slightly distal to the buccal groove.

The mesiobuccal canal entrances can vary greatly in the shape of the orifices due to the many variations of the mesiobuccal canal system. As a general guideline, the first mesiobuccal canal entrance (MB1) can be found slightly more buccally than the distobuccal canal entrance, just palatal to the mesiobuccal cusp tip. The second mesiobuccal canal entrance (MB2) is most often described to be slightly mesial of an imaginary line connecting MB1 with the palatal canal orifice, approximately 1.5–2.0 mm from the entrance of mb1. Many combinations, however, are possible, including two completely separate canals, one single canal, two orifices with two canals joining, two canals splitting from one orifice, splitting and reuniting or three separate canals, with the third canal being located next to mb1 or mb2 or more disto-palatal [52–57].

### 6.4

### Mandibular First Molar Access Cavity

Most mandibular first molars typically have two roots with either three or four root canals. Commonly, two root canals are found in the mesial root and one or two canals in the distal root. In order not to miss any canals in the distal root, the access cavity should not be triangular, but rather trapezoid to rectangular in shape, and in most cases be located in the mesial two-thirds of the occlusal surface (Fig. 6.4).
Mesiobuccal and mesiolingual canals are found slightly centrally in relation to the mesiobuccal and mesiolingual cusp tips. The distal canal(s) will be found distally to the furcation, respectively distal to the buccal developmental groove. Due to the curvature of the distal root, it is mostly sufficient to limit the access cavity extension in distal direction. Straight-line access will still be possible. If only one distal canal is present, it is located centrally in buccolingual direction. It commonly has a long oval canal cross-section stretching buccolingually.

Generally, the developmental grooves help identify the root canal orifices. Mesiobuccal and mesiolingual are often connected by a developmental groove, which may extend into a partial or complete isthmus between the canals. In approximately 5% of the clinical cases, a third mesial canal or mid-mesial canal, between the mesiobuccal and mesiolingual canals, has been reported. Isthmus structures between the mesial canals should be carefully instrumented to allow for the removal of vital or necrotic tissue remnants and to disinfect these ramifications.
The opening and cleaning of the isthmus will also enable the identification of any mid-mesial canals, which may only become accessible at a deeper level. Four canals are rarely present in the mesial root.

A three-rooted configuration of the mandibular first molar is found mainly in
East or Southeast Asian populations; a second distal (distolingual) root was reported in 5–15% of clinical cases (‘radix entomolaris’) [55–65]. In these situations, the main distal canal in the distobuccal root can be found closer to the mesiodistal midline, whereas the distolingual canal entrance would be distinctively closer to the distolingual cusp. For these situations, the access cavity must be extended in the direction of the distolingual canal to allow for almost vertical access to the canal.
This is necessitated by the sharp buccal curvatures that frequently exist in combination with a distinctive apical kink in these separate distolingual roots. This curvature is often invisible on an ortho-radial projection. A third distolingual root is often considerably shorter than the mesial or distobuccal roots.

If only a thin isthmus structure exists and the C-shape is complete, it will need to be carefully instrumented from coronal to apical. This can be done initially by using small long-shank round burs, then followed by LN-burs and ultrasonic instrumentation. It will require direct visibility of the area to be instrumented to prevent perforations or transportations.

### 6.5

### Third Molar Access Cavity

Both maxillary and mandibular third molars may present with significant variability in root and root canal system anatomy. No general guidelines can be given. Roots are often entirely or partially fused, ranging from one to four or even more. Root lengths can be very variable, even within the same tooth. Third molar root canal therapy may be limited by the access to the tooth, not only by tooth inclinations in mesiodistal or buccolingual directions, but also due to rotations, soft-tissue impactions or limited mouth opening by the patient. Canals may be difficult to negotiate due to size or abrupt curvatures. If a limited mouth opening is encountered, a children’s head handpiece and endodontic handpieces with reduced head size can provide better access and correct alignment of the bur. Pre-bendable rotary nickel–titanium files manufactured from controlled memory may aid easier access to these root canals.

### 6.6

### Access Through Existing Full Coverage Restorations

Certain clinical situations may prohibit the removal of an existing coronal restoration, which include the following:

•	 Irreversible pulpitis after a newly cemented crown or fixed partial denture
•	 A crown restoration that anchors a removable partial denture, such as survey

crowns or telescopic crowns
•	 Large restorations that would have to be completely replaced after the restoration

on the tooth in need of endodontic treatment is removed
•	 Teeth with questionable restorability after the coronal restoration is removed

Whereas for newly cemented restorations, the remaining decay should not be an issue, both practitioner and patient must be aware that any existing restoration may not allow a complete inspection of the underlying hard tissue structure. Prior to any access through an existing coronal restoration, it must be checked for sufficient margins by bitewing radiographs and clinical inspection. If any existing decay cannot be removed prior to or during the endodontic procedure, the restoration must be removed so as not to compromise the treatment. Access through an existing full coverage restoration has become easier by the availability of magnification and illumination provided by the dental microscope (Fig. 6.4). However, the clinician is still advised to exert great caution since existing topographical landmarks, such as cusps, grooves and ridges, are removed by full-coverage restorations. A close inspection of the preoperative radiographs and the roots at the crown margins will allow a better understanding of potential tooth rotations or inclinations to prevent iatrogenic complications such as perforations.

### 6.7

### Summary: Molar Access Checklist

•	 Establish proper diagnosis, including sufficient radiographs.
•	 Do not rush the procedure.
•	 Using all available information, form a clear mental picture of the steps neces-

sary for proper access, potential complications and precautions.
•	 Provide proper anaesthesia and dental dam isolation. Disinfect the working field

before endodontic access.
•	 Remove all decay.
•	 If possible, do not leave existing restorations.
•	 Avoid iatrogenic contamination.
•	 Verify the circumference of the CEJ. The outline of a typical pulp chamber bears

a close resemblance.
•	 Access the pulp chamber carefully.
•	 To avoid iatrogenic errors, refrain from deep drilling without verification of pulp

chamber location.
