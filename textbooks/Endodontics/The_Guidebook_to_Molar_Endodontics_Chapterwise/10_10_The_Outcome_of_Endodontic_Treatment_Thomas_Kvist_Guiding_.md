### 10.2

### Outcome and Prognosis

In both medical and dental practice, outcome and prognosis are essential concepts that inform treatment decisions and help predict a patient’s long-term health.
Although often used interchangeably, these terms refer to distinct aspects of care.
Outcome describes the actual results of treatment, assessed over time, whereas prognosis involves predicting the likely future course of a condition based on various factors. While outcomes provide valuable insights into the efficiency of treatment, prognosis offers a reasoned prediction of the patient’s future health, considering treatment specifics and other influencing factors.

Outcome extends beyond clinical results to include the patient’s overall experience, such as pain or discomfort during treatment, the effectiveness of pain management, and the general experience at the dental practice. An important consideration is whether the patient perceives the treatment as worthwhile, both financially and in terms of time and effort. These elements are integral to a comprehensive evaluation of treatment outcome.

The two concepts—outcome and prognosis—are closely interrelated. To make an accurate prognosis, a dentist must have a deep understanding of the possible outcomes of a given treatment. The ability to foresee how a condition will evolve after endodontic care relies heavily on the dentist’s knowledge of expected outcomes and the factors that may influence them. Knowledge of outcome and prognosis is, of course, crucial not only for the individual patient who is affected by disease and may require treatment but also for third parties such as insurance companies, policymakers responsible for health care—including dental care—and taxpayers.
For insurance companies, understanding these factors helps in assessing treatment costs and long-term effects, while policymakers and taxpayers are concerned with the economic and societal implications of treatments and health care interventions.

### 10.2.1.1	 Comfort and Freedom from Pain

One of the most immediate and tangible results of endodontic procedures is relief from the pain caused by inflammation. Most patients experience a significant reduction in pain or discomfort in the days following endodontic treatment. However, this short-term pain relief does not always reflect the complete picture of the treatment’s long-term impact. Pain relief must also be evaluated over time, as ongoing or recurrent discomfort can indicate treatment failure, such as persistent infection, reinfection, or complications.

### 10.2.1.2	 Retention and Function

Retention refers to the tooth’s ability to remain functional and intact in the mouth over time. This aspect of the outcome is not immediate and takes years to fully evaluate. The key to retention lies in the quality and efficiency of the endodontic procedures, such as the effectiveness of cleaning, shaping, and sealing the canal.
Long-term retention also depends on factors such as the integrity of the tooth structure, the presence of additional caries, and whether the tooth remains free from reinfection. Retention of the tooth over time can, from several perspectives—particularly that of the patient—be considered the most essential and overarching goal of all endodontic therapy, including both vital pulp therapy and root canal treatment.

### 10.2.1.3	 Periapical Status

The objective of vital pulp therapies is to preserve a healthy dental pulp in order to maintain an unremarkable periapical status, while periapical healing refers to the resolution of infection and inflammation in the tissues surrounding the root tip.
Historically, periapical healing has been considered one of the most important indicators of the efficiency of root canal treatment. However, the process of healing in the periapical area can take months or years to fully evaluate. Even after treatment, some periapical lesions may only partially resolve or remain stable. In some cases, signs of infection may persist or recur. The use of advanced imaging technologies, such as cone beam computed tomography (CBCT), has improved the ability to monitor periapical healing, but measuring complete healing remains a challenge. It is therefore essential to assess this aspect of the outcome over time, alongside other factors like pain relief and tooth retention.

Thus, the outcome of endodontic treatment is a gradual process, and the full outcomes—particularly regarding periapical status and retention—are not immediately clear. These aspects must be assessed in the long-term following treatment, which makes evaluating both outcomes and prognosis particularly challenging, both scientifically and for individual patients and their dentists.

### 10.2.2	 Prognosis of Endodontic Treatment

While outcome reflects the actual results of a treatment, prognosis refers to the dentist’s ability to predict the future course of the patient’s condition. In the case of root canal therapy, prognosis helps forecast the long-term impact of the treated tooth.
This is not simply a guess, but an informed prediction based on an understanding of several key elements, including the nature of the disease, the specifics of the treatment, and the individual characteristics of the patient.

The prognosis for a tooth undergoing root canal treatment largely depends on the initial condition of the tooth and surrounding tissues, as well as how effectively the treatment addresses the underlying infection or damage. It also considers the patient’s general health and their ability to heal, as well as the complexity of the root canal system. In general, a more favorable prognosis is associated with cases where the tooth is less damaged, the root canal system is straightforward, and the patient is in good health. On the other hand, a more complex case, involving extensive infection or structural damage, may be associated with a less predictable outcome.

Although outcome and prognosis are closely linked, it is important to understand that prognosis is inherently an estimate, shaped by a combination of clinical judgment and available knowledge. A dentist will use their expertise to assess the likelihood of long-term benefits based on the factors they observe, but unforeseen complications may arise. Ultimately, the prognosis helps to guide the treatment plan and expectations for the patient, providing an informed prediction of how the tooth is likely to fare in the future.

For the dentists to answer the clinical questions comprehensively and with confidence, they must draw on a broad base of knowledge. This includes understanding the nuances of treatment outcomes, the potential for disease recurrence, the variables that influence prognosis, and the long-term effects of both disease and treatment. Therefore, to accurately respond to these types of questions, a dentist must possess a deep understanding of the biological, clinical, and technical factors that influence both the outcome and prognosis of endodontic treatments.

### 10.3

### Outcome and Prognosis of Molar Endodontics

Root canal treatments for molars are particularly challenging due to the complex anatomy and functional demands of these teeth. Molar teeth often have multiple canals, with varying shapes and sizes, which makes cleaning, shaping, and sealing more difficult compared to other tooth types. Additionally, molars are subject to higher functional forces during chewing and can be more prone to fractures or reinfection if not treated meticulously.

As a result, the outcomes of root canal treatments in molars can be significantly different from those in other teeth, and often less predictable. While pain relief might still be a primary outcome for all treated teeth, the long-term retention and periapical healing in molars may be more challenging to achieve.

### 10.4

### The Multifaceted Nature of Knowledge

In order to practice dentistry professionally and to address diseases of the pulp and periradicular tissues—particularly when these occur in molars—comprehensive knowledge is required. This knowledge must come in different forms, and it is essential that we, as dentists, possess and apply these various types to make informed clinical decisions and deliver the appropriate treatment. There are three core forms of knowledge that are crucial in our field: episteme, techne, and phronesis. These three types of knowledge, as defined in ancient philosophy, particularly by Aristotle
[6], are foundational for effectively understanding and treating complex clinical situations, such as those encountered in general endodontics and in molar endodontics in particular.

### 10.4.1	 Episteme

Episteme refers to theoretical and scientific knowledge, the kind that helps us understand how the world is structured and how things function. In the context of endodontics, this includes knowledge of the biology of the pulp, the microorganisms that inhabit root canals, the procedures used in clinical practice, and the outcomes of various treatments. This type of knowledge is vital for understanding the underlying causes of pulp diseases and how to treat them effectively.

Scientific knowledge provides the support that guides our practice. It produces facts about biological systems and helps predict the outcomes of clinical interventions. It is essential in ensuring that we apply good standard of care to stay up to date with the scientific foundations and advancements.

### 10.4.2	 Techne

The second essential form of knowledge is techne, which refers to practical knowledge or craftsmanship. In endodontics, techne is about the ability to perform specific procedures, such as cleaning and shaping root canals or placing root fillings,

### 10.4.3	 Phronesis

The third form of knowledge is phronesis, or practical wisdom. This type of knowledge enables us to make sound judgments in specific situations, applying both experience and ethical deliberation to navigate complex circumstances. In dentistry, phronesis is crucial for addressing clinical uncertainties and conflicts of objectives, ensuring that decisions are well-informed and contextually appropriate.

A key aspect of phronesis is recognizing that the same disease or condition can manifest differently in different patients or in different circumstances. While scientific knowledge and technical expertise may suggest a particular approach, each patient’s unique situation—such as their overall health, preferences, or response to previous treatments—must be considered. The same condition in one patient may require a different treatment plan or a more conservative approach than in another, depending on various factors. Understanding and applying this flexibility in treatment is a critical component of phronesis.

To develop phronesis, one must engage in self-reflection and learn from real-life experiences. This wisdom develops over time through continuous practice, patient interactions, and the opportunity to learn from colleagues, mentors, and inspirational people. It is further strengthened by the process of articulating knowledge when teaching others, as well as by reflecting on both achievements and disappointments.

### 10.5

### Episteme of Endodontics

It is generally acknowledged by the profession, their patients, and society that practitioners have gathered lengthy clinical experience and that results from in vitro, animal, and clinical studies provide a basis for understanding how the pulp and the periapical tissues respond to therapeutic interventions. Experimental studies involving laboratory animals and in vitro research have provided us with the fundamental understanding of the biology of the dental pulp and periradicular tissues, in both health and disease. This bulk of knowledge has repeatedly been presented in scientific journals, reviews, and textbooks of endodontics and was summarized in a comprehensive Health Technology Assessment [1].

### 10.5.1	 Pulpitis

Numerous teeth with only small caries lesions in the dentin, sometimes even when lesions are confined to the enamel only, develop inflammatory changes in the pulp.

As long as the caries is still in the periphery, the pulp is regularly able to endure the challenge caused by bacteria in the dentine. The quicker the progression and the deeper the lesion gets, the more severe the inflammatory response in the pulp. When the caries lesion continues to progress, pulp vitality is jeopardized. Once the bacterial front extends into reparative dentin or the pulp tissue proper, it may finally reach a point of no return. Pulp necrosis will sooner or later follow. It is known that disease can be severe without causing patients’ symptoms, even if the pulpitis eventually leads to pulp necrosis. Additionally, dental procedures and various forms of accidental trauma can result in injuries that lead to pulp necrosis. In molars with two or more roots, it is not uncommon for one root to exhibit complete pulp necrosis, while another may still retain a vital pulp, albeit with severe inflammation.

### 10.5.2	 Apical Periodontitis

Collapse of the dental pulp by any cause results in loss of the defense mechanisms that can counteract microorganisms in the oral cavity from entering the root canal system. In cases of direct exposure such as caries or fracture, microorganisms rapidly invade the available pulpal space. In apparently intact teeth, microorganisms will ultimately find ways to access the root canal through fractures, cracks, or from accessory lateral and furcal canals in teeth with periodontal breakdown. In restored teeth, the pathway may be via dentinal tubules under restorations with marginal gaps. The invasion of microorganisms is a prerequisite for the development of apical periodontitis.

As the oral microbiota infiltrates the necrotic pulpal tissue, an inflammatory response—namely, apical periodontitis—develops in the tissues surrounding the root canal system, adjacent to the apical foramina. One of the main features of apical periodontitis is the appearance of an osteolytic area due to the increased activity of osteoclasts. In the early stages, the loss of mineral is not enough to be detected in traditional intra-oral radiographs. However, eventually a visible periapical radiolucency will develop. Inflammatory periapical lesions associated with an infected necrosis of the root canal system may prevail without clinical or subjective signs (pain, tenderness, fistulae, or swelling). However, symptomatic apical periodontitis may develop spontaneously. Severe pain might develop with or without soft tissue swelling. Spread of an infection may occasionally lead to life-threatening

### 10.6

### Management of the Diseased Vital Pulp

As long as the pulp remains vital, there is a possibility of reversing the progression of disease and preserving the pulp. The advantage of preserving the pulp tissue is most obvious in the case of a young permanent tooth with a large pulp chamber and undeveloped root, because removing the pulp arrests root development. The dentinal walls in the root will then be thin, increasing the risk of root fracture. A root-­ filled tooth in an adult also carries with it the risk of fracture. The prognosis in terms of tooth retention of root-filled teeth is not as good as vital teeth, especially in molars. Possible reasons include the loss of proprioceptive function, damping property, and tooth sensitivity, once a vital pulp succumbs to necrosis or is removed by pulpectomy.

The most basic and simple treatment of pulpitis consists of stopping the progression of caries. Beyond a certain point, excavation of a caries lesion is considered necessary, and a filling is placed to restore the dentin. The prognosis for the pulp following caries removal and filling placement is very good, as it is well established that many restored teeth remain asymptomatic and exhibit a responsive and thus vital pulp upon sensitivity testing. The residual dentine thickness is generally believed to be a key prognostic factor in these situations. Deep lesions are considered to be more at risk for pulpal collapse than shallow ones.

If the carious lesion with its bacterial front enters the primary dentin and progresses into the reparative dentin zone or even into the pulp tissue proper, the inflammatory response in the pulp becomes massive.

There are basically two therapy methods available to avoid stop the progression and hence prevent exposure of the pulp: indirect pulp capping and stepwise excavation. With both methods, a layer of carious dentine is left undisturbed. The key difference between the two methods is that, in the former, the tooth is left without further intervention, whereas stepwise excavation involves a follow-up session for re-entry, examination, and potentially additional excavation. Both stepwise excavation and partial excavation without re-entry help reduce the incidence of pulp exposure in both vital and carious primary as well as permanent teeth.

If, however, the pulp is exposed, there are four more or less distinct treatment options available, provided that the aim is still to preserve all or part of the pulp. The pulpal wound can be covered with a dressing (direct pulp capping). Another

### 10.6.1	 Pulpectomy

Complete removal of the pulp, pulpectomy, and placement of a root filling are the most radical treatment approach when a pulp has been exposed. This procedure is not substantially different from root canal treatment. However, since the pulp in the root canals remains vital, there is no established biofilm or infection, which generally increases the likelihood of maintaining healthy periradicular tissues. This contrasts with the situation where the goal is to restore healthy tissue following root canal treatment, once an infected root canal has already led to periapical inflammation. Therefore, maintaining strict aseptic conditions during pulp removal is a crucial factor in ensuring the best possible prognosis for the health of the periradicular tissues.

### 10.7

### Management of Apical Periodontitis

### 10.7.1	 Root Canal Treatment

The non-vital or necrotic pulp is defenseless against microbial invasion and will sooner or later be infected by indigenous microorganisms. No established methods exist to allow for debridement and antimicrobial combat and a subsequent reestablishment of a vital adult pulp in fully developed teeth. However, ongoing research within this area may change the treatment options in the future. For the time being, the only established treatment modality, in teeth with completed root formation is root canal treatment. The root canal is cleaned to eliminate microbes and their substrates. In addition to disinfecting irrigants, antimicrobial substances are often used as dressings to enhance the antibacterial effect. A root canal treatment is finished as the tooth receives a permanent root filling. Postoperative discomfort sometimes follows, but after a short period, most teeth become asymptomatic. Normally, the tooth is restored with a filling or crown immediately or a short while after completion.
When the conditions for root canal treatment are favorable and the procedure is performed to a high standard in accordance with well-established fundamental principles, the prognosis for tooth survival, absence of symptoms, and the establishment of healthy periapical tissues is generally good (see Box 10.2).

Most teeth with pulp necrosis and apical periodontitis present without pain or other symptoms. Nevertheless, symptoms may develop spontaneously or be initiated in conjunction with root canal treatment. In an acute situation, the clinician needs to deliberate on the seriousness and deploy adequate measures.
These can range from simple root canal instrumentation to incision of an abscess with or without prescription of antibiotics. The appropriateness of different measures depends on the risk of spread of infection and the patient’s general health. As with vital pulp therapy, it is common practice to assess the outcomes of root canal treatment. The clinician and patient typically review the results together at an appropriate stage. The desirable and best possible long-term outcome of root canal treatment is a retained and functional asymptomatic tooth with no clinical or radiographic signs of apical periodontitis. Over time, root-filled teeth are lost more frequently than non-root-filled teeth. This may be due to several factors, but not infrequently, the cause is related to cracks and fractures in the root-filled tooth. Ongoing caries, or sometimes toothache or other persistent inflammatory symptoms, can be other contributing factors.

In epidemiological cross-sectional studies, the frequency of periapical radiolucencies is high. Although cross-sectional studies cannot distinguish between conditions that will eventually heal and those that will persist, it is well established that persistent apical periodontitis in root-filled teeth is a common feature in adult populations.

The vast majority of root-filled teeth with apical periodontitis remain asymptomatic, and their potential negative impact on general health is likely to be minimal, except in very rare and isolated cases.

Persistent asymptomatic apical periodontitis of root-filled teeth may be cured by endodontic non-surgical or surgical retreatment. In clinical practice, a number of factors influence the choice of retreatment method, for example, the size of the bone destruction, the technical quality of previous treatment, accessibility to the root canal, future restorative requirements of the tooth, the cost of treatment, the preferences of the clinician and the patient, medical considerations, and the availability of various types of special equipment. ### 10.8

### Evidence-Based Endodontics

Considering what is commonly referred to as evidence-based medicine and dentistry, the demands for scientific support of clinical work have significantly increased in the last 25 years [7].

To answer specific clinical questions—such as which treatment method, equipment, or materials yield the best results—high-quality clinical studies are essential.
These studies provide the solid evidence required to guide decision-making in dental practice.

Clinical outcome studies can be conducted in various ways, each with its own advantages and limitations. For example, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are often regarded as providing high-quality evidence because they are designed to eliminate many of the external variables that could influence outcomes. These studies typically assess efficacy, that is, the performance of a treatment under controlled conditions, often in ideal settings. However, RCTs can be expensive, time-­ consuming, and may not always reflect real-world clinical practice. On the other hand, controlled clinical studies, while more adaptable to real-world settings, may not control for all variables as rigorously, and may not always measure efficacy in the same precise manner as RCTs.

Cohort studies, which track groups of patients over time, provide insights into effectiveness, in other words, how well a treatment works in a real-world context.
They offer valuable data on long-term outcomes, but they are more prone to confounding factors, as they do not randomize participants to treatment protocols.
Another important method for assessing effectiveness is register studies, which make use of large, real-world patient databases to track outcomes on a population level. These studies can provide a wealth of data on how treatments perform in everyday practice, but they may also be subject to biases or incomplete data.

In clinical outcome studies, a group of patients with a specific condition, such as pulpitis, or those receiving a particular treatment, such as root canal therapy, is identified and followed over time. Clinical outcomes are measured, and attention is

given to identifying prognostic factors—conditions that are associated with a particular outcome of the disease. When differences in outcomes are observed between two groups in a scientific study (e.g., comparing treatment protocol A with protocol
B), there are four possible explanations. The first is that a real difference exists, meaning that protocol A results in a better outcome than protocol B, or vice versa.
However, the observed difference may also be due to chance. Another possibility is that other unknown factors, known as confounders, may differentiate the groups and influence the outcome. Finally, the difference might stem from some form of bias in the study’s design or in the interpretation of its results.

Addressing these potential explanations is a challenging but essential task.
Studies must be carefully designed and interpreted to minimize bias and ensure the validity of their findings. Therefore, relevant studies must be scrutinized for quality.
This involves evaluating their relevance in terms of subject matter and their methodological rigor—study design, internal validity (the degree to which the study avoids systematic errors), statistical power, and generalizability.

In addition to assessing the scientific quality of clinical studies, it is crucial for both the reviewer and the reader to consider the relevance of the findings. In other words, is the research question particularly interesting and relevant to clinical practice? Furthermore, even if a difference in outcome is statistically significant—such as a comparison between two treatment methods—does this difference hold clinical significance? In many cases, a statistically significant result may not necessarily translate into a meaningful or relevant change in practice. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate whether the observed differences in outcomes are not only statistically reliable but also clinically important and applicable to real-world patient care.

Since the turn of the millennium, it has become increasingly common to summarize and analyze the scientific evidence underpinning clinical diagnostics and treatment options in endodontics through systematic reviews. These reviews have highlighted significant shortcomings in many of the published scientific articles on methods employed in the field [1, 8–17].

As is clearly evident, simply compiling a large number of articles of varying quality does not, on its own, resolve the issue. The studies upon which they are based remain what they are, and their quality does not improve merely by their quantity. More often than not, the conclusion is that there are insufficient studies of adequate quality, meaning that the evidence remains lacking—thus, the call for more and better clinical studies persists [7].

At the same time, the standards for what constitutes high-quality clinical studies have been carefully assessed and defined in guidelines for researchers [18]. These guidelines often involve numerous detailed requirements. While these increasing demands for rigorous documentation and ethical review are positive and ensure better research quality, they also make it more difficult to conduct research projects that can effectively address the key clinical questions essential to endodontics. The growing complexity and cost of high-quality clinical research make it challenging to carry out studies that directly answer the most pressing clinical issues in the field.

At least four key areas need further research to make our endodontic diagnostics and interventions more evidence-based.

First, it is well-established that an inflamed pulp can often be successfully treated with conservative procedures aimed at preserving the vital pulp. However, we are still unable to clearly identify which clinical conditions lead to the best outcomes or when pulpectomy is the more appropriate option [1, 8, 9, 10].

Second, follow-up studies show that teeth with necrotic, infected pulps can often be successfully treated with root canal therapy, leading to long-term pain-free results. However, strong scientific evidence is lacking on which treatment protocols are most effective in preventing persistent or recurring symptoms, ensuring a healthy periapical status, and minimizing tooth loss in root-filled teeth [1, 12–14].

Third, we lack clarity on the level of healing required to minimize new symptoms or prevent complications, such as local spread or systemic effects. Determining the time needed for apical periodontitis to heal remains challenging. Furthermore, our assessments still rely mainly on radiographic methods, whose reliability and validity are far from assured. This is despite the higher diagnostic accuracy of
CBCT compared to conventional radiographs [1, 11].

Finally, there is a lack of high-quality health economic studies comparing different endodontic treatments, and particularly clinical studies comparing root canal treatment or retreatment with therapeutic alternatives such as extraction and replacement with implants or prosthetics are rare [1, 19].

As seen above, the scientific evidence base for making statements about outcomes and prognosis in endodontics, including molar endodontics, reveals significant knowledge gaps. However, a relatively well-established understanding of what can be achieved exists and attempts to summarize the available episteme to guide clinicians are made in several position statements or so-called guidelines (see, e.g., references 20–22).

Recommendations are largely based on the interpretation of the available literature by the contributing experts. In the lack of solid evidence, the solution in order to establish recommendations often involves making systematic so-called consensus-­based statements. It is reasonable to assume that the experts’ knowledge, in addition to what can be considered epistemic knowledge, also draws from other sources.

For the clinician responsible for treating the individual patient, knowledge of the scientific literature alone is not sufficient to perform the delicate task of diagnosing, selecting appropriate therapy, making statements about prognosis, and evaluating outcomes. Therefore, to carry out our daily practice to the best of our ability, we need to complement our episteme with other forms of understanding.

In clinical practice, the outcome of treatment often depends on the clinician’s experience, skill, and ability to adapt the treatment to the unique circumstances and needs of each patient. The technical complexity of many endodontic procedures, especially in molar endodontics, means that the clinician’s expertise is crucial in determining the outcome. The ability to diagnose accurately, plan the treatment effectively, and perform procedures skillfully in real time is essential for achieving the most favorable outcomes. To perform one’s practice proficiently, the right equipment is essential, as is the capably to handle it correctly.

However, mastering techne requires more than simply knowing how to use these tools—it requires experience in applying them effectively in real-world clinical situations. Hands-on practice, observing skilled clinicians, and engaging in continuous learning are essential for developing this practical expertise.

In particular, in molar endodontics, the diagnosis and the treatment are often complex, and the influence on the results of the operator cannot be overvalued.

In many cases, factors such as the available tools, the time constraints of a busy practice, and the practitioner’s level of comfort with specific techniques play a significant role in shaping the outcome. Unlike in academic or specialist settings, where controlled environments and advanced equipment can greatly facilitate procedures, general practice settings require clinicians to work with more varied conditions and resources. A significant reason why the outcomes of various endodontic procedures tend to be poorer in general dentistry compared to specialist and university clinics is likely rooted in the disparity in technical expertise. Achieving the clinical proficiency required to treat molars demands not only talent and interest but also extensive training. This training must be conducted with the appropriate equipment and under proper supervision. Given the technical complexity of molar endodontics, it could be reasonably argued that the procedure is challenging enough to require specialized training for those who will be performing it.

### 10.10	 Phronesis of Endodontics

A clinician’s practical knowledge is not only comprised of technical skills but also a familiarity with and deep understanding of the procedures that have contributed to the development and growth of both epistemic and technological knowledge. For fully developed professionalism, there is also a kind of clinical wisdom required when interacting with patients and other involved parties. This knowledge is known as phronesis, and without it, no practice can be perfected. This approach ensures that clinical judgment is informed by the best available scientific evidence, while still allowing for the flexibility to account for individual patient circumstances. A clinician with phronesis can assess each case individually, considering not only the scientific evidence but also the unique nuances of the patient’s situation.

In this sense, phronesis is dynamic and requires continuous reflection and adaptation. It is not merely about following a set of rules but involves critical thinking and moral insight to navigate the complexities of human health and behavior. A dentist must be able to make decisions that balance clinical efficiency with ethical considerations, acting in the patient’s best interest while also addressing their individual circumstances.

### 10.10.1	 Uncertainties

In medicine as well as in dentistry outcome and prognosis, uncertainty in clinical settings can take many forms [23, 24]. One form is when the outcomes are known, and probabilities can be calculated, such as healing of apical periodontitis following proper root canal treatment. This type of uncertainty is what’s usually referred to as at risk. However, in some cases, the conditions are unusual, making it difficult to estimate these probabilities. This is known as fundamental uncertainty. By clinical research, we try to reduce fundamental uncertainty to a calculable risk. Additionally, research may reveal unexpected things. Such uncertainties are characterized as ignorance. While impossible to predict, we still need to be aware of unexpected outcomes.

### 10.10.2	 Post-Treatment Disease

There is limited scientific evidence regarding the outcome of endodontic post-­ treatment disease—a tooth that had had root canal treatment but exhibits signs of apical periodontitis [23]. Studies show that clinical and radiographic signs of persistent disease often heal after non-surgical and surgical retreatments [15]. Few studies explored the consequences of no intervention [25]. However, it is a common experience that many root-filled teeth remain asymptomatic for years and are only detected during routine check-ups or accidentally through more sensitive imaging, such as cone-beam computed tomography. Even when radiographs show no signs of inflammation, histological studies can reveal inflammatory tissue [26]. Thus, there is great uncertainty in both diagnosing and predicting the outcome in the long term for post-­ treatment disease.

The uncertainty about the prognosis and outcome of this condition may lead to overdiagnosis or underdiagnosis. For instance, a root-filled tooth may show minor bone destruction on a radiograph, but the patient is asymptomatic. If no diagnosis of persistent disease is made and no action is taken, and then later severe symptoms occur, it is an example of underdiagnosis.

Overdiagnosis, on the other hand, involves diagnosing a condition that would not have caused symptoms if left undetected. Overdiagnosis is a difficult issue because predicting it in advance requires a level of foresight that is almost impossible to achieve. You need to know whether a condition (such as an indicator, precursor, or risk factor) will lead to a disease in the future [27, 28]. Even when looking back, it is hard to determine if someone has been overdiagnosed. Once a condition has been identified and treated, it is difficult to say if it was truly overdiagnosis. In fact, overdiagnosis can only be confirmed in cases where individuals were diagnosed but left untreated and showed no negative physical or psychological effects of their condition by the end of their lives.

This complicates communication with patients, as they often believe early detection is always beneficial. It is important to discuss the risks of overdiagnosis and overtreatment with patients.

There is also uncertainty in how we define clinical conditions. These definitions often reflect our social commitments and treatment goals, adding another layer of uncertainty—indeterminacy.

In endodontics, there are different classification systems for pulp and periapical conditions. For instance, Strindberg’s system defines success as symptom-free with normal periapical tissues [29], while others suggest less strict criteria, such as arrested bone destruction and a tooth in function [30, 31] or different stages on an ordinal scale [32].

The way we define “disease” affects how we classify post-treatment conditions.
There are two main approaches: naturalist and normativist. Naturalist theories define disease as a biological phenomenon, while normativist theories view disease as a socially constructed concept, often linked to pain, suffering, and societal expectations.

The aspects address different aspects of medicine and dentistry but have been critiqued for not fully capturing all dimensions of human malady, which includes terms such as disease, illness, and sickness [33]. The disease perspective, from a professional point of view, focuses on the physiological, biochemical, genetic, and mental factors that professionals observe, measure, and treat in order to cure. The illness perspective relates to the personal experience of the individual, including pain, anxiety, and suffering. Finally, the sickness perspective examines how disease and illness affect a person’s social status, expectations, and access to health care.
This societal perspective can vary across cultures, with different definitions of what qualifies as “sick” and how conditions, such as a root-filled tooth, are viewed and treated.

In endodontics, the classification of a root-filled tooth with post-treatment apical periodontitis may vary depending on the perspective [23]. From a disease perspective, we focus on measurable, biological changes. From an illness perspective, we consider the patient’s experience—whether the condition causes pain, anxiety, or discomfort. In practice, conditions that do not cause symptoms or suffering may not always and/ or by everyone be classified as sickness and consequently, no candidate for further interventions (i.e., retreatment or extraction) [34].

To engage in clinical practice and perform endodontic treatments on molars requires more than just theoretical knowledge; it demands practical skills and wise judgments. ### 10.11	 Conclusions

Existing classification systems for the outcomes of endodontic treatment are, as we have seen, problematic in many ways. Viewing the outcomes of root canal treatments digitally in terms of success and failure has proven problematic in practice, for both dentists and patients. This is partly because the condition of the root-filled tooth often falls somewhere between “completely healthy” and “completely diseased.” It is also because the terms success and failure themselves are value-laden and often poorly align with how dentists and patients perceive the postoperative situation.

Looking ahead, something different will likely be needed. There are many aspects to consider and weigh in when it comes to the outcomes of various endodontic procedures, but the specialty must also be able to communicate the results of its interventions to other dental and medical specialties, as well as to patients and the general public, in a comprehensive and straightforward manner. We need to be able to present our results in a more diversified way, recognizing that, even if the outcome is not optimal, it may still meet the goals that both the patient and the dentist are satisfied with. Perhaps, one potential way forward could be to describe the outcome as a percentage of the best possible result. This approach would provide a more nuanced and realistic reflection of the treatment’s outcome, accommodating the complexity of individual cases and expectations.

### 10.11.2	 Knowledge Gaps

As we have observed, there are several knowledge gaps in clinical endodontics.
Consequently, there is a continued need for expanding the body of evidence in endodontics. In particular, studies of high quality are necessary to give our patients, the dental community, and other stakeholders less vague answers to the following questions:

•	 In a case involving a tooth with a vital pulp affected by deep caries, is it prefer-

able to preserve pulp vitality rather than perform a pulpectomy and root canal treatment in the long term?
•	 Which methods can be used to distinguish pulps that can be preserved and

heal from those where the disease progression toward pulp necrosis is inevitable?
•	 Is it more cost-effective in the long term to have a root canal treatment and res-

toration rather than to extract the tooth and replace it with a fixed prosthesis or an implant?
•	 Which specific prognostic factors predict treatments that do not achieve an opti-

mal outcome, that is, those that result in extraction, remain painful, or develop/ persist with apical periodontitis?
•	 How often will a root-filled tooth with persistent but asymptomatic periapical

inflammation result in the occurrence of pain and swelling?
•	 Which are the prognostic factors to predict an exacerbation of asymptomatic

periapical inflammation, particularly in a root-filled tooth?
•	 Are there any risks to general health when teeth with a periapical inflammatory

process, particularly in a root-filled tooth, remain untreated?
•	 Under what circumstances, does endodontic retreatment result in favorable out-
