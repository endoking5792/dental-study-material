Setzer FC, Shah SB, Kohli MR, Karabucak B, Kim S. Outcome of endodontic surgery: A meta-analysis of the literature-Part 1: Comparison of traditional root-end surgery and endodontic microsurgery. J Endod 2010;36:1757–65.

This study investigated the outcome of root-end surgery, specifically when comparing traditional root-end surgery and endodontic microsurgery. The latter was defined to include ultrasonic preparation and filling materials as well as microsurgical instruments, high-power magnification, and illumination. A meta-analysis based on 21 studies indicated that the application of modern microsurgical methods resulted in 95% success, while the traditional method achieved success only in 59% of the cases. Therefore, the use of microsurgical techniques is superior for root-end surgery when compared with traditional techniques.

### 12.1

### Introduction

As discussed previously in this book, failing endodontic treatments are based, in principle, on the percolation of bacteria or their by-products from inside the root canal system to outside the periodontium, where interaction with the host immune system occurs. Therefore, success is related to clinical strategies and techniques that prevent this interaction [1].

As detailed in Chap. 11, non-surgical retreatment may provide acceptable clinical outcomes; however, this is only realistic when the clinician can overcome procedural errors that prevent thorough disinfection [2]. Surgical endodontics is then often the last opportunity to save a tooth; the decision to perform a surgery should only be made after all previous non-surgical attempts have failed or are not feasible.

### 12.1.1	 Rationale for Endodontic Microsurgery

This treatment provides healing outcomes when spaces of the root canal system that harbor microorganisms can be readily identified, debrided, and filled with a biocompatible material.

To this end, and in brief, surgical access to the involved root apices is gained, and
3 mm of the respective apex is resected under magnification. The granulation tissues are then removed. A retrocavity of 3 mm depth is adequately prepared and sealed with a biocompatible material in each of the sectioned roots. The need for guided bone regeneration techniques must be evaluated before starting the surgery by clinical (periodontal probing) and radiographic exploration (e.g., cone beam computed tomography (CBCT)).

### 12.1.2	 Diagnostic Steps Before Endodontic Microsurgery

Periodontal probing must be performed for teeth in the areas, as it informs about the profile of the bony crest all around the molar, and most importantly, the width and the length of any discontinuity of it.

Classical radiological explorations like periapical and panoramic radiographs can only detect a few periapical bone lesions, and therefore CBCT [5] is now the standard radiological technique used to prepare a treatment plan in surgical endodontics (see Box 12.1). Only after gathering all the information provided by the clinical and RX exploration, the clinician must decide the correct flap design.

### 12.2

### Endodontic Microsurgery Step by Step

### 12.2.1	 Soft Tissue Management

Similar to the access cavity in non-surgical endodontics, which provides access to canal entries and makes the treatment feasible, flap design is the “access cavity” at the soft tissue level that allows good visibility to the bone level. It is also extremely important because it must allow sufficient blood supply to the mobilized and non-­ mobilized soft tissues [6–10].

Also, all factors, possible incidents, and modifications of the ongoing microsurgery must be taken into consideration before the first incision is made. Therefore, when facing an apicomarginal bone defect, flap design should be made larger enough to have the bone donor site under the same flap, as well as it must allow covering the bone graft without tension on the flap [11].

Modifications to the flap after the incisions have been made can jeopardize the result.

Rectangular and triangular flaps are the typical designs for microsurgery in the molar region. The first incision consists of one horizontal sulcular incision and two vertical ones: one mesial, normally a tooth and a half from the lesion, and one distal.
The triangular one is similar, but with only one vertical mesial incision.

As long as possible, distal incision should be avoided because it is difficult to suture in molar regions. Horizontal incision is normally made intrasulcular, but when the patient has a thick periodontal biotype (wide and thick papillae) and a limited mouth opening, the surgeon can choose papilla base incision technique [12].
This incision at the base of the papilla allows the surgeon to perform the entire surgical procedure from the buccal side, therefore, escaping from the passing the needle and suture back and forth procedure under strong interdental contact points, in order to suture the buccal to the palatal papilla. This technique should not be used when a root dehiscence is present, and the guided bone regeneration technique is expected to be used.

“The golden rules of the flap design” [13] are critical factors the clinician must pay attention to them all the time, to perform an easy, fast microsurgery with less complicated post-op for the patient:

•	 The incision must never cross a bone defect. Incisions should be made away

from the lesion if possible.
•	 Releasing incisions between bone eminences should be over concave bone sur-

faces and should never cross over convex bone eminences.

The end of a vertical incision at the gingival crest should finish at the angular line of the tooth (red line in Fig. 12.1a), perpendicular to the gingival margin (Fig. 12.1b).

Elevation of the Flap
The purpose of this step is to raise the already cut flap to allow visibility of the bone.
The flap should be elevated in such a way that, when back in place, it will rest passively in the desired position. A sharp, small, straight or curved elevator, for example, Molt 9 (Fig. 12.2b), P145S, P9HM, or Prichard, is placed at the junction of the horizontal and vertical incisions, with its concave surface against the bone (Fig. 12.2c).

Retraction
Retraction is done to maintain the flap, allowing maximum access and visibility without causing damage to the flap or neighboring tissues. A correct retraction technique improves ergonomics and reduces the surgery time and the postoperative pain and inflammation. During surgery, soft tissue management is commonly overlooked because, once the flap is retracted, attention is usually concentrated on the defect (Figs. 12.3a, b).

Retractors are one of the most important instruments for surgical endodontics.
They must be:

•	 Wide enough to hold the flap completely (15 mm)
•	 Thin enough (~0.5 mm) to improve access
•	 Have a serrated working end to prevent sliding
•	 Have a matt surface, so light is not reflected

The working end of a retractor should adapt, as far as possible, to the cortical bone plate of the specific surgical area.

### 12.2.2	 Hard Tissue Management

Osteotomy Window (Low Power Magnification)
An osteotomy window approximately 5 mm diameter must be made to allow easy access for instruments and devices to the apex. A round tungsten bur or a 5 mm trephine may be used to create the “access cavity,” at the hard tissue level, to the involved molar apices. When a larger osteotomy window is required in the mandible, a piezosurgery tip can be used instead.

The endodontic surgeon can now encounter three different clinical situations:

•	 Intact cortical bone table
•	 Perforation of the cortical bone plate without periosteum
•	 Perforation of the cortical bone plate with periosteum

Curettage (Medium Power Magnification)
This is to remove pathological tissues, foreign bodies, and bone particles from the periradicular or lateoradicular areas. Curettage provides visibility and accessibility to the apex or lateral canal. Curettage should be performed as clean and fast as possible because the surgical hemostasis is directly related to a complete curettage.
Lingual/palatal bone crypt walls are the most difficult areas to clean. A Lucas bone curette (Figs. 12.4a, b), 33 L spoon excavator, and Molt curettes for medium and large lesions. Alternatively, Columbia #13/14, Molt, Jaquette 34/35 curettes may be used for small lesions and lingual-palatal bone crypt walls (Fig. 12.4c).

Hemostasis (Medium Power Magnification)
Clearly, hemostasis is an indispensable factor for microsurgery because a dry surgical field provides better visualization of the microanatomy, dramatically improves the surgical efficiency, and minimizes root-end filling contamination. Anesthesia (see also Chap. 4 of this book) is essential in providing effective presurgical hemostasis; specifically, solutions with 1:50,000 epinephrine should be used if there are no medical contraindications. Both block and buccal/lingual–palatal injections are required to get effective hemostasis. The use of anesthetics during surgery to achieve surgical hemostasis is typically ineffective.

Topical hemostasis may be achieved with cotton pellets with or without epinephrine (Figs. 12.5a–d) in the osseous crypt; ferric sulfate pellets can be used to achieve hemostasis outside the crypt when applied to bleeding sites on the buccal bone.

Apicoectomy (Low Power Magnification for Apicoectomy and Medium to
High Power Magnification for Inspection)
The apical 3 mm of the root(s) involved are resected (Figs. 12.6a, b), where more complex anatomy exists; this has, for example, been shown histologically in extracted molars, in electron microscopic studies, and micro-computed tomography data (see also Chap. 1 of this book). Preferably, there should be a 0° bevel, and a fluted carbide or Lindemann bur should be used with an Impact air handpiece, under copious irrigation. Current micro instruments such as micromirrors, small ultrasonic (US) tips, and micro pluggers permit working with such a small bevel. After resecting the apical root segment, methylene blue dye is used to ensure that the resection is complete and for canals and isthmus inspection (Fig. 12.7a, b).

Isthmus
One small detail left uncorrected can render the whole procedure ineffective. In this context, the “micro-tissue” within the isthmus is as important as the “macro-tissue” of the main canals.

Although an isthmus is an important structure in endodontic microsurgery, it was ignored in dental textbooks or scientific literature until 1983 [17]. It represents the communications that normally exist, at different levels, between two canals inside a root (Fig. 12.8a), but surgically exposed at the resected apex root area. It contains pulp tissue and should be treated in the same way as the pulp tissue of the canals.

### 12.3

### Bone Defect Classification

Classical studies of endodontic surgery [22, 23] noticed that some failures originated not from the surgical technique itself, but from the size of the lesion and the configuration of cortical bone being present at the time of the surgery [24–26].
Although every step of endodontic microsurgery has dramatically improved over the last few years, comparatively little attention has been paid to the bone defect topography underlying the soft tissue.

The shapes, sizes, and locations of the bone defects encountered during endodontic microsurgery differ substantially. However, bone defect topography dictates the long-term prognosis of microsurgery, independently of the perfectly performed steps of the microsurgery itself [3].

The areas of interest in apical surgery can be divided into two major classes: the apical endodontic area (green) and the cervical or periodontic area (red) (Fig. 12.11a).
Then clinically osseous defects may be classified into Endo lesions (A, B, C), Endo-­
Perio lesions (D), and a combination of them (E). Specific clinical conditions associated with these bone defects are described in detail below.

### 12.3.3	 Through-And-Through Bone Defects

There is a close relationship between the apexes of the upper molars and the maxillary sinus. This is the reason for the fact that an inflammation sinus membrane is an early radiological sign for a periapical infection starting in the apex area [27]. Also, after the root canal treatment, the return to its normal width is an early sign of a successful treatment. The spatial position of the roots of the upper molars and the maxillary sinus produces a simple bone lesion contained inside the bone compartment or perforates one of the cortical bone or two cortical walls, ending up in a complex osseous defect (Figs. 12.12). Typically, through-and-through defects require a specific surgical strategy: an apicoectomy and sealing the retrocavity to prevent bacterial percolation, as well as a guided bone regeneration procedure, using membranes, to regenerate the lost periodontium.

### 12.3.4	 Apicomarginal Bone Defects

This condition can be seen as a mix of two bone defects: a periapical bone defect plus a total root dehiscence. Due to the structural differences between bone maxilla and mandible, a root dehiscence in maxilla tends to be larger overall and wider than the ones encountered in the mandible (Figs. 12.13a–k).

### 12.4

### Intentional Replantation

Intentional replantation is defined as the “insertion of a tooth into its alveolus after the tooth has been extracted for the purpose of performing treatment, such as root-­ end filling (s) or perforation repair” [28]. This is not an exotic procedure but a safe and standard clinical technique to retain an otherwise untreatable tooth, with a high success rate [29–33].

Potential indications for an intentional replantation procedure are as follows:

•	 Difficult access, for example, to apices of second or third mandibular molars is

•	 Anatomical limitations, for example, the proximity of the apices of premolar

•	 Difficult access to perforation or resorption areas.
•	 An intraradicular post cannot be removed either non-surgical or surgically.
•	 Previously attempted and failing retreatment.
•	 Previously attempted and failing apicoectomy.
•	 Severe periodontal problem and/or removal of bone to gain surgical access,

which would leave the patient with an untreatable periodontal problem.
•	 Patient limitations: medical or geriatric patients who cannot cooperate during the

surgery time.

Contraindications for an intentional replantation include the following:

•	 Medical history with anticoagulation medication or compromised healing
•	 Non-restorable tooth
•	 Large periodontal lesions that compromise the tooth stability
•	 Difficult extraction with high risk of bone cortical plate fracture
•	 Root fracture during extraction leaving a poor crown–root ratio

### 12.4.1	 Case Selection for Intentional Replantation

Apart from the technique procedure itself, case selection is the most important factor in replantation prognosis. A clinical case is detailed in Fig. 12.16; here, the inability to debride and prepare via an orthograde approach necessitated the surgery, and the access was too close to the mandibular canal.

### 12.4.2	 Technique

Replantation is biologically based on the viability of the cells and fibers of the periodontal ligament after its stay outside of the socket. It was previously believed that a tooth that was outside of the alveolar socket would suffer from resorption or ankylosis upon replantation, but current data overwhelmingly show that this depends on the viability of the cells of the periodontal ligament: the longer the root is outside the socket, the more readily resorption and ankylosis will occur [34–36]. This is the reason that, before the extraction, the clinical team must rehearse all the surgical steps, so that once the tooth has been removed, the procedure can be carried out efficiently and in minimal time.

### 12.4.3	 Recommendations

Intentional replantation is the last resort to maintain a tooth; therefore, case selection is critical. Crown or root fracture is always a possibility of replantation procedure abortion, and the patient must be informed orally and in writing in the informed consent form.

A fast procedure is significantly related to success. Therefore, “ergonomics is everything.”

More specifically, expedited but gentle manipulation during the extraction, without touching the root surface or compressing the alveolar walls, promotes periodontal healing.

•	 The extraction forceps handles should be grabbed, aided with a rubber band.
•	 Root inspection should be done at maximum power magnification, preparation,

and obturation of cavities at middle power magnification.
•	 The tooth should be rinsed constantly with saline, serum, or tissue culture

solution.
•	 It is best not to curette the socket; rather, the socket clot should be aspirated

without touching the walls.
•	 The shorter the time the tooth is out of the socket, the better. Maximum extraoral

time is about 5 min.

### 12.5

### Conclusions

As shown, the scope of contemporary apical microsurgery includes more than apicoectomies; however, procedures such as hemisections and root amputations have largely fallen into disfavor, since long-term outcomes do appear to compare favorably with implant therapy [37]. Regardless, the overall success rate of endodontic microsurgery is, according to the literature, in the range of non-surgical root canal treatment [4, 38, 39] and clearly superior to root resections [40].
