### 3.1

### Introduction

Apical periodontitis (AP) is an inflammatory condition affecting the periodontal tissues adjacent to the apex of teeth and is caused by bacterial colonisation of the root canal system. This bacterial invasion typically follows the death of pulp tissue, triggering an immunological response aimed at containing the infection. However, this defence mechanism also contributes to the destruction of periradicular tissues and bone resorption. Despite the host’s immune response, the infection cannot be fully eradicated due to the compromised vascular supply. Without treatment, the only way to eradicate the infection is to exfoliate the tooth. In most cases, an equilibrium is established, and the AP prevails as a chronic condition causing minimal

Proper diagnosis and intervention by a dentist are essential to prevent potential complications, ensure appropriate management and restore patient’s health. AP is a global health problem affecting more than half of the adult population worldwide, molars being the most compromised teeth [4–7]. Therefore, molars, with their complex anatomy and strategic functional importance, demand a comprehensive approach to endodontics.

Clinical diagnosis is the cornerstone of decision-making process, balancing clinician-­centred outcomes with patient-centred goals (see Chap. 2). Nonetheless, imaging techniques become an important diagnostic tool for accurate diagnosis of pulpal and periodontal diseases, offering insights that cannot be discerned by clinical examination findings alone [8]. From two-dimensional (2D) periapical radiographs to advanced three-dimensional (3D) imaging modalities such as cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), the evolution of imaging technologies has expanded the diagnostic and therapeutic possibilities in endodontics [9].

Most probably, the colleagues reading this chapter agree that clinical diagnosis forms the foundation of endodontic decision-making. It involves a thorough evaluation of the patient’s symptoms, clinical findings and responses to a set of tests (e.g.
percussion, pulp vitality tests), so the most appropriate treatment for the tooth, and the patient, is selected [10]. Periapical tenderness, pain on mastication and other clinical signs are critical indicators that guide preliminary diagnostic impressions.
However, clinical examination has its limitations, particularly in molars with intricate root canal systems and overlapping anatomical structures. Imaging thus serves as an extension, complementing clinical findings rather than replacing them. As stated in the literature, imaging should always support, but never substitute, the clinical diagnostic process [11]. This chapter delves into the role of imaging in endodontics, with a particular focus on molars, and evaluates its utility as a supplement to clinical diagnosis, with reference to key studies and recent advancements.

### 3.2

### From 2D Images to 3D Volumes: Balancing Diagnostic

### Information and Radiation Dose

Radiographic imaging serves several critical functions in endodontics, from initial assessment until patient follow-up. Traditional 2D imaging techniques, such as periapical radiographs, are often utilised to assess root canal anatomy (i.e. the number, curvature and orientation of roots and root canals), detect periapical pathologies (i.e. radiolucencies indicative of apical periodontitis or cysts) and also assess treatment outcomes (i.e. identifying patterns suggestive of healing, persisting or emerging lesions) [12]. However, while indispensable, 2D radiographs have inherent limitations, particularly for molar teeth, which exhibit complex and variable root canal morphologies. Superimposition of anatomical structures, distortions and the inability to provide cross-sectional views often hinder accurate interpretation [13].
For instance, overlapping roots and the proximity of molars to vital structures like the maxillary sinus or mandibular canal can obscure critical details when assessed from a simplistic bidimensional manner.

The development of CBCT has marked a paradigm shift in dental imaging, offering three-dimensional visualisation that overcomes the limitations of traditional 2D radiography (Fig.  3.1). CBCT‘s ability to generate high-resolution volumetric images has broadened its applications in endodontics, especially for complex molar cases. CBCT introduced an enhanced visualisation of anatomy, with precise representations of the root canal system, facilitating the identification of accessory canals, complex curvatures and isthmuses or C-shaped canals that might be missed on 2D images [14]. This extended anatomic depiction also comes to play in the pre-­surgical planning in endodontics, considering those cases in which surgical re-­treatment of root canal-treated molars is necessary, and neighbouring anatomical landmarks, such as the maxillary sinus or inferior alveolar nerve, must be also visualised in detail. In addition, the assessment of root fractures, a challenging diagnosis with 2D imaging, can be performed more reliably with CBCT [15].

Finally, there has been growing evidence that CBCT is effective in identifying periapical pathosis and at an earlier stage than what could be achieved with 2D images. The literature reports that CBCT significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy for periapical lesions, particularly in the posterior maxilla and mandible [16,
17]. However, at the same time, some concerns have emerged regarding the potential for a larger number of false-positive diagnoses arising from CBCT imaging as periodontal ligament space may present anatomically normal width variations and associated histopathology evidence for lesions detected solely based on CBCT is not always present [18, 19]. On the other hand, the diagnosis of endodontic diseases established from X-ray-based images (i.e. radiographs or CBCT) also runs into the risk of providing false-negative results as these modalities need a certain amount of bone resorption until a radiolucent region can be depicted on the image [13]. The consequence is that the agreement between the radiographic and the histopathological diagnosis of AP ranges from 36% to more than 90% [18]. It is relevant to mention that the AP diagnostic uncertainty is more pronounced in root-filled teeth. As we cannot use the usual clinical testing (e.g. thermal or electric sensibility tests) in root-filled teeth, there is an increased risk of overtreatment which will be time consuming and costly for the patient [18].

CBCT has important inherent limitations; the primary limitation of CBCT is the formation of artefacts, which are distortions or errors in image reconstruction unrelated to the actual anatomical structures. Artefacts originate from various sources, including scanner imperfections, patient movement or beam hardening. These artefacts decrease image quality, jeopardise diagnostic accuracy and, in some cases, make image interpretation impossible [20–22]. Beam hardening artefacts originate from metallic or high atomic number materials—commonly found in teeth that have undergone endodontic treatment—resulting in a brighter appearance of the metal and distortion of adjacent structures. Patient motion artefacts can cause double contours and a general lack of sharpness in the image, leading to deteriorated diagnostic image quality. Additionally, since endodontic CBCT protocols typically use longer exposure times, there is a higher likelihood of motion artefacts, especially in high-­ resolution scans. Studies indicate that artefacts can significantly hinder the identification of accessory canals or the diagnosis of root fractures [15], highlighting the need for advanced imaging techniques or artefact reduction strategies to improve diagnostic reliability in endodontics.

It is also relevant to highlight that CBCT presents higher radiation doses than radiographs and thus is not a replacement for clinical evaluation or 2D radiography but comes as a complementary diagnostic tool. Current clinical guidelines advocate the selective use of CBCT, emphasising the importance of balancing diagnostic benefits against patient radiation exposure [14, 23–26]. This approach aligns with the ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) and ALADA (As Low As
Diagnostically Acceptable) principles, emphasising the need to minimise radiation exposure while maintaining diagnostic efficacy [27]. Therefore, CBCT is recommended in cases where traditional 2D imaging fails to provide sufficient information, when clinical and radiographic information remain contradictory or inconclusive, or when treatment complexity necessitates advanced visualisation of anatomical structures [14]. Lastly, CBCT remains restricted to the depiction of hard tissue.

CBCT accuracy and acquisition parameters have been widely studied to help clinicians determine when CBCT is necessary and which protocol to use.
Examinations should be tailored to the patient’s clinical condition, adjusting exposure parameters to optimise diagnostic quality while minimising radiation. CBCT is superior to 2D radiographs for detecting root fractures, adding a third dimension, with smaller voxel sizes and field of view (FOV) improving accuracy [15]. However, artefacts from radiopaque materials (e.g. posts and restorations) remain a challenge.
Artefact severity varies by scanner and protocol, but proper selection can help mitigate effects. In metal-free cases, small FOV protocols yield better image quality, whereas large FOVs may reduce spatial resolution and increase radiation exposure.
Further research is needed to better understand the impact of FOV on metal artefact visualisation, while also considering the trade-off with radiation dose [14, 22].

In endodontics, high-resolution, small FOV protocols are recommended as they optimise image quality [14], but same FOV high-resolution protocols can increase exposure up to 15-fold, whereas low-resolution images may be sufficient in certain clinical tasks [24, 27–29]. Despite its limitations, CBCT has revolutionised the evaluation of dental and maxillofacial structures by providing detailed three-dimensional imaging, enhancing diagnostic accuracy in specific clinical scenarios and ultimately improving patient care. Its ability to overcome the drawbacks of conventional 2D radiography has made it invaluable endodontic complex cases. Nonetheless, CBCT’s own deficiencies underscore the growing need for alternative imaging modalities that can effectively assess the dentomaxillofacial region while eliminating radiation exposure. Endodontic inflammatory processes occur within the root canal system and periapical tissues and often progress without noticeable clinical symptoms or radiographic signs. In some cases, the inflammation may develop into an acute condition, causing significant pain for the patient. However, at this stage, bone resorption may not have occurred yet, preventing the detection of any changes on radiographic or tomographic images. X-ray-based imaging depends on the attenuation of the X-ray beam by hard tissues, which is influenced by their density and degree of mineralisation. As a result, chronic lesions and scar tissue often appear identical on radiographs, regardless of the differences in cellular composition or physiological processes [31]. Given these challenges, the use of imaging techniques capable of distinguishing tissue components and disease activity would greatly enhance the accuracy of endodontic diagnosis.

### 3.3

### Future Perspectives: Non-ionising Imaging

The introduction of CBCT marked a significant advancement in dental diagnostics, offering greater accuracy and improved visualisation of hard tissues. However, certain clinical scenarios still lack sufficient imaging data, particularly when assessment depends on soft tissues or inflammatory processes. To address this limitation and facilitate a digital workflow without radiation exposure concerns, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasonography (US) have emerged as promising alternatives [32, 33]. While US is a more affordable, portable and time-efficient option, it is primarily suitable for assessing superficial soft tissues. The presence of bone disrupts acoustic transmission, limiting its application in deeper structures. In contrast, MRI represents a cutting-edge advancement in dental imaging, providing detailed visualisation of internal tissues and offering unique advantages over traditional radiographic techniques. Furthermore, MRI has the potential to provide information beyond structural imaging, such as assessing pulp vitality and detecting oedema within periapical (soft) tissues [34]. feasibility of using MRI for endodontic applications, paving the way for its integration into clinical practice [9].

MRI enables both the anatomical assessment of soft tissues and the evaluation of pathological conditions by measuring the radiofrequency (RF) signal and its ‘echo’ when tissues are exposed to a strong magnetic field. This field, combined with the gradient coils, aligns the normally randomly oriented hydrogen protons within the water molecules of the patient’s tissues. When an external RF pulse is applied, the aligned protons are temporarily displaced, and their subsequent realignment generates a measurable signal. Over a specific period, as a series of RF pulses are emitted and received, these signals are processed using mathematical Fourier transformations to create grayscale images. The intensity of the signal determines the brightness of the image: tissues with a higher measured signal appear brighter. Since different tissues contain varying amounts of water, their responses to RF pulses differ accordingly. In pathological conditions, particularly in cases of inflammation where water content increases, these variations also alter the RF response and influence how the images appear. Moreover, MRI parameters can be adjusted through different RF pulse sequences. Two key parameters include repetition time (TR), which is the interval between successive RF pulses, and time to echo (TE), the duration between the RF pulse and the received echo signal. By modifying these parameters, different greyscale contrasts are obtained, allowing for optimised visualisation of specific tissues and clinical conditions. Depending on the diagnostic need, one or more specific acquisition pulse sequences should be selected to ensure the most accurate assessment [32, 35]. Given this brief overview, it becomes evident that the physics behind MRI acquisition is significantly more complex than the straightforward ‘absorb or pass through’ mechanism of X-ray-based imaging.

MRI has been used for oral and maxillofacial evaluation for many years, though its application has traditionally been limited to specific areas, such as temporomandibular joint (TMJ) diagnosis and oral pathology assessment [36, 37]. Despite its diagnostic advantages, MRI has remained largely inaccessible for routine dental clinical practice. Several factors contribute to this, including the high cost of the machine and its maintenance, making the examination itself expensive. Additionally,
MRI scanners are typically housed in hospitals or medical facilities, where dental applications must compete for priority with more critical medical conditions.
Another limitation is the quality of dental imaging as skull or general-purpose coils are commonly used, leading to a loss of detail. Although efforts have been made to develop extraoral and intraoral coils, they are not yet widely available. Furthermore,
MRI examinations are generally interpreted by medical radiologists rather than dental professionals, creating an additional barrier to integration into dental practice
[35]. In recent years, however, there has been growing interest in MRI within both the dental clinical and research communities, as well as among health industry stakeholders. This increasing attention has positioned MRI as a viable future tool for dental practitioners, culminating in the development of a dental-dedicated MRI (ddMRI) system tailored specifically for oral and maxillofacial applications (Fig. 3.2).

The ddMRI has optimised MRI technology for dental applications by customising hardware and software, reducing costs, improving patient comfort and streamlining workflows for dentomaxillofacial imaging [34]. One of its key advancements is the use of a low-field strength scanner (i.e. 0.55 T), which lowers machine costs and complexity compared to high-field systems while still providing sufficient image quality for dental applications. Additionally, advanced techniques such as compressed sensing and artificial intelligence have been incorporated into imaging protocols to enhance scan efficiency, accelerating acquisition times while maintaining an optimal signal-to-noise ratio. Another crucial innovation is the dental radiofrequency surface coil, specifically designed to the dentomaxillofacial region. This coil features bendable wings that ensure a close fit to the patient, improving signal detection and enhancing image quality. Furthermore, ddMRI employs customised software and specialised pulse sequences tailored to common dental diagnostic tasks, such as orthodontic planning, TMJ assessment, evaluation of impacted teeth and endodontic imaging. These sequences offer short acquisition times, a restricted field of view and appropriate resolution and contrast. dental procedures. The process includes scout images, precise field-of-view delimitation and pulse sequence selection specifically adapted to the dentomaxillofacial region, making MRI more accessible and practical for dental applications.

Contemporary ddMRI can generate both 3D volumetric images optimised for visualising dentomaxillofacial anatomy and 2D image slabs. The pulse sequences are based on T1, T2 or proton density (PD) weightings, with the latter relying on the sheer number of hydrogen nuclei present in the tissue and being particularly useful for bone visualisation. To assess signs of inflammation, fat suppression pulse sequences are also included in the available imaging protocols. For endodontic evaluation, both anatomical and inflammation-focused pulse sequences are utilised, enabling the identification of structures inside the tooth, such as the pulp chamber and canals, as well as external structures like the periapical tissues and bone marrow. Unlike X-ray-based imaging, MRI allows for the visualisation of pulp contents, offering direct insights into pulp vitality. In ddMRI images, a tooth with a vital pulp appears with a bright pulp chamber and canals due to the presence of tissue hydration, whereas a necrotic pulp is seen as dark. When diagnosing periapical tissues, the earliest indicator of endodontic disease is inflammation within the soft tissues. To highlight this, images are acquired using fat suppression protocols, which enhance the visibility of increased fluid content in the affected area.
Consequently, in cases of AP, the apical bone marrow appears brighter compared to a tooth with a healthy periodontal ligament. By providing this additional layer of diagnostic information, ddMRI may serve as a valuable new tool for supporting clinical findings suggestive of endodontic disease.

As with radiographs and CBCT, MRI also has technical limitations that must be considered. Image quality can be affected by the presence of metal and other materials that interfere with the magnetic field, potentially compromising diagnostic accuracy. Artefacts from dental fillings, crowns and orthodontic appliances can significantly hinder the evaluation of the oral cavity [35, 38]. This is particularly relevant in endodontic assessments as teeth undergoing imaging for such purposes often contain root and crown restorative materials. In this context, it is essential to assess the behaviour, appearance and impact of dental materials on image quality and diagnostic accuracy. However, artefact severity tends to be lower in MRI systems with reduced field strength (1  T and below) compared to high-field units, which may favour the use of ddMRI. Motion artefacts also pose a challenge as MRI acquisition requires longer scan times, making it more susceptible to patient movement—even respiration can impact image quality. Additionally, MRI has both relative and absolute contraindications. Patients with pacemakers or metallic implants in the body, especially those containing iron, may not be eligible for scanning, necessitating a thorough medical evaluation and, when in doubt, consultation with their physician. Furthermore, individuals with claustrophobia or sensitivity to loud noises may find the MRI experience challenging, potentially limiting its feasibility in certain cases.

### 3.4

### Clinical Challenges: From Low-Dose

### to No-Dose Imaging

When assessing periapical diseases in the molar region, as in any other region of the mouth, it is relevant to define a diagnostic plan that starts from the less complex image possibilities (intraoral radiographs) and climbs to more complex imaging modalities (e.g. CBCT), but that also offer larger radiation doses to the patient.
Recently, the possibility of adding ddMRI to this latter provided additional information on the soft-tissue status, highlighting the presence of oedema within the periapical area (see Fig. 3.2).

The lack of soft-tissue visualisation provided by CBCT can lead to incorrect diagnosis and may thus compromise the treatment plan, directly impacting the accuracy of CBCT for detecting periapical lesions. In that direction, the additional value provided by ddMRI, which also includes the depiction of the soft-tissue status, might significantly add to the accurate diagnosis of disease. Figure 3.3 presents a case in which the CBCT was suggestive of AP, while ddMRI showed healthy periapical tissues. Still, the literature currently lacks evidence pointing at how accurate the ddMRI-based diagnosis of AP is compared to histological gold standards and clinical reference standards. Considering the soft-tissue diagnosis, a substantial added value regarding the use of MRI in endodontics is the possibility for assessing the pulp vitality status directly from the images as a living pulp will provide brighter (i.e. hyperintense) images, as visible in Fig. 3.3. On the contrary, both root-filled canals and necrotic pulps will present darker (i.e. hypointense) on the images.

### 3.5

### Conclusions

Imaging plays an undeniably significant role in endodontics, particularly for molars with their intricate and variable anatomy (see Box 3.1). The transition from 2D periapical radiographs to CBCT and emerging modalities like ddMRI has expanded diagnostic capabilities, yet clinical diagnostic process remains paramount. Rather than replacing traditional assessments, imaging provides additional layers of information that enhance the clinical decision-making process. As technology continues to evolve, the integration of advanced imaging techniques into routine practice should be guided by evidence-based protocols, ensuring optimal diagnostic accuracy while prioritising patient-centred care and radiation safety.
