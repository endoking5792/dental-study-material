### 5.1

### Introduction

The management of deep caries is a daily issue in endodontic practice, with a root canal treatment of frequently prescribed therapy for carious exposure. On the other hand, for caries in the pulpal quarter of the dentin, randomised trials have shown that a stepwise excavation approach led to a significant decrease in the number of pulp exposures as opposed to complete (non-selective) excavation.

Before performing vital pulp therapy (VTP) in a molar, clinicians will have to answer two questions: (A) How invasive a treatment needs to be in fulfilling the aims of preserving and maintaining the pulp tissue in a healthy clinical condition, and whether (B) the chosen modality is a permanent or temporary treatment solution? Both topics depend upon the cause and time of the injury; is the pulp exposed because of trauma or through caries? What is the aim of performing vital pulp therapy and when could a pulp exposure particularly in carious teeth be avoided, by changing towards an indirect pulp therapy approach, that is, a less invasive carious removal procedure?

The controversy about caries and the pulp goes back more than a century [1, 2] and represents a problem of deciding the right treatment in dentistry. G.V. Black wrote in his textbook: ‘... it will often be a question whether or not the pulp will be exposed when all decayed dentine overlaying the pulp is removed...and It is better to expose the pulp of a tooth than to leave it covered only with softened dentine’ [1], whereas Sir John Tomes wrote decades before [2]: ‘...It is better that a layer of discolored dentine should be allowed to remain for the protection of the pulp rather than run the risk of sacrificing the tooth’.

Still, the same deep caries scenario may be interpreted and treated differently by clinicians [3–5]. The caries and pulp dilemma are partly due to the lack of a non-­ invasive diagnostic device for monitoring degrees of inflammation in the pulp [6], as well as a different interpretation of available scientific evidence addressing the best treatment [7–9].

In this chapter, we will detail the rationale behind separating the extensive carious lesion into three stages using the radiograph [10]. When describing caries, a deep lesion (DL) 1 is one that is just into the threshold of 2/3 of the dentin thickness.
A DL 2 lesion is defined as being within the inner quarter of the dentin but with a radio-dense zone separating the lesion from the pulp while extremely deep caries (DL3) is penetrating the entire thickness of the dentin (Fig. 5.1).

A pulp exposure is defined relatively broadly being the result of a pathological change in the hard tissue of a tooth caused by caries, a mechanical factor or trauma
[11]. Here, a classification of pulp-capping procedures is introduced separating two scenarios: direct pulp-capping class I (pulp exposure has occurred due to a trauma or made accidentally and site is expected to be contaminated) and direct pulp-­ capping class II (pulp exposure is expected, as part of caries excavation and the infection level is high prior exposure of the pulp). This chapter aims to align updated treatment guidelines on the best clinical evidence for VPT in molars, including less invasive carious removal procedures.

### 5.2.2

### Tertiary Dentinogenesis

The stimulation of tertiary dentinogenesis is a main objective of the vital pulp therapy. The classification and definition of tertiary dentin has in the past been described by many synonyms [13, 14]; here, it is presented in the context of dentinogenesis in general (Box 5.2). Tertiary dentin defines new hard tissue produced in relation to an external injury [15]. The subdivision of tertiary dentin into reactionary dentinogenesis and reparative dentinogenesis introduces a biological platform with more emphasis on the cellular origin [16, 17], as well as causes for the upregulation of the aged odontoblast secretion [18–20]. Reactionary dentin can be interpreted as the elevated growth of dentinogenesis, related to early caries lesions [21], and the odontoblast cells are stimulated by dentin matrix proteins released during caries demineralisation [22, 23]. These upregulated primary odontoblast cells [19] are involved directly, but also a mixture with a new population of so-called secondary odontoblast-­ like cells can be observed in natural caries development [24]. In case of a pulp exposure, a completely new dentin formation leads to the reestablishment of a dentinal bridge formation, defined as reparative dentinogenesis (Box 5.2). Initially, it is composed of a layer of atubular mineralised matrix, previously described as fibrodentin [15], followed by a tubular dentin formation, presumably laid down by new secondary odontoblast-like cells. Recent research has shown that these pre-­ mineralised stages of atubular matrix may represent cartilage-like matrix (Fig. 5.2), most often seen within DL3 lesions [25].

A significant portion of the dental literature has been dealing with the study of a newly formed hard tissue barrier and the status of the pulp after vital pulp therapy.

### 5.2.3

### Inflammation and Tertiary Dentin

Most experimental studies on pulp capping and protective agents have been carried out in healthy teeth with intact pulps, which makes it difficult to translate information into the clinical situation, where the exposed pulp most often is expected to be associated with various grades of inflammation. The severity of pulp inflammation may influence tertiary dentin deposition. At the extreme, inflammation will lead to some degree of pulp necrosis; hence, delay or even eliminate any tertiary dentinogenesis. In contrast, milder degrees of pulp inflammation may seem beneficial for tertiary dentinogenesis [27]. ### 5.4

### Indications and Treatment Concepts for Vital

### Pulp Therapy

The indications for performing vital pulp therapy are carious lesions, mechanical iatrogenic injury and trauma. In the context of molar endodontics, the most frequent cause of vital pulp therapy has, in the general practice environment, been caries
[35]. Therefore, the aligned treatment guidelines are described within the ‘scenario’ of a carious pulp exposure. From the literature, it has previously been unclear during the management of extensive caries, whether the clinicians are creating a pulp exposure by accident or whether it is expected due to the actual closeness towards the pulp as the penetration of the carious lesion has not been reported [36]. However, this has changed; a recent observational study with a 5-year follow-up [37] examined the event during extensive carious lesion as it could be carried out amongst dental practitioners. ### 5.4.1

### Direct Pulp-Capping Class I (Non-infected

### Scenarios/Accidental Pulp Exposure)

No additional enlargement is made of the exposed pulp. The exposure has just occurred following a trauma or an accidental perforation during a preparation procedure. It is a prerequisite that the pulp is vital and the patient is without subjective pain prior treatment, and haemostasis can be expected. The pulp protective agent can be placed directly at the pulp exposure and the cavity can be sealed efficiently.
It may also take place during excavation of caries, but in an area not directly related to the carious dentin, because the pulp exposure has been made by accident.

### ‘Partial’ Pulpotomy

### 5.4.4

### ‘Full’ Pulpotomy

In single-rooted teeth, the definition includes the surgical amputation of the coronal portion of an exposed pulp, whereas in a molar it covers the removal of the entire pulp tissue in the pulp chamber down to the orifices (1–2 mm into in the root canals).
The rationale is to completely remove the inflamed pulp tissue.

### 5.4.5

### ‘Emergency’ Pulpotomy

So-called dental emergency treatments have to be considered with a practical aspect, lack time, as the patient arrives unexpectedly to the dental office and in acute pain.
Thus, an achievable treatment is urgently needed to reduce the level of pain in the first place and then a treatment session can be scheduled to complete the final pulpectomy, when proper time is available. An emergency pulpotomy has been shown to be able to reduce pain in cases of painful pulpitis, without the need for additional medicaments [50]. It is a temporary treatment, with the clinical approach the same as the ‘full’ pulpotomy, except that in an emergency treatment a temporary rather than a definitive restoration is placed.

### 5.5

### Brief Comments on Capping Agents

For decades, the capping agent to promote healing and maintain viability of the remaining pulp tissue has been a calcium hydroxide-containing material applied either as a base or as a liner. Over the last 30 years, so-called hydraulic calcium silicate cement (HCSC) has emerged as the material of choice for VPT. While there is a demonstrated benefit of this material class over calcium hydroxide [51], only limited clinical information is available to show potential outcome differences among most commercially available HCSCs. Clinical experience suggests that the postoperative stain following application is not the same among the brands.

One biological reason for selecting silicate-containing materials has been that that these materials may create a better quality of the hard tissue laid down, without so-called tunnel defects. Also, it is important to note that reparative dentin with or without so-called ‘tunnel defects’ will not be able to block bacterial invasion from untreated progressing caries and/or a leaking coronal restoration.

The optimal endpoint for evaluating VPT would be to determine the long-term clinical outcome of the pulp, that is, function, healthy state and capacity to prevent a secondary infection [26]. Taken together, the concept of several clinical aspects together may be the very most important aspect. This is expressed as the mandatory use of an ‘enhanced’ protocol (i.e. dental dam, antimicrobial lavage, magnification and use of an HCSC, class II), as presented later in this chapter and aligned with the
‘state of the art’ for treating carious lesions [39].

### 5.6.4

### Indications for Indirect Pulp Therapy (Selective Carious

### Removal or Stepwise Excavation)

The so-called indirect pulp therapy addresses the excavation of deep dentin, aiming to avoid pulp exposure. The therapy may be performed either in one or two treatment sessions. Much research during the past decades has modified the original concept of a pulp close excavation during the indirect pulp therapy [52]. Kerkhove et al. [53] described it as leaving only residual caries, and if a further limited excavation was carried out, a pulp exposure would be present. Other studies have applied the selective approach leaving soft or firm dentin behind permanently and with an apparent good prognosis, primarily in children [54–56]; therefore, the original invasive concept of indirect pulp therapy appears outdated.

Stepwise Exaction (Two-Step Approach): The concept of performing two treatment sessions is also described as a stepwise excavation. The focus of the first treatment/excavation appointment is to perform selective carious removal to soft dentin to an extent where a temporary restoration (treatment restoration) can be placed [57,
58]. The aim of the first treatment is to change and inactivate the cariogenic environment. The consequence of leaving carious dentin temporarily subjacent the treatment restoration is that the clinical features of carious dentin will change to that of slowly progressing or arrested caries [59, 60]. The active deep carious environment can clinically be recognised as a soft discoloured and wet tissue, which may change towards a darker, harder and drier appearance of the carious dentin; moreover, it will shrink. Clinically, a second and non-selective excavation of arrested carious dentin will be easier to accomplish without risking a pulp exposure. A gap often created between the treatment restoration and the retained arrested carious dentin due to shrinkage between visits will be gone following the removal of the restoration, and the final restoration can be placed in a stable cavity.

High-level evidence data indicates that a stepwise excavation approach led to a significant decrease in the number of pulp exposures as opposed to non-selective complete excavation [61–63]. This approach may be carried out within a practice-­ based environment even with the concept of two visits [64]. Within communities where it would be difficult to receive the patient for an additional appointment, a one-step selective caries removal approach might be the treatment of choice; however, the evidence supporting this is of lower quality [57]. A guide for choosing either one or two steps is based on the best available evidence [65] and could be the penetration of the carious lesion on the radiograph, being either DL 1 for a selective approach and DL 2 for a two-step approach.

### 5.7

### Changing Indications for Vital Pulp Therapy

Undoubtedly the vital pulp therapies have been established and developed in their own context of time and tradition. The focus of full pulpotomy has been to maintain a vital radicular pulp in order to secure the completion of the root complex in immature teeth (apexogenesis), and particularly to avoid the necrotic and bacterial-­ infected incomplete root complex.

About 50 years ago, the prevalence of caries worldwide as well as the speed of caries progression was higher than it is today [66]. If a ‘deep’ or an ‘extreme deep’ lesion is present even before the root complex is fully completed, the speed of caries progression has obviously been very high. Accordingly, it may be speculated that the degree of inflammation in ‘pulps’ with carious exposures in the 1960s was often in a more severe and irreversible stage than what can be observed today. In other words, the rationale and the magnitude of using ‘full’ pulpotomy may have changed today as the speed of the caries progression has reduced.

As previously introduced, two classification systems are presented in this chapter. Having a more nuanced system may separate outcome better as reported recently.
In adult patients, a vital pulp therapy (partial pulpotomy) was 90% successful using the binary classification (100% success when pulp diagnosed as reversible and 78% success when pulp was diagnosed as irreversible). Using the graded classification, the distribution was 100% (mild), 88% (moderate) and only 60% success when treating patients with severe pulpitis. A significant difference in outcome between mild and severe pulpitis groups was observed using the graded classification.
Notably, lesion penetration was indicative of a failure, being the extreme deep lesion scenario [46].

The ‘full’ pulpotomy treatment has started to be re-evaluated [67], and it may be an alternative to the vital pulpectomy. Also, the research area of pulp regeneration has developed rapidly. In this chapter, ‘full’ pulpotomy has been presented with respect to (I) securing apexogenesis, and (II) being the emergency treatment approach for the acute ‘irreversible pulpitis’ in molars.

However, pulpotomy is a highly integrated treatment approach for the future revascularisation procedure, and perhaps particularly in necrotic teeth with an incomplete root complex [68, 69]. A revascularisation protocol has been suggested but currently being modified and tested [70, 71]. At this point in time, only limited evidence from case reports illustrates the potential for regenerative therapy in molars as opposed to single-rooted teeth.

### 5.8

### Conclusions: Checklist for Molar Vital Pulp Therapy

•	 Carious pulp exposures are the most frequent cause for vital pulp therapy

in molars.
•	 Case selection should be performed with attention towards the age of the patient

and depth of the caries lesion.
•	 Vital and indirect pulp therapies should be seen as permanent treatments.
•	 Direct capping procedures shall use an ‘enhanced protocol’ (i.e. dental dam,

antimicrobial lavage, magnification and use of an hCSC, class II).
•	 No difference can be found between direct pulp capping and partial pulpotomy

in adults.
•	 Pulpotomy might be the future technical approach for even cases with irrevers-

ible signs of pulpitis.
•	 The two-step indirect approach (selective and/or stepwise excavation) should be

the treatment of choice in carious lesions extending into the pulpal 1/4 of the dentin with a radiopaque zone separating the carious lesions.
•	 Indirect pulp capping (selective and or stepwise excavation) shall not be per-

formed in extreme deep lesions or with pulpal symptoms.
