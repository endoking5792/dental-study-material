### 6.2

### The Challenge of Treating

### Tooth Sensitivity

Tooth sensitivity is a common dental condition for which there have been many remedies proposed over the years (Emling 1982; Rosenthal
1990; Markowitz 2009). Since the turn of the previous century, the importance of the dentinal tubules in tooth sensitivity has been recognized (Gysi 1900). Many early attempts at treatments for tooth sensitivity either attempted to occlude the dentinal tubules by using harsh chemical treatments to coagulate the poorly understood
“protoplasmic” contents of the dentinal tubules or use the anesthetic compounds known at the time such as cocaine derivatives (potent topical and local anesthetics) to inactivate the neuronal elements in the tooth (Rosenthal 1990).

### 6.3

### How Can Sensitivity

### Treatments Work?

In the treatment of most diseases, drugs block or disrupt the events associated with the development or symptoms of the disease being treated.
The physiologic process responsible for tooth sensitivity (Chap. 5) involves stimuli activating intradental nerves, resulting in action potential generation. Odontoblasts have been shown to participate in this process. The ability of stimuli to cause fluid shifts in the dentin tubules is an important way of activating both odontoblasts and nerves. Several stimuli such as hot and cold may also activate these cells directly (independently of tubule fluid movements) as well. As stated in works that applied the hydrodynamic

hypothesis to tooth sensitivity therapy research (Markowitz and Pashley 2008), materials that reduce dentin permeability or, alternatively, target the activation or conduction of the intradental nerves should reduce tooth sensitivity pain.
Advances in the physiological basis of the nerve activation process should provide new and better therapeutic opportunities. Although oversimplified and neglecting many aspects of the pain experience, targeting the initial, peripheral mechanisms of pain generation is something a consumer product can do.

### 6.4

### Desensitization Treatments

### Targeting Dentin

### Permeability

The reduction of dentin permeability via tubule occlusion is an important strategy for treating sensitive teeth (Pashley 1986; Cummins 2009).
Reducing dentin permeability acts to not only attenuate stimulus-evoked dentin fluid flow but also to prevent the activation of the intradental nerves and odontoblasts by directly acting physical stimuli and by reducing the inward diffusion of irritating chemical agents (Hirvonen et  al.
1984; Rifai et  al. 2004; Chidchuangchai et  al.
2007). This is still true in view of the recent observations clarifying the role of odontoblasts in dentin sensitivity reviewed in Chap. 5.

In vitro studies measuring the impact of treatments on the dentin’s permeability and microscopic appearance are used to screen the ability of materials and products to reduce dentin fluid flow (see Chap. 7). Treatments that are effective in vitro and otherwise acceptable are candidates for more focused research activities including clinical trials. Due to the steep relationship between tubule radius and flow (flow ∝ r4), it is expected that dentin may be desensitized by treatments that induce partial tubule occlusion, reducing the stimulus-evoked flow to values that fail to activate a sufficient nerve response needed to result in conscious pain sensation. Several clinically tested desensitizing treatments cause high reductions in flow when tested in  vitro (Pashley et  al. 1978a, b). Other desensitizing

As a practical matter, it may be easier to make dentin sensitive by increasing dentin permeability than to desensitize dentin by reducing dentin permeability.
The dentin–pulp complex has the capacity for repair (Smith et al. 2012). However, as is evident from clinical observation, individuals with dentin that is exposed to the oral environment do not necessarily experience DH.  Several natural mechanisms are known to reduce dentin permeability (Daculsi et al. 1987). In animals with continuously erupting teeth, dentin is exposed, and the tubules mineralize rapidly. Continuously erupting teeth like rat incisors or horse molars wear rapidly. In these teeth, dentin exposure is followed by rapid dentin formation and tubule occlusion (Dimuzio and Veis 1978). In humans, age, chronic exposure to the oral environment, and being subjacent to caries are accompanied by physiological occlusion of dentin tubules (Daculsi et al. 1987; Tagami et al. 1992).
Tertiary and peritubular dentin formation are pulpal processes that reduce dentin permeability and DH (Lundy and Stanley 1969; Senawongse et al. 2008). Growth factors that stimulate tertiary dentin formation are left in the mineralized dentin during development. These growth factors may become exposed and subsequently released by tooth wear, erosion initiated by dietary acids and dentin etching (Smith et  al. 2012). ### 6.5

### Strontium Salts

Strontium (Sr) salts are delivered as active agents in dentifrices to treat sensitive teeth (Hughes et al. 2010; Olley et al. 2012; Seong et al. 2013;
West et  al. 2013). Application of strontium-­ containing dentifrices to dentin in  vitro caused partial tubule occlusion, and as discussed below, shares with other divalent cation solutions some ability to reduce intradental nerve excitability.
Strontium shares many biological features of calcium including its ability to be incorporated into bone and some capacity to suppress caries development. This “heritage” prompted the formulation of 10% SrCl2 as an active agent in toothpastes for sensitive teeth in the early 1960s (Markowitz
2009). In contrast to SrCl2, strontium acetate has greater compatibility with fluoride sources in dentifrices. In addition to problems with fluoride compatibility, SrCl2 proved to be a demanding active to formulate owing to the tendency of Sr++ to bind many of the common anionic detergents and abrasive particles used in dentifrices (Markowitz 2009). The incorporation of 10%
SrCl2 into dentifrices also imparts a strong
“medicinal” flavor to the dentifrice that can be a benefit as it signals an expectation of effectiveness in the minds of consumers.

Using an in situ methodology where subjects wore acrylic intraoral devices with dentin chips exposed on the buccal surface, brushing with an
8% strontium acetate toothpaste resulted in progressive build-up of tubule occlusion over time (Claydon et  al. 2009). Exposure of intact and demineralized dentin surfaces with strontium acetate solutions and gels results in tubule occlu-

sion and some improvement in the mechanical properties of the surface (Saeki et  al. 2016).
Although some effects such as the ability to localize Sr on the surface and within the tubules were reduced by water washing, these results support the concept that strontium-containing dentifrices act by reducing dentin permeability.
Applications of Sr-containing biomaterials and glasses materials including novel polymer complexes (Xu et al. 2024) demonstrate that future applications of strontium to tooth sensitivity are likely.

### 6.6

### Desensitization Treatments

### Targeting the Intradental

### Nerves

In sensitive teeth, stimuli either activate or reach the intradental nerves by way of the dentin tubules. These tubules can also act as a route of administration for therapeutic agents intended to treat sensitive teeth. Drugs delivered topically to the tooth surface may potentially diffuse through dentin to reach the intradental nerves and other targets located in the deep dentin and pulp (Ciarlone and Pashley 1992). Potassium nitrate (KNO3) was introduced into clinical use as a desensitizing agent based on empirical observations (Hodosh 1974). Several clinical studies indicate that KNO3 and other potassium salts reduce sensitivity symptoms over a period of approximately 2  weeks when applied by twice daily brushing (Orchardson and Gillam 2000) (see Chap. 8). Potassium salts have also been used to treat pain caused by tooth whitening procedures (Lima et al. 2024). These salts have little effect on dentin permeability when tested in vitro (Greenhill and Pashley 1981), suggesting that they alleviate DH symptoms by interfering with the activation of the intradental nerves.

When potassium salt solutions are applied to deep dentin cavities or placed on isolated nerves in concentrations far exceeding their physiological level, they have a profound action on nerve excitability (Orchardson 1978; Markowitz et al.
1991; Peacock and Orchardson 1999). When first applied to deep dentin cavities, or when applied

These experiments demonstrate that potassium ions have a desensitizing action, and today, a variety of potassium salts are commercially available in desensitizing products.

When solutions containing high potassium ion concentration are applied using positive pressure to test cavities in human teeth, pain is initially evoked (Ajcharanukul et  al. 2007). This pain response corresponded to the nerve excitation observed in the animal experiments. When the dentin floor of these test cavities was probed or air blasted after pressure application of a potassium solution, the pain responses were reduced compared to those measured prior to potassium solution application. Solutions containing elevated sodium ion concentration did not share this desensitizing effect. In contrast, when these potassium ion-containing solutions were applied

to human dentin cavities without positive pressure, there was no pain. Furthermore, the pain inhibiting action of potassium salts was modest and of short duration when these solutions were applied to human dentin cavities under atmospheric pressure (Noparatkailas et  al. 2009).
These results indicate that without the assistance of inward pressure, the penetration of potassium ions to the intradental nerve endings by diffusion is limited.

These experiments imply that although potassium salts can inhibit pain induced by clinically relevant stimuli, this effect may be limited when these agents are applied to the dentin surface of sensitive teeth in clinical use. In a published clinical study designed to test an arginine-calcium carbonate-based toothpaste, a commercially available potassium salt-containing dentifrice was used as a positive control. The potassium salt-based product performed better than the fluoride toothpaste negative control but had an efficacy that was inferior to the arginine compound-containing product (Docimo et al. 2009).
Although this and many other clinical studies support the use of potassium salts as a desensitizing agent (Orchardson and Gillam 2000), evidence demonstrating potassium salt’s efficacy is considered weak by several authors (Poulsen et al. 2006; Karim and Gillam 2013) (Table 6.1, see also Chap. 8).

Dentin’s outward fluid flow acts to oppose and reduce the inward diffusion of solutes such as potassium ions through dentin (Stead et al. 1996;
Vongsavan et  al. 2000). This aspect of dentin physiology accounts for the difficulty clinicians encounter when attempting to anesthetize teeth by placing local anesthetic solutions on exposed dentin (Rirattanapong et al. 2013). When applied to the dentin surface of sensitive teeth, little of the
500 mmol/l potassium ion concentration found in potassium salt-containing dentifrices diffuses into the innervated inner dentin. ### 6.7

### Possible Links Between

### Potassium and Tubule

### Occlusion: Other Ions

In experiments that measured the diffusion of potassium ions or other solutes under conditions where there was an opposing transdentinal pressure gradient generating flow, partial tubule occlusion enhanced diffusion of solutes across dentin (Pashley and Matthews 1993; Pashley et al. 2002). Although both convective flow and diffusion are reduced by partial tubule occlusion, occlusion has a greater impact on flow, since flow is ∝ r4 (Eq. 5.2 in Chap. 5) as opposed to diffusion which is ∝ r2. Hence partial tubule occlusion in dentin with outward flow greatly reduces the opposing action of flow and has a modest blocking effect on diffusion. The net effect being an enhancement of diffusion.

Partial tubule occlusion has been examined as a potential method of enhancing dentifrice-­ derived potassium ion delivery to the inner dentin (Pashley et  al. 2002). There is limited clinical evidence supporting the idea of using partial

tubule occlusion as a means of enhancing the effectiveness of potassium-based dentifrices (Schiff et al. 2000; Sowinski et al. 2001). In a pilot clinical trial examining the combination of potassium salts with a polymer that reduces dentin permeability, use of the combination dentifrice exerted slightly greater desensitizing effect;
however, this was not statistically significant (Parkinson et al. 2017). Tubule-occluding materials can also release potassium ions. Potassium-­ releasing bioactive glasses (BAGs) have also been developed and subsequently may offer improved relief for DS in toothpaste formulations (Tiskaya et al. 2021).

The ionic composition of dentin fluid in intact teeth is not known. When the dentinal tubules are exposed and patent, there is an outward flow of dentin fluid that can be observed with sensitive tooth impression techniques (Kerdvongbundit et al. 2004). Since the flowing fluid in exposed sensitive tubules gets replaced by extracellular fluid derived from blood, it is reasoned to be like extracellular fluid elsewhere in the body being high in sodium and low in potassium. The application of oxalate to permeable dentin surfaces causes an immediate drop in dentin permeability when tested in vitro. In contrast, oxalate application to dentin surfaces in human teeth exhibiting sensitivity symptoms reduced pain over a period of approximately half an hour. This was observed using a variety of dentin stimuli including cold, a stimulus that the authors believed would still cause pain after tubule occlusion. The authors hypothesized that occluding the dentin tubules causes a change in the composition of the dentin tubule fluid in addition to reducing stimulus-­ induced dentin flow (Chinajitphan et al. 2021).
Following tubule occlusion, the ionic concentration of the dentin fluid would shift to concentrations found in intact teeth. The odontoblasts are the likely source of the dentin fluid in intact teeth.
If the dentin fluid has a potassium concentration exceeding typical extracellular fluid, this may maintain the action potential gates of the intradental nerves in an inactivated configuration-­ suppressing excitability.

### 6.8

### Pharmacological

### Approaches to Tooth

### Sensitivity Treatment

Pharmacological agents that bind to receptors or have other specific targets are effective at far lower concentrations (have greater potency) and have longer durations of action compared to the effects of potassium ions on intradental nerve activity which is dependent on maintaining high concentrations of potassium in the deep portions of the dentin. For example, the low concentrations of eugenol (0.1 mmol/l) that leach into the dentin fluid from zinc oxide-eugenol fillings have both analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects on the dental pulp (Kozam 1977; Hume 1984;
Markowitz et al. 1992). Eugenol has both excitatory and inhibitory actions on nerve cells and interacts with a variety of neuronal receptors (Park et al. 2006; Klein et al. 2014). These studies determining the mechanism of action for eugenol, an old drug, lead us to conclude that

more effective targeted treatment of DH and other forms of dental pain is achievable.

One innovative approach is to use the heat/ capsaicin receptor (TRPV1) ion channel as a pathway to allow a highly effective but normally impermeant cationic local anesthetic drug to cross cell membranes and block nociceptor activation. This treatment would be accomplished by the simultaneous administration of capsaicin and the cationic local anesthetic. Since many nociceptors (unfortunately not including the neurons responding to dentin stimulation (Närhi et  al.
1992a, b)) possess TRPV1 receptors, this approach would selectively target pain without the loss of non-pain sensation (Kim et al. 2010).

Identification of the receptors used by intradental nerves and odontoblast in transducing stimulation into nerve signals can provide targets for future research. As described in Chap. 5, piezo channels are important in mechanical force transduction in both odontoblasts and the intradental nerve endings. There are several drugs that act as antagonists to piezo including gadolinium (Ga3+ salts) (Savadipour et al. 2023). In addition, drugs that target the GABA and ATP ion channels associated with release of excitatory transmitters from odontoblasts or agents that block the actions of these transmitters on intradental nerves are attractive targets for pharmacological research (Nishiyama et  al. 2016). The drawback for a receptor-targeted pharmacological approach would be safety concerns arising from the drug’s unwanted actions on other tissues as well as the likely high cost.

Inflammation and inflammatory mediators sensitize the intradental nerves to pain-evoking dentin stimuli (Ngassapa et  al. 1992; Olgart
1996; Byers and Närhi 1999; Ajcharanukul et al.
2011). In acute inflammation triggered by tissue injury, prostaglandins sensitize nociceptors and enhance the actions of other mediators (Goodis et al. 2000). These cell membrane lipid-derived mediators contribute to the alterations in neuronal VGNa channels observed in inflammation (Rush and Waxman 2004).

### 6.9

### An Ecological Overview

It is evident that the goal of all DH treatments is to return the dentin and pulp to a state of health.
Starting with the work of Martin Addy and associates (Addy 1990, 1992), the link between tooth wear and sensitivity became clear (Markowitz and Pashley 2008). Hence in a manner not unlike the etiology of caries, the oral environment is critical to the origin and resolution of tooth sensitivity. Like caries, tooth wear and sensitivity are caused by loss of tooth mineral. Behavioral factors such as oral hygiene practices and diet help determine the oral environmental condition along with the saliva. Control of the etiological factors damaging the dentin and making it sensitive is the first step and an indispensable part of any treatment strategy (Chap. 8).

One way of conceptualizing a desensitizing agent that works with the oral environment would be a material that catalyzes and directs the forma-

tion of mineral on the dentin surface (Fig. 6.3).
Based on studies of the smear layer, David
Pashley recognized the importance of the dentin’s capacity to heal as key to both sensitivity treatment and restorative dentistry (Pashley
1984). For example, agents such as the amino acid arginine are metabolized by oral bacteria into alkaline compounds. This favors mineral formation and has been reported as having both anticaries and desensitizing properties (Cummins and Marsh 2018). As mentioned above, the application of the bioactive glasses such as NovaMin and BioMin F (toothpaste) will react with the Ca, phosphate, and fluoride ions in saliva and deposit a crystalline hydroxyl-carbonate apatite (HCA) or a protective fluorapatite layer, respectively, on the tooth surface. Both products have been shown to be effective in the treatment of DH and the remineralization of lost mineral from enamel (Chaps. 7, 9, and 10). The translation of in vitro results to the clinical arena is complex. Ideally, in vitro testing of agents which encourage mineral formation on the dentin surface would involve incubation of the dentin surface in sterile saliva or by use of an in situ approach. As will be discussed in other chapters in this book, the clinical evaluation of tooth sensitivity treatments is complex and presents unique difficulties in interpreting the outcomes from these studies to the consumer’s real-life experience in terms of quality of life (QoL) (Bekes and Hirsch 2013) (see
Chaps. 3 and 8).

### Research and Development

One of the problems in research generally is the validation of the results from in vitro, in situ, or animal studies. There is a danger of extrapolating these results into the clinical environment.
Invariably what promises to be an exciting new ingredient or product fails to materialize in either the lab or in the clinical environment which not only produces frustration and disappointment to researchers but may also have financial implications in terms of any future development. It is important to acknowledge that most of the his-

torical laboratory studies in DH were in part trying to identify the mechanism of action of the materials and products under test. For example, the action of potassium ions in toothpastes appeared to be successful in clinical practice, but in the laboratory, it was evident that the mechanism involved was not by tubular occlusion (Greenhill and Pashley 1981). Furthermore, it should be recognized that when evaluating desensitizing products currently on the market, the product formulations may not be the same as the original products that were evaluated as being clinically effective. For example, the original strontium chloride did not have fluoride in the formulation (due to interaction between strontium and fluoride within the toothpaste tube) and the abrasive was diatomaceous earth. Subsequent formulations used a silica-­based toothpaste (Gillam et al. 1992). The strontium source was also subsequently changed in certain products, to strontium acetate to accommodate fluoride in the toothpaste formulation (Karim and Gillam 2013).
Potassium-based toothpastes (and mouthrinses) originally contained the nitrate salt. Later, the product selection was expanded to include chloride and citrate compounds depending on the commercial market. More recently, stannous fluoride (SnF2) and hydroxyapatite-based products have become popular (Martins et al. 2020).

The problems of assessing and evaluating the efficacy of the various desensitizing toothpaste have been detailed elsewhere (Gillam et al. 2000), but it is evident that the assessment tools and stimuli used to evaluate the effectiveness in clinical studies do not relate to the types of stimuli and daily experiences of the consumer in day-to-­ day living. Perhaps this is an area for future research. The direction of future laboratory and clinical research has been addressed in Chaps. 7,
9, and 10.
