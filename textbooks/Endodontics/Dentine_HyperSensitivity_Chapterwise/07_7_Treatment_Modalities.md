### 7.1

### Brief Overview

### of the Chapter

There is evidence that patients might not know that dentine hypersensitivity (DH) is a preventable and treatable condition and might not seek professional assistance (Gillam et  al. 2013;
Exarchou et  al. 2019; Mosquim et  al. 2023).
Therefore, it is essential for the clinician to identify possible aetiological factors for the disease

and educate the patients regarding hygiene, dietary, and parafunctional deleterious habits that have been associated with DH development and/ or aggravation over time (Canadian Advisory
Board on Dentin Hypersensitivity 2003; Gillam et al. 2013) (see also Chaps. 4 and 8).
It is important to highlight that, without a definite diagnosis of DH, the clinician cannot successfully treat the condition. As previously defined, DH is essentially a diagnosis of exclusion, and this will clearly take the clinician some time to record the necessary information during the screening process and subsequent clinical examination (Canadian Advisory Board on
Dentin Hypersensitivity 2003). Following these steps is important because a treatment based on the prescription of products and procedures without the removal of the aetiological and causal factors that originated the problem in the first place will likely fail. Once the clinician is confident that the correct diagnosis of DH has been made, then the treatment of the condition can be undertaken (see also Chap. 8).
Nonetheless, the clinician who treats patients suffering from DH is often faced with a variety of products from different companies claiming to be effective against the symptoms of DH. According to Orchardson and Gillam (2006), the treatment of DH has been traditionally based on products or procedures (in-office and over-the-counter (OTC)) that may either block the tubules on the exposed dentine surface or desensitize the nerves in the pulp. ### 7.2

### Classification

### of Desensitizing Agents

To be both effective and acceptable to the patient and clinician, an ideal product for DH should have specific criteria, such as fast action, sustained efficacy, easy application, not dangerous to the pulp, not cause pain, and not stain the teeth.
To this day, a product that fulfils all these requirements is still not available, but knowing the characteristics of the patient as well as the mode of action of the products might help the clinician in the decision-making process of what desensitizing agent to use.

There have been several previous classifications published in the literature, some relatively simple, others more complex in nature. For instance, there is a simple classification of products into in-office and OTC products (Orchardson and Gillam 2006), but there is also a classification for in-office products, dividing them into those that (1) do not polymerise (varnishes/precipitants/ primers containing glutaraldehyde and HEMA), (2) undergo setting or polymerisation reactions (varnishes, conventional glass ionomer cements, or resin-modified  glass ionomers/compomers, adhesive resin primers, adhesive resin bonding systems) or whether these agents are used in conjunction with (3) mouthguards, (4) iontophoresis combined with fluoride pastes or solutions, or (5) lasers (Pashley 2000). However, clinicians appear uncertain not only about the most successful way

to treat DH but also express a level of dissatisfaction with the various products and techniques available (Cunha-Cruz et al. 2010). For this reason, the classification that gained most popularity is based on the mechanism of action of the desensitizing products (Miglani et al. 2010).

As addressed in Chap. 5, the most accepted theory to justify DH is the hydrodynamic theory, which assumes that the abrupt movement of the fluid within the dentinal tubules can stimulate the nerve cells within the dentinal tubules or in the pulp, which leads to the onset of pain. Based on that, there are basically two mechanisms to prevent this pain from being generated: (1) preventing the nerve cells from being stimulated (neural action) and (2) blocking the flow of fluid by occluding the patent dentinal tubules, which can be either by protein precipitation, mineral deposition onto dentine, or simply by mechanically covering the dentine with a restoration or periodontal tissue (see also Chaps. 5 and 6). It is important to note that an agent can also accommodate both mechanisms (for example, potassium oxalate-containing products). ### 7.4

### In-Office (Professionally

### Applied) Versus Over-the-­

### Counter (OTC) Products

After a successful diagnosis of DH and treatment of the aetiological factors associated with its development, patients with moderate to intense pain in a few teeth might benefit from a localized professional in-office application of a desensitizing agent. Due to its higher cost, this approach is normally conducted after several unsuccessful attempts to resolve the patient’s pain with OTC products. Nonetheless, one major benefit from in-office procedures is that it might present an immediate desensitizing effect, whereas OTC products might take 2–4  weeks to relief the symptoms of DH (Lynch et al. 2012). There are, however, a vast array of desensitizing products currently available which may make the choice of the most appropriate product both confusing and difficult for the clinician (Cunha-Cruz et al.
2010).

Nonetheless, individuals with mild to moderate local or generalized DH may seek products aiming to resolve the pain arising from DH without going to see a clinician. In the study of
Mosquim et al. (2023), 21.2% and 30.1% of the participants reported never and rarely scheduling an appointment with a dentist after experiencing and episode of DH, respectively. According to
Mantzourani and Sharma (2013), there are three main reasons that contribute to this behaviour:
(1) the need of a fast and urgent relief, (2) the easy availability of OTC products (also known as home-care products) in pharmacies and markets, and (3) their low cost, as this does not include the expenses of professional appointments.

Normally, OTC products are generally based on formulations with the same active ingredients as in-office products, yet the concentration of the active ingredients in OTC products is considerably lower than those used professionally. These
OTC products are available most commonly in the form of toothpastes or mouthrinses that are

sold without prescription, but their indications of use are limited, so patients should be advised to see a clinician if the symptoms last longer than
2–4 weeks. Also, it is likely that the clinician recommends the patient to associate an OTC product with an in-office procedure when the pain from DH is generalized and/or mild to moderate in severity (Miglani et al. 2010; Orchardson and
Gillam 2006; Karim and Gillam 2013; Talioti et  al. 2014; França et  al. 2015). Examples of
OTC products with different desensitizing ingredients are provided in Table 7.2, but it is worth mentioning that occluding agents that work by protein precipitation are available solely in professional in-office products.

### 7.5

### In-Office Fluoride

Application (Gels, Varnishes,

### and Resins)

High concentrated-fluoride gels, varnishes, and resins have been recommended for in-office treatment for DH (Orchardson and Gillam 2006;
Cummins 2010; Trushkowsky and Oquendo
2011), promoting instant and relative long-term relief for 3–6 months (Al-Sabbagh et al. 2009;
Pradeep et  al. 2012; Petersson 2013; Glockner
2013; Schmidlin and Sahrmann 2013). The combined use of fluoride with iontophoresis has also been reported in the literature (Aparna et  al.
2010; da Rosa et al. 2013; Das et al. 2023). The mechanism of this technique has been based on three different hypotheses: (1) reparative dentine formation; (2) paraesthesia by modifying the pain conduction mechanism; and (3) helps to transfer fluoride ions deep into dentine, which appears to prolong the effect of the desensitizing agent (da Rosa et al. 2013; Das et al. 2023). This effect has been proved in some clinical studies (Aparna et al. 2010; Das et al. 2023), but more long-term evidence still appears to be necessary.

The mode of action of conventional fluorides, such as NaF, is by precipitation of CaF2 globules that can occlude the dentinal tubule (Table 7.1).

Nevertheless, the efficacy of toothpastes with high fluoride concentrations, such as those containing 2800-5000 ppm F (e.g. Duraphat), has not been comprehensively investigated as desensitizers; although highly concentrated products have a recognized anti-caries effect and may be effective on preventing and controlling root caries (Walsh 2010;

The use of a fluoridated mouthrinse therapy in controlling DH in association with the regular

use of a desensitizing toothpaste may also be recommended (e.g., potassium and amine fluoride) (Yates et al. 2004; Pradeep et al. 2012; Glockner
2013; Petersson 2013), although currently there are limited data on their effectiveness (Petersson
2013). Other fluoridated mouthrinses incorporating the various desensitizing agents mentioned in this chapter may also enhance the effectiveness of fluoride toothpaste and perhaps favour the remineralisation of dentine (Glockner 2013).

Irrespective of the actual active agent, the effectiveness of OTC products is dependent on the individual (patient or consumer) maintaining regular and effective oral hygiene practices by applying the toothpastes and/or mouthrinse on a twice daily basis (Petersson 2013) without exerting over-zealous brushing that could potentialize
DH. However, depending on the nature of these
OTC products, its active component may take up to 2–4 weeks to achieve relief from DH (Talioti et al. 2014).

One of the problems in evaluating the claims of these desensitizing products in clinical studies is the heterogeneity of the published studies, which makes the results difficult to understand (Karim and Gillam 2013; Talioti et  al. 2014).
Furthermore, both placebo and non-placebo effects may also impact the results of these studies, although Lin et  al. (2013) and Bae et  al.
(2015), in their systematic reviews, reported that despite these observed effects both in-office and
OTC products, techniques, and treatment approaches may have a real effect on DH.  In addition, the systematic review of Bae et  al.
(2015) support the use of stannous fluoride with or without potassium-containing toothpastes for
DH treatment.

### 7.7

### Potassium Salts in In-Office

### Products

One of the most investigated agents to treat DH are those containing potassium salts, such as potassium oxalate (Wang et  al. 2016; Osmari et  al. 2018; Sgreccia et  al. 2020, 2022). The mechanism of action of this product is based on the presence of potassium and the formation of calcium oxalate. Potassium ions can prolong the duration of the repolarization stage of the nerve fibre, which is essential for a stimulus to occur (Nanjundasetty and Ashrafulla 2016). A study by
Peacock and Orchardson (1995) demonstrated that 8–16 mM concentrations of potassium ions around axons are necessary to sustain the nerve depolarization. However, there has been a degree of controversy because of its proposed mode of action (nerve desensitization) based on an animal

model (Markowitz et  al. 1991; Schmidlin and
Sahrmann 2013; Boneta et  al. 2013). Several investigators have, however, challenged this mode of action as indicated above and have suggested that these formulations may instead be through tubule occlusion and attributed to other constituents (e.g., abrasives or even the presence of fluoride salts) than the potassium ion per se (Addy and Mostafa 1989; Gillam et  al. 1996;
West et al. 1997; Orchardson and Gillam 2000).
Conversely, the oxalate is known to react with calcium and precipitate calcium oxalate, which can occlude the patent dentinal tubules and has been shown to resist a subsequent erosive challenge (Calabria et  al. 2014; Francisconi-Dos-­
Rios et al. 2021) (see also Chap. 6).

Formulations are launched in different vehicles to the dental profession (Fig. 7.1) (Sgreccia et al. 2020, 2022) or for OTC use (Orchardson and Gillam 2000; Lynch et al. 2018; Burnett et al.
2018; Anderson et  al. 2020; Chapman et  al.
2020), including solutions (Lynch et  al. 2018;
Burnett et al. 2018) and strips (Amini et al. 2016;
Lynch et  al. 2018; Chapman et  al. 2020).
Regardless of the vehicle, potassium oxalate shows reliable and long-lasting effect compared with other occluding products. Along the clinical effect, the self-reported quality of life (QoL) has also been investigated, in which the individuals demonstrate satisfaction once treated with potassium oxalate (Sgreccia et al. 2022). ### Calcium Sodium

### Phosphosilicate (Bioactive

### Glass) in In-Office Products

Calcium sodium phosphosilicate glasses (bioactive glasses) were developed by Professor Larry
Hench in the late 1960s, originally called Bioglass
45S5 (Hench 2006). The mechanism of action of this material was previously reported by Hench (2006) and further elaborated by Hill (1996) and is initiated with a rapid exchange of ions from the glass with protons from the solution in which it is immersed, followed by hydrolysis. As this ion exchange occurs, there is migration of Ca2+ and
PO4
3− to the surface of the glass and formation of an amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) film, which will further incorporate more Ca2+ and
PO4
3−, as well as OH−, CO3
2−, and even F− from the solution in which it is immersed (e.g., saliva), leading to the crystallization of the film, forming octacalcium phosphate (OCP) and/or different forms of apatite. It is important to note that ACP

and OCP are considered precursors of apatite (Brauer et al. 2010; Kovrlija et al. 2021), and it has been shown over the years that these glasses are able to precipitate different forms of apatite (hydroxyapatite, hydroxycarbonate apatite, and even fluorapatite) (Brauer et  al. 2010; Tiskaya et al. 2021), depending on their composition and on the composition of the solution in which they are immersed.

Despite also being known as “calcium sodium phosphosilicate”, these glasses were shown to be bioactive (i.e., precipitate apatite) even without sodium in their composition (Chen et al. 2017;
Sugumaran et al. 2022). Moreover, due to its ability to form apatite, which is the natural mineral component of teeth (both enamel and dentine) and bones, 45S5 has also been used for orthopaedic applications (Hench 2006); however, it was only in the 1990s that it started being used for dental applications under the name of NovaMin (NovaMin Technology Inc., Alachua, FL, USA, now Haleon), especially for the treatment of
DH. Based on the Brännström and Åström hydrodynamic theory (Brännström and Åström 1964) explaining the mechanisms involved in DH (see also Chap. 5), the bioactive glasses would be able to precipitate apatite on the tooth surface and help occlude the patent dentinal tubules, thus reducing the permeability of fluids that may lead to the pain sensation (Brauer et al. 2010; Mneimne et  al. 2011; Lynch et  al. 2012; Tiskaya et  al.
2021).

Based on this premise, several studies were developed over the years to investigate the behaviour of these glasses in situations simulating the oral environment (Brauer et al. 2010; Mneimne et  al. 2011; Lynch et  al. 2012; Tiskaya et  al.
2021). These studies evidenced that these glasses were able to increase the local pH (buffering effect), and a higher content of phosphate led to an increase in the amount of apatite being precipitated (Mneimne et al. 2011). Moreover, due to the understanding that fluorapatite (FAp) is more acid-resistant than conventional hydroxyapatite (HAp) and hydroxycarbonate apatite (HCAp) (Tiskaya et al. 2021), fluoride-­containing glasses such as Biomin F (Biomin Technologies

### 7.17

### Dentine Bonding Agents/

### Adhesives and Adhesive

### Restorations

Adhesive systems have proven to be valuable tools in the reduction of DH, providing effective solutions for patients experiencing discomfort.
The use of adhesive systems for DH involves various techniques and materials designed to reduce pain and reinforce the integrity of compromised structure (Abuzinadah and Alhaddad
2021).

In adhesion evolution, previous adhesive systems relied on the application of phosphoric acid to facilitate the interlocking of the resin material with the dental surface. However, the necessity for acidic dentine conditioning prior to the adhesive application may be detrimental to the integrity of the pulp. The advent of self-etching and universal or multi-mode agents has revitalized the interest in employing adhesive systems as dentine desensitizers. This was attributed to their ease of application and less aggressiveness to the dentine–pulp complex when compared to traditional etch-and-rinse systems (Pei et  al. 2013;
Abuzinadah and Alhaddad 2021). These systems, incorporating 10-methacryloyloxydecyl dihydrogen phosphate (10-MDP) and/or other functional monomers, establish a chemical bond with calcium from the apatite, leading to the formation of a stable salt (Yoshida et al. 2000; Pei et al. 2013).
This introduces an additional chemical bond to enhance the micromechanical bond promoted by conventional systems, presenting an advantageous approach for patients with DH, especially for inducing less postoperative sensitivity (Costa et al. 2017).

One common application of adhesive systems is as a dentine surface sealant, which involves the application of a thin layer of adhesive material directly onto the exposed dentine. The adhesive forms a protective barrier, sealing the dentinal tubules and reducing the transmission of external

stimuli that can trigger DH (Wegehaupt et  al.
2012a, b; Abuzinadah and Alhaddad 2021). This technique not only provides immediate relief but also helps prevent further enamel erosion (Wegehaupt et al. 2017). Whilst there have been promising results, the enduring effectiveness of adhesive systems as dentine desensitizers remains uncertain. Potential drawbacks are linked to durability and mechanical challenges, as studies on this subject report treatment durability ranging from 3 to 12 months (Wegehaupt et al. 2012a, b; Abuzinadah and Alhaddad 2021). Moreover, the absence of comprehensive clinical studies on this matter exacerbates the uncertainty regarding their long-term efficacy. Another concern with certain products was the absence of a filler material, which could potentially increase susceptibility to wear on the material’s surface (Schmidlin and Sahrmann 2013).

Considering postoperative sensitivity and substrate resistance to new insults in the oral environment, the use of glutaraldehyde-based agents, applied either before the use of the adhesive system or incorporated directly into the system itself, has been successfully reported in the treatment of DH (Albar 2022). This product’s efficacy relies on the coagulation of proteins within the dentinal tubules, resulting in the creation of a precipitate of serum albumin that seals off these tubules. This phenomenon is commonly linked to
2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA), whose polymerization is initiated by the precipitation of serum albumins, thereby aiding in the closure of dentinal tubules (Jiang et  al. 2022). Regarding adhesives containing glutaraldehyde and HEMA, numerous researchers have documented its effect on dentine permeability and both immediate and sustained alleviation of pain resulting from DH (Lopes and Aranha 2013; Albar 2022; Mosquim et al. 2022a). Special attention, however, should be paid to the clinical use of HEMA-based products, particularly when applied in proximity or in direct contact with pulpal and epithelial gingival tissues. The chemical bonding between the material and substrate is primarily driven by ionic forces, where the carboxylic groups in the material replace phosphate (PO4
3−) ions and form ionic bonds with calcium (Ca2+) ions present in the hydroxyapatite (Yoshida et al.
2000; Fukuda et  al. 2003). For decades, glass ionomer-based materials were the main option due to their ability to conciliate both good bonding ability to hypermineralized tissues and ideal elastic modulus (Zabeu et al., 2022; Boing et al.
2018). However, due to the high surface roughness and colour alteration over time (Boing et al.
2018), coupled with the emergence of new resinous materials, the use of ionomeric materials has experienced a decline in their recommendation.
Numerous researchers still recommend the use of
GICs in conjunction with periodontal procedures, such as root coverage techniques. It has also been reported that the application of GICs may enhance the attachment of periodontal fibres and

facilitate the release of fluoride (Francisconi et al.
2009).

Resin materials are often chosen for the restoration of non-carious lesions due to their versatility, aesthetic appeal, and ability to bond effectively to the tooth structure, providing a durable and natural-looking solution (Fig. 7.4a, b). Also, monomeric materials offer more resistance compared to glass ionomers upon toothbrushing forces for abrasion lesions and resist more than GICs during acidic erosive episodes (Peumans et al. 2020). Despite the evolution of restorative materials, the restorative approach in these cases remains challenging, requiring further clinical studies to assess the longevity and marginal integrity of the tooth.

Based on these parameters, different mechanisms can be involved in the laser treatment for

The low-­ power laser therapy has been suggested to have anti-inflammatory effects by stimulating cell activity, potentially influencing the inflammatory response, and diminishing the neural response to a hypersensitive stimulus (Kong et al. 2020; Pion et al. 2023). Furthermore, this type of laser can interact with the dental pulp, leading to an increase in odontoblastic activity and, consequently, to formation of a microscopically altered surface layer, which could act as a barrier to external stimuli, reducing the transmission of sensory signals to the nerve cells in the pulp, thereby alleviating pain associated with DH (Sgolastra et al. 2013; Kong et al. 2020).

Alternatively, the most common category of high-power laser for treating DH is the erbium family of lasers, which includes the erbium:yttrium–aluminium–garnet (Er:YAG), erbium, chromium:yttrium–scandium–gallium– garnet (Er,Cr:YSGG), and carbon dioxide (CO2) laser. These lasers operate in the infrared spectrum, and their wavelengths are well-absorbed by water and hydroxyapatite, the primary mineral component of tooth enamel and dentine (Pion et al. 2023). The Nd:YAG laser, with power density parameters ranging from 0.3 to 2 W, has been reported to reduce discomfort immediately after application (Al-Sabbagh et al. 2009; Lopes and
Aranha 2013) and exhibits an additional analgesic effect compared to other high-power lasers (Lin et al. 2013). Despite the limited number of studies, the Er,Cr:YSGG laser, with an output power varying from 0.25 to 0.5  W, has been shown to be effective in reducing DH when compared to placebo (Aranha and Eduardo 2012;
Kong et al. 2020).

The GaAlAs diode laser, with a wavelength ranging from 780  nm (low power) to 980  nm (high power), stands as one of the most frequently employed lasers for DH treatment due to its cost-­ effectiveness and compact design. Despite demonstrating satisfactory results in both in vitro and clinical studies when used in high-power mode

(Umberto et al. 2012; Rizzante et al. 2016), low-­ power utilization of the diode laser has garnered a greater number of promising outcomes in the published literature (Tolentino et al. 2022; Pion et al. 2023). With protocols ranging from 1 to 3 applications with 15 mW to 1 W of power and the combined use of desensitizing agents, the diode laser has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing pain after 3 and 6  months (Bal et  al. 2015;
Tolentino et al. 2022; Pion et al. 2023).

The long-term effectiveness of lasers compared to the other types of treatment approaches has also been favourably reported (Lopes and
Aranha 2013; Tolentino et al. 2022; Pion et al.
2023). Furthermore, according to Pion et  al.
(2023), high-power lasers showed lower average pain scores after 3 months of treatment compared to low-power lasers on pretreatment pain (baseline); however, no significant differences were observed between the groups. Given the similar outcomes promoted by different types of lasers, low-power options, such as diode lasers, appear to be more cost-effective for clinicians. However, currently there is a lack of cost-effectiveness assessments, and additional long-term studies are required (Pion et al. 2023).

### 7.20

### Periodontal Plastic Surgery

### (Root Coverage Procedures)

Recession of the marginal tissues (gingival recession) has been reported to be associated with DH because of the exposure of the root surface to the oral environment. Several factors have been associated with gingival recession, such as anatomic characteristics, restorative procedures, habits, smoking, and even with the natural ageing process (Kassab and Cohen 2003; Chambrone and
Tatakis 2016; Massaro et al. 2018). According to a recent systematic review, more than three-­ fourths of the worldwide population appear to present some level of gingival recession (Yadav et al. 2023).

Root coverage procedures associated with root scaling and the covering of the exposed root through surgical periodontal procedures have been reported to reduce or completely resolve
DH over time (Orchardson and Gillam 2006; Lin and Gillam 2012; Kotsailidi et  al. 2023).

Nevertheless, despite promising results of root coverage procedures against the DH symptoms, the clinician should be aware that a thin periodontal biotype is more frequently affected by gingival recession and may tear during both probing and surgical intervention, making the root coverage a more challenging procedure than in a thicker biotype, in which higher mean gain or complete root coverage has been observed (Müller et al. 2000; Hwang and Wang 2006; Kim et al. 2020). Therefore, it is necessary for the clinician to be trained and to consider not only the root coverage aspect but also the resultant aesthetics together with the treatment of DH whilst bearing in mind that the aetiological factors that led to the root exposure must also be addressed (Ahmedbeyli et  al. 2014; Cairo et  al. 2014;
Polack and Mahn 2013; West et al. 2013).

Three different gingival biotypes have been described in the published literature: (1) thin scalloped, (2) thick flat, and (3) thick scalloped (Zweers et al. 2014). Teeth in the thin scalloped category have the characteristics of a thin gingival tissue with a more pronounced contour of the gingival margin, with a greater height of the interproximal papilla and with an underlying thin buccal bone that can present fenestrations. The thick flat biotype category is characterized by thicker bone tissue surrounding the teeth and covered by a thick and smooth contour gingival tissue, which results in a small height of the papilla. The thick scalloped category displays the characteristics of the two other patterns (Zweers et al. 2014).

### 7.21.1	 Protease Inhibitors

Host-derived proteolytic enzymes (also known as proteases), such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and cysteine cathepsins (CCs) were advocated to contribute to ETW progression since they hydrolyse the demineralized organic matrix (DOM) remaining over the dentine surface when enamel is irreversibly lost (Tjäderhane et al. 2013, 2015). The use of protease inhibitors is thus currently evaluated for the prevention or arrest of the DOM degradation, ETW and, supposedly, DH (see also Chap. 4). Chlorhexidine (CHX) (Magalhães et al. 2009; Kato et al. 2010a;
Vertuan et al. 2021), iron sulphate (FeSO4) (Kato et  al. 2010b), epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) (Magalhães et al. 2009; Kato et al. 2010a; Silveira et al. 2014), sodium fluoride (NaF) (Kato et al.

2010a, 2014; Silveira et al. 2014), anacardic acid (Silveira et  al. 2014), proanthocyanidin (PA) (Boteon et al. 2017; Vertuan et al. 2021), quercetin (Jiang et  al. 2020; Hong et  al. 2022),
1,10-phenanthroline, trans-epoxysuccinyl-L-­ leucylamido-[4-guanidino] butane (E-64), and even combined quercetin and E64 (Yang et  al.
2023) have been investigated.

Most MMP inhibitors act because of their ability to chelate metal ions, such as zinc in
MMPs active site (Wojtowicz-Praga et al. 1997), and most CCs inhibitors work by binding to CCs’ highly reactive thiol group through a thioether covalent bond (Yamamoto et  al. 1991). Some protease inhibitors are capable of inhibiting both
MMPs and CCs (Scaffa et al. 2012); furthermore, it is also speculated that CCs inhibition can also reduce MMPs activity as the CCs themselves are also believed to activate MMPs (Bafail et  al.
2020). Some inhibitors still have a collagen crosslinking effect and could maybe enhance the mechanical properties of demineralized dentine (Epasinghe et al. 2014; Hong et al. 2022).

Several reviews are categorical in stating that the use of protease inhibitors leads to a significantly thicker DOM and to a reduced substance loss in the case of dentine erosive wear (Buzalaf et al. 2012, 2014, 2015; Tjäderhane et al. 2015).
Nonetheless, further evidence, and especially that derived from randomized controlled clinical trials, are needed to endorse the use of such inhibitors for tooth wear management (Yang et al. 2023).

Regarding their effect in DH, it has been inferred that some protease inhibitors could also act as traditional desensitizers (Sales-Peres et al.
2011; Calabria et al. 2014), or they could be used in association with these desensitizers (Bhavan
Ram et al. 2023). The studies evaluating the role of protease inhibitors on treating DH, or mainly on in  vitro dentine permeability, are, however, scarce. ### 7.22

### Conclusions

Given this wide range of agents and procedures used to treat DH, it would be unreasonable for the clinician to expect that one product would resolve every aspect of the problem or that these treatment strategies would be appropriate for every individual. In general, depending on the desensitizing agent used to treat the problem and on the most painful form of stimulus (e.g., air blast, tactile, or cold), in-office treatments are successful in treating DH in patients with a localized severe problem in one or more teeth. Conversely, OTC products are recommended for mild to moderate pain or generalized DH and they usually demonstrate a reduction in DH over time. The main advantage of using OTC products is that they are easily available and more affordable than in-­

office products. Conversely, longer periods of time are required for the remission of symptoms (2–4 weeks), whilst in-office products might promote instant DH relief (see also Chap. 8).

Despite comparing the outcomes of studies on
DH agents being problematic due to their high heterogeneity, reasonable evidence is available to guide the clinician on the decision-making process of choosing the most appropriate agent for each case. Additionally, evidence is also available to help the clinician to effectively diagnose
DH, control the aetiological factors associated with the development of the condition, and monitor the patient over time. Nonetheless, it cannot be overstressed that following these steps still is the key condition that needs to be addressed to achieve a successful and long-lasting result.
