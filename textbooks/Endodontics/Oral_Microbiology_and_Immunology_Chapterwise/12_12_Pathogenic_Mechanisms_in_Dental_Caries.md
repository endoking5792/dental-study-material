INTRODUCTION
Dental car­ies is a dietobacterial dis­ease of the hu­man den­ti­tion that in­volves com­plex in­ter­ac­tions be­tween oral mi­cro­or­gan­isms (and their se­creted prod­ucts), sal­i­vary con­stit­u­ents, and di­e­tary car­bo­hy­drates on a tooth sur­ face (see Chap­ter 11). The hu­man mouth is a di­verse en­vi­ron­ment that con­ tains a com­plex con­sor­tium of bac­te­ria, fungi, and vi­ruses that is gen­er­ally re­ferred as “the oral microbiome.” However, there are sig­nif­i­cant var­i­a­ tions be­tween in­di­vid­u­als and their microbiome com­po­si­tion as well as the lo­ca­tion and path­o­genic po­ten­tial of the oral microbiome. Therefore, it is rather chal­leng­ing to de­fine what rep­re­sents a healthy or dis­eased oral mi­ crobiome by sim­ply pro­fil­ing the mem­bers of a given mi­cro­bial com­mu­nity.
Current re­search in the mi­cro­bi­ol­ogy of den­tal car­ies has fo­cused on un­ der­stand­ing the mech­a­nisms by which the ac­qui­si­tion and ac­cu­mu­la­tion of path­o­gens mod­u­late the eco­log­i­cal changes in the lo­cal microbiota that leads to the de­vel­op­ment of a car­i­o­genic biofilm. Environmental changes, such as fre­quent ex­po­sure to fer­ment­able car­bo­hy­drates, trig­ger path­o­gens to emerge, re­sult­ing in a shift in the met­a­bolic bal­ance and in­creas­ing the vir­u­lence of the plaque com­mu­nity. This eco­log­i­cal con­cept is ad­van­ta­geous as it con­sid­ers all­ of the or­gan­isms in the mouth, as well as the host diet, along with the over­all ac­tiv­ity of the plaque microbiota and its path­o­genic ef­fects.

The tooth sur­faces are coated with a pro­tein­a­ceous film known as ac­ quired enamel pel­li­cle, which is de­rived from mam­ma­lian and mi­cro­bial sources, such as sal­i­vary gly­co­pro­teins and bac­te­rial exo­en­zymes and po­ly­sac­cha­rides. Within the oral microbiome, cer­tain bac­te­ria (e.g., mitis group strep­to­cocci and Actinomyces spp.) are known to ad­here to pel­li­clecoated sur­faces via com­bi­na­tions of hy­dro­pho­bic or elec­tro­static forces (low af­fin­ity and non­spe­cific) and highly spe­cific ad­he­sin-receptor in­ter­ ac­tions (also see Chap­ter 5). Cariogenic or­gan­isms can also be pres­ent in this ini­tial com­mu­nity, al­beit usu­ally in low num­bers, de­pend­ing to a large ex­tent on the host di­et.

The dif­fer­ent mi­cro­bial spe­cies in­hab­it­ing the oral cav­ity phys­i­cally and met­a­bol­i­cally in­ter­act with each other. Some of these in­ter­ac­tions are ben­e­fi­ cial to the host via an­tag­o­nis­tic or com­pet­i­tive mech­a­nisms with car­i­o­genic

The dy­ namic bal­ance be­tween com­men­sals and path­o­gens can be dis­rupted by the host diet, pro­mot­ing the for­ma­tion of bio­films on teeth that are es­sen­tial for the tran­si­tion from a healthy to a car­i­o­genic state, and the on­set and pro­gres­sion of den­tal car­ies.
The de­vel­op­ment of car­i­o­genic bio­films is a dy­namic and mul­ti­fac­to­rial pro­cess trig­gered by the fre­quent avail­abil­ity of sug­ars in the diet and their uti­li­za­tion by oral bac­te­ria (Fig. 1). Cariogenic bac­te­ria such as Strepto­ coccus mutans and Streptococcus sobrinus (herein des­ig­nated mutans group strep­to­cocci) can uti­lize sug­ars to pro­duce both ex­tra­cel­lu­lar po­ly­sac­cha­ rides and or­ganic ac­ids, thus form­ing highly ad­he­sive bio­films. If biofilms are al­lowed to form and per­sist, the acid­i­fi­ca­tion brought about by bac­te­ria en­meshed within the biofilm ma­trix re­sults in lo­cal­ized eco­log­i­cal changes and the de­vel­op­ment of mi­cro­en­vi­ron­ments that fa­cil­i­tate the pro­lif­er­a­tion of car­i­o­genic spe­cies. Moreover, the ex­tra­cel­lu­lar po­ly­meric sub­stance (EPS) pres­ent in the ma­trix of­fers bind­ing sites for ad­di­tional or­gan­isms while the aciduric and acidogenic microbiotas pros­per within the acidic mi­lieu, thus greatly mod­i­fy­ing the biofilm struc­ture and mi­cro­bial com­plex­i­ty.
The cre­a­tion of acidic mi­cro­en­vi­ron­ments, de­lin­eated by a dif­fu­sionlimiting ma­trix, ben­e­fits not only ac­id-tolerant spe­cies but also those that use lac­tate as a car­bon source (e.g., Veillonella), whereas other spe­cies that are acid sen­si­tive or that can­not me­tab­o­lize the ac­ids pres­ent in their sur­ round­ings may per­ish. At the same time, other bac­te­rial spe­cies com­pete against the car­i­o­genic bac­te­ria by se­cret­ing in­hib­i­tory amounts of hy­dro­gen per­ox­ide (H2O2) (e.g., Streptococcus gordonii) or coun­ter­ing the del­e­te­ri­ous ef­fects of acid­i­fi­ca­tion by gen­er­at­ing al­kali that neu­tral­izes the en­vi­ron­men­ tal pH (e.g., Streptococcus salivarius). However, if the acid­i­fied mi­cro­ en­vi­ron­ment per­sists, the mi­cro­bial di­ver­sity is fur­ther re­duced in fa­vor of the more po­tent ac­id-tolerant strains.
The per­sis­tent acid­i­fi­ca­tion of the biofilm even­tu­ally leads to acid dis­ so­lu­tion of the min­er­al­ized enamel. The enamel sur­face can be­come per­ ma­nently dam­aged (char­ac­ter­ized by white spots due to de­min­er­al­iza­tion), and the un­der­ly­ing den­tin is then at risk of be­ing ex­posed, re­sult­ing in cav­i­ta­tion, a late man­i­fes­ta­tion of the dis­ease that re­quires costly in­ter­ven­ tion and res­to­ra­tion. Although the im­me­di­ate cause of enamel dis­so­lu­tion is acid pro­duc­tion, the ab­sence of the “shel­ter­ing” ef­fect of the biofilm EPS would min­i­mize the abil­ity of acid to de­min­er­al­ize enamel in the pres­ence of buff­er­ing sa­liva. Thus, the for­ma­tion of an EPS-rich ma­trix and acid­i­fi­ ca­tion of the mi­lieu com­bined with the pathogens’ abil­ity to sur­vive a va­ ri­ety of en­vi­ron­men­tal stresses are con­sid­ered the ma­jor vir­u­lence fac­tors mod­u­lat­ing the de­vel­op­ment of car­i­o­genic bio­films.
The re­sults of de­cades of in­ves­ti­ga­tions into the caus­a­tive agents of den­tal car­ies have shown con­clu­sively, though not ex­clu­sively, that the mu­ tans group strep­to­cocci are closely as­so­ci­ated with the dis­ease, as they are uniquely adapted to uti­lize di­e­tary sug­ars and live in den­tal plaque formed on the sur­faces of teeth. Characterization of the mi­cro­bial com­po­si­tion of den­tal plaque sam­ples, ini­tially by stan­dard mi­cro­bi­o­log­i­cal ap­proaches and more re­cently by deep se­quenc­ing ap­proaches, has con­sis­tently shown the pres­ence of mutans strep­to­cocci in car­ies le­sions, al­though with var­i­ able lev­els and not nec­es­sar­ily in great­est nu­mer­i­cal abun­dance. In the oral cav­ity, the polymicrobial in­ter­ac­tions start with early col­o­niz­ers that can rap­idly ad­here to the tooth sur­face and then coadhere with other mi­cro­or­gan­isms. During this pro­cess, the var­i­ous spe­cies in­ter­act phys­i­cally and met­a­bol­i­cally to shape the ini­tial biofilm com­mu­nity. The in­ter­ac­tions can be both an­tag­o­nis­tic and co­op­er­a­tive, which can dy­nam­i­cally change de­pend­ing on the host. Certain in­ter­ac­tions are ben­e­fi­cial to the host; for example, S. gordonii/S. salivarius and A. naeslundii in­ter­fere with car­ies path­o­gens such as S. mutans via pro­duc­tion of al­kali, bac­te­rio­cins, and hy­dro­gen per­ox­ide (H2O2), which act as chem­i­cal weap­ons. However, the bal­ance be­tween com­men­sals and path­o­gens can be dis­rupted by fre­quent sugar con­sump­tion and poor oral care. A diet rich in sugar, par­tic­u­larly su­crose, pro­vi­des sub­strate for the pro­duc­tion of ex­tra­cel­lu­lar glu­ cans by S. mutans-derived glucosyltransferases (Gtfs). Gtfs are pres­ent in the pel­li­cle and also bound to dif­fer­ent mi­crobes (in­clud­ing C.
albicans) pro­duc­ing large amounts of glu­cans. The exopolysaccharides en­hance mi­cro­bial ad­he­sion-cohesion and ac­cu­mu­la­tion on the tooth sur­face, while form­ing a po­ly­meric ma­trix that em­beds the cells. The ma­trix pro­vi­des a mul­ti­func­tional scaf­fold for struc­tured or­ga­ni­za­tion and sta­bil­ity of the biofilm mi­cro­bial com­mu­nity. If the biofilm re­mains on teeth and the con­sump­tion of car­bo­hy­draterich di­ets persists, the amount of ex­tra­cel­lu­lar po­ly­meric sub­stance (EPS) and the ex­tent of acid­i­fi­ca­tion of the ma­trix in­crease. The dif­fu­sion-modifying prop­er­ties of the ma­trix com­bined with the met­a­bolic ac­tiv­i­ties of em­bed­ded or­gan­isms help cre­ate highly lo­cal­ ized and acidic pH mi­cro­en­vi­ron­ments. Such con­di­tions elicit eco­log­i­cal and struc­tural changes that fa­vor the sur­vival and dom­i­nance of highly acid stress-tolerant or­gan­isms that can synergize with each other. The mi­cro­bial di­ver­sity can fur­ther re­duce in fa­vor of an aci­ duric microbiota, help­ing to main­tain an acid­i­fied mi­cro­en­vi­ron­ment. The low-pH con­di­tion at the tooth-biofilm in­ter­face pro­motes de­min­er­al­iza­tion of the enamel, lead­ing to the on­set and pro­gres­sion of den­tal car­ies. This model may ex­plain the rapid ac­cu­mu­la­ tion of car­i­o­genic plaque in the pres­ence of su­crose (and other fer­ment­able sug­ars).

Recent ef­forts to dis­sect the oral microbiome us­ing high-throughput
DNA se­quenc­ing tech­nol­o­gies (in­clud­ing metagenomics anal­y­sis) have re­ vealed the pres­ence of nu­mer­ous bac­te­rial spe­cies at the var­i­ous stages of the car­ies pro­cess. The role of most of these or­gan­isms in dis­ease, if any, re­mains un­clear. However, S. mutans is not al­ways iden­ti­fied in car­i­ous le­ sions, and metagenomic se­quenc­ing re­veals sub­stan­tial mi­cro­bial di­ver­sity even in ad­vanced car­ies, in­di­cat­ing the ne­ces­sity for ex­pand­ing our knowl­ edge of the role of non-mutans group bac­te­ria in car­ies path­o­gen­e­sis. In ad­di­tion to the pres­ence (or ab­sence) of mutans group strep­to­cocci, sev­ eral phyla have been iden­ti­fied that con­tain or­gan­isms with met­a­bolic traits sug­ges­tive of cariogens. These in­clude oral Lactobacillus spe­cies, bifido­ bacteria, Actinomyces, and yeasts, all­ of which ex­hibit acidogenic traits, or the abil­ity to in­ter­act with S. mutans and sur­vive in the mi­cro­bial com­ mu­nity that re­sides in car­i­o­genic bio­films.
The bifidobacteria group in­cludes Bifidobacterium dentium, Scardo­ via wiggsiae, Parascardovia denticolens, and Alloscardovia omnicolens, many of which have been de­tected in plaque and are well known for their acidogenicity. These or­gan­isms show a strong pos­i­tive cor­re­la­tion with the pres­ence of mutans group strep­to­cocci, lac­to­ba­cilli, and yeasts, as well as plaque in­dex. While not show­ing cau­sa­tion of car­ies di­rectly, there is a strong case for the oral bifidobacteria as car­ies-associated or­gan­isms, re­ sid­ing in car­i­o­genic bio­films or car­i­ous le­sions ini­ti­ated by other bac­te­ria, and func­tion­ing as part of the car­i­o­genic com­mu­nity. Likewise, oral lac­to­ ba­cilli, which in­clude Lactobacillus fermentum, L. rhamnosus, L. gasseri,
L. casei, L. paracasei, L. salivarius, and L. plantarum, have been found in the plaque of car­ies-active in­di­vid­u­als but not in sam­ples from car­ies-free sub­jects, in­di­cat­ing an ac­tive role in the dis­ease pro­cess. Lactobacilli are acid pro­duc­ers and aciduric but have low bind­ing af­fin­ity to tooth sur­faces, sug­ gest­ing that these or­gan­isms may oc­cupy re­ten­tive phys­i­cal niches cre­ ated by ac­id-mediated de­min­er­al­iza­tion of teeth caused by mutans group strep­to­cocci, or the nat­u­ral an­a­tom­i­cal fea­tures of the tooth (e.g., pits and fis­sures). Actinomyces spp. have been as­so­ci­ated with root car­ies, al­ though they are less acid tol­er­ant and can be ben­e­fi­cial to the host as al­ ka­li-generating bac­te­ria. The role of yeast in the path­o­gen­e­sis of den­tal car­ies re­mains un­clear de­spite its acidogenic/aciduric po­ten­tial, al­though re­cent stud­ies have shown that Candida albicans can de­velop a syn­er­gis­tic in­ter­ac­tion with S. mutans to en­hance biofilm cariogenicity (see be­low).
Altogether, the par­tic­i­pa­tion of or­gan­isms in car­ies ini­ti­a­tion and pro­ gres­sion must be con­sid­ered in the con­text of their abil­ity to ad­here to teeth, form a ma­trix, pro­duce acid, and sur­vive in the sub­se­quent low-pH en­vi­ ron­ment. It is clear that the mutans group strep­to­cocci are im­por­tant and pos­sess a num­ber of vir­u­lence fac­tors that con­trib­ute to dis­ease (Table 1).

There are three key fea­tures of S. mutans phys­i­ol­ogy that ex­plain its prom­i­nent role in the de­vel­op­ment of den­tal car­ies (Fig. 1). The first is that
S. mutans read­ily me­tab­o­lizes di­e­tary su­crose to form in­sol­u­ble glu­cose po­ly­mers (glu­cans). These po­ly­mers aid in the per­ma­nent col­o­ni­za­tion of hard sur­faces and in the de­vel­op­ment of the EPS ma­trix in si­tu, lead­ing to the for­ma­tion of highly ad­he­sive and co­he­sive bio­films. The sec­ond fea­ ture is that S. mutans can ef­fi­ciently trans­port and me­tab­o­lize a large num­ ber of dif­fer­ent sug­ars into ac­ids. Finally, this bac­te­rium can sur­vive at acidic pH val­ues that are le­thal to most other bac­te­rial spe­cies pres­ent in the mouth by ac­ti­vat­ing a num­ber of ge­netic and phys­i­o­logic re­sponses known as the acid tol­er­ance re­sponse (ATR), which is dis­cussed in more de­tail be­ low. In ad­di­tion to these fea­tures, the re­peat­edly high cor­re­la­tion of S. mu­ tans with den­tal car­ies in clin­i­cal and ep­i­de­mi­o­log­i­cal stud­ies and its abil­ity to cause the dis­ease in an­i­mal mod­els have earned for S. mutans a rep­u­ta­tion as a model oral path­o­gen and an or­ches­tra­tor (within the com­plex oral mi­ cro­bial com­mu­nity) of car­i­o­genic biofilm de­vel­op­ment.
If bac­te­ria are not phys­i­cally re­moved from the tooth sur­face, a car­i­ o­genic plaque will be as­sem­bled. This in­volves ac­cu­mu­la­tion of EPS en­ cas­ing mi­cro­bial cells and the in­ter­ac­tion of those cells, by phys­i­cal prox­im­ity and the in­ter­play of met­a­bolic by-prod­ucts. In the pres­ence of high lev­els of sugar, the pre­dom­i­nant acid pro­duced by oral strep­to­cocci is lac­tic acid, which read­ily dis­so­ci­ates into a pro­ton and its con­ju­gate base. The free pro­ton rap­idly acid­i­fies its sur­round­ing mi­lieu, re­sult­ing in the se­lec­tion of a more aciduric microbiota (Fig. 1).

Exopolysaccharides and Biofilm Matrix
As in­di­cated above, the ma­jor EPS com­po­nents in car­i­o­genic biofilm ma­ trix are glu­cans, po­ly­sac­cha­rides com­prised of glu­cose moi­e­ties linked pri­mar­ily by α-1,3- and α-1,6-glucosyl linkages, al­though ad­di­tional po­ ly­sac­cha­rides such as fructans are also found in the ex­tra­cel­lu­lar ma­trix.
Glucans are chiefly pro­duced from su­crose and starch hydrolysates by exo­ en­zymes called glucosyltransferases (Gtfs). These po­ly­sac­cha­rides pro­vide an abun­dance of new bind­ing sites that can pro­mote lo­cal col­o­ni­za­tion of S.
mutans (through sev­eral cell mem­brane-associated glu­can-binding pro­teins) and other mi­cro­or­gan­isms, while form­ing the core of the ma­trix-scaffold in car­i­o­genic bio­films. However, biofilm ma­tri­ces har­bor other bio­mol­e­cules such as pro­teins and ex­tra­cel­lu­lar DNA (eDNA), which is de­rived from the dis­rup­tion of dy­ing bac­te­ria within the biofilm or through se­cre­tion us­ing an exosome-like pro­cess. These mac­ro­mol­e­cules also play an im­por­tant role in the biofilm struc­ture, al­though they are less influ­enced by the di­et.
While nu­mer­ous and dis­tinct mi­cro­bial spe­cies are found in plaque bio­ films, most of them do not con­trib­ute to the syn­the­sis of in­sol­u­ble po­ly­sac­ cha­rides un­til they are coated by se­creted Gtfs. In ad­di­tion, the ma­trix it­self may also serve as a lo­cal sugar res­er­voir. On the other hand, in­sol­u­ble glu­cans can­not be de­graded, as there are no en­ zymes in the biofilm break­ing the α-1,3-glucosyl linkages, and hence these are es­pe­cially im­por­tant for the scaffolding of the biofilm structure. As a re­sult, the sug­ar-based met­a­bolic ac­tiv­ity of densely packed bac­te­ria within an in­creas­ingly in­sol­u­ble EPS ma­trix pro­duces co­pi­ous amounts of ac­ids that are re­tained lo­cally. Thus, it is the com­bi­na­tion of phys­i­cal and met­a­ bolic fac­tors that ex­plains the cre­a­tion of acidic niches at the biofilm-tooth in­ter­face.
The de­vel­op­ment of highly struc­tured and lo­cal­ized acidic mi­cro­ en­vi­ron­ments has ad­di­tional bi­o­log­i­cal rel­e­vance. Niches dis­play­ing low pH val­ues ac­ti­vate the ATR of bac­te­ria clus­tered within these niches. At the same time, tran­scrip­tion and ac­tiv­ity of S. mutans GtfB and GtfC are el­e­vated un­der acidic pH, lead­ing to con­tin­u­ous biofilm ac­cre­tion via glu­ can pro­duc­tion.

Sugar Transport and Acid Production
The pro­cesses of ad­her­ence and ac­cu­mu­la­tion would not lead to tooth de­cay with­out­ ef­fec­tive sugar trans­port and pro­duc­tion of lac­tic acid. S. mutans can trans­port and fer­ment a wide range of sug­ars, gen­er­at­ing pri­mar­ily lac­ tic, for­mic, and ace­tic ac­ids as met­a­bolic end prod­ucts. Despite the need to en­dure pe­ri­ods that near star­va­tion (the pe­ri­ods be­tween meals) where sal­i­vary mol­e­cules, dead mi­cro­or­gan­isms, sloughed ep­i­the­lial cells, and stored in­tra­cel­lu­lar po­ly­sac­cha­rides (see be­low) are the pri­mary nu­tri­ents, bac­te­ria re­sid­ing in den­tal bio­films must also ad­just to the abrupt ex­po­sure to ex­cess amounts of car­bo­hy­drates (dur­ing meal­times). Without ef­fi­cient mech­a­nisms to op­ti­mize the use of avail­able sub­strates, ac­cu­mu­la­tion of toxic gly­co­lytic in­ter­me­di­ates, overacidification of the cy­to­plasm, and os­ motic stress could rap­idly lead to cell death. To with­stand these “feast-orfamine” cy­cles, S. mutans has de­vel­oped so­phis­ti­cated reg­u­la­tory net­works that com­bine tran­scrip­tional reg­u­la­tion with al­lo­ste­ric mod­u­la­tion of en­ zy­matic ac­tiv­i­ties to co­or­di­nate op­ti­mal flow of car­bo­hy­drates ac­cord­ing to their avail­abil­ity and source (Fig. 2A).

Acid Stress Tolerance and Survival
Once bac­te­ria have col­o­nized the tooth sur­face, their sur­vival will largely de­pend on the abil­ity to cope with sev­eral types of en­vi­ron­men­tal stress.
For ex­am­ple, fluc­tu­a­tions in the source and avail­abil­ity of car­bo­hy­drates will af­fect growth rates. The pres­ence of ox­y­gen and its me­tab­o­lites would be in­hib­i­tory to an­aer­o­bic me­tab­o­lism, and ac­cu­mu­la­tion of or­ganic ac­ids will even­tu­ally in­hibit gly­col­y­sis.
The ac­id-adaptive strat­e­gies of S. mutans in­volve mul­ti­ple mech­a­ nisms, in­clud­ing in­duc­tion of the F1F0-ATPase (F-ATPase) sys­tem, changes in the com­po­si­tion of mem­brane fatty ac­ids, and upregulation of stressassociated mo­lec­u­lar chap­er­ones (Fig. 2B). This is known as the ATR. Other Stress Resistance- and Virulence-Related Mechanisms
As briefly men­tioned above, the biofilm mi­lieu is a dy­namic set­ting un­der­go­ ing a num­ber of en­vi­ron­men­tal changes that ex­tend be­yond pH. These ad­di­ tional changes in­clude fluc­tu­a­tions in nu­tri­ent avail­abil­ity and source, tem­per­a­ture, ox­y­gen ten­sion, and os­mo­lal­ity. As in the case of ATRs, the bulk of our un­der­stand­ing of the mo­lec­u­lar mech­a­nisms uti­lized by car­i­o­genic bac­te­ria to cope with other types of stress comes from stud­ies con­ducted with
S. mutans.
Under con­di­tions of nu­tri­ent star­va­tion, most bac­te­ria em­ploy a reg­u­ la­tory re­sponse mech­a­nism known as the strin­gent re­sponse. This re­sponse is me­di­ated by two mod­i­fied gua­no­sine nu­cle­o­tides, col­lec­tively known as (p)ppGpp, and is char­ac­ter­ized by global downregulation of growth-related path­ways with con­com­i­tant ac­ti­va­tion of amino acid bio­syn­the­sis, nu­tri­ent trans­port, and stress sur­vival path­ways. In S. mutans, changes in (p)ppGpp pools af­fect biofilm for­ma­tion, sugar me­tab­o­lism, and stress tol­er­ance, sug­ gest­ing an over­lap be­tween cir­cuits that gov­ern gen­eral stress tol­er­ance and biofilm for­ma­tion. The oral strep­to­cocci also en­code a num­ber of nu­tri­entsensing tran­scrip­tional reg­u­la­tors, namely CcpA and CodY, which reg­u­late cen­tral me­tab­o­lism but also con­trol the ex­pres­sion of stress, biofilm, and vir­u­lence-related traits, fur­ther sug­gest­ing an over­lap be­tween cir­cuits that gov­ern gen­eral stress tol­er­ance and biofilm for­ma­tion. While low pH pro­vi­des a se­lec­tive ad­van­tage for car­i­o­genic spe­cies, ox­ i­da­tive stress may be an equally im­por­tant en­vi­ron­men­tal fac­tor influ­enc­ ing the path­o­genic po­ten­tial of oral bio­films. Oral bio­films are tran­siently ex­posed to ox­y­gen, and while ox­y­gen is not di­rectly toxic to bac­te­ria, chem­ i­cally and en­zy­mat­i­cally me­di­ated sin­gle-electron re­duc­tions of ox­y­gen pro­duce re­ac­tive ox­y­gen spe­cies (ROS) that can very rap­idly cause ir­re­vers­ ible dam­age to DNA, pro­teins, and mem­brane lip­ids. In the oral cav­ity, dam­ ag­ing ROS arise from sa­liva, bac­te­rial me­tab­o­lism, and even H2O2-containing oral hy­giene and tooth-bleaching prod­ucts. Moreover, global tran­scrip­tional and phe­no­typic an­a­ly­ses in­di­cate that the pres­ence of ox­y­gen strongly in­ hib­its biofilm for­ma­tion by S. mutans and al­ters biofilm me­tab­o­lism in a way that ren­ders it less car­i­o­gen­ic.
To avoid ex­po­sure to toxic lev­els of ROS, bac­te­ria uti­lize mul­ti­ple and of­ten over­lap­ping de­tox­i­fi­ca­tion mech­a­nisms, such as the pro­duc­ tion of cat­a­lase, su­per­ox­ide dis­mut­ase, and per­ox­i­dase en­zymes. Oral strep­to­cocci lack cat­a­lase but har­bor a man­ga­nese-dependent su­per­ox­ ide dis­mut­ase, an al­kyl hydroperoxidase, com­po­nents of thioredoxin and glutaredoxin sys­tems, and the iron-binding pro­tein Dpr. All of these sys­tems have been linked to ox­i­da­tive stress sur­vival in vi­tro, but their di­rect roles in car­ies for­ma­tion has not been de­ter­mined. In S. mutans, the SpxA1 reg­u­la­tor is re­spon­si­ble for the tran­scrip­tional ac­ti­va­tion of vir­tu­ally ev­ery ma­jor ox­i­da­tive stress gene. Unsurprisingly, an spxA1 de­ le­tion strain caused fewer sulcal car­ies le­sions in rats fed with a car­i­o­ genic di­et.
Many S. mutans strains are ­able to take up DNA from the en­vi­ron­ ment, a well-orchestrated pro­cess known as bac­te­rial com­pe­tence. The com­pe­tence path­way of S. mutans is linked to the pro­duc­tion of bac­te­rio­ cins, which are used to elim­i­nate closely re­lated com­pet­i­tors, such as mem­ bers of the mitis group streptococci. In ad­di­tion to the ob­vi­ous ad­van­tage in in­ter­spe­cies com­pe­ti­tion, the pres­ence of com­pe­tence and bac­te­rio­cin genes may help ex­plain the great ge­netic di­ver­sity and phe­no­typic het­ero­ ge­ne­ity among S. mutans strains and might also be linked to their car­i­o­genic po­ten­tial. It has also been pro­posed that the co­or­di­na­tion of com­pe­tence and bac­te­rio­cin pro­duc­tion is part of an al­tru­is­tic sui­cide mech­a­nism among sub­pop­u­la­tions of com­pe­tent cells within the com­mu­nity. However, com­ pe­tence and bac­te­rio­cin genes are not ubiq­ui­tously found in S. mutans iso­ lates, and es­sen­tial com­pe­tence genes are ab­sent in the ge­nome of the equally car­i­o­genic S. sobrinus. Thus, the sig­nif­i­cance of these path­ways in biofilm per­sis­tence, den­tal plaque ecol­ogy, and, more im­por­tantly, den­tal car­ies still war­rants fur­ther stud­ies.

As the sci­ence of microbiomics evolves, it now be­comes clearer that while caus­a­tive agents do ex­ist for oral dis­eases, those or­gan­isms may be only a small part of the oral microbiome. A very large num­ber of other spe­cies or taxa are pres­ent in the oral cav­ity, all­ of which ap­pear to pro­vide struc­ture to mi­cro­bial com­mu­ni­ties and so con­ found at­tempts to log­i­cally elim­i­nate these dis­eases. Although the microbi­ ota of car­ies-active in­di­vid­u­als is dis­tinct from that of their car­ies-free coun­ter­parts, there is sig­nif­i­cant het­ero­ge­ne­ity in microbiome study out­ comes, sug­gest­ing that novel ap­proaches may be re­quired to fur­ther de­ fine the mi­cro­bial sig­na­tures as­so­ci­ated with the on­set and pro­gres­sion of den­tal car­ies. In many ways, a shift in abun­dance of groups of spe­cies rather than the “ap­pear­ance” of novel car­i­o­genic spe­cies or the path­o­ge­ nic­ity of a sin­gle spe­cies best de­scribes the dif­fer­ences be­tween car­ies-free and car­ies-active microbiotas. Whether some of these or­gan­isms are just by­stand­ers or play an ac­tive role in the dis­ease pro­cess needs to be ex­ plored us­ing rel­e­vant in vi­vo mod­els.

Commensal-Pathogen Interactions
Numerous bac­te­rial spe­cies are rec­og­nized as com­men­sal, as they are linked to the health sta­tus of the oral cav­ity and ex­hibit di­verse pro­tec­ tive prop­er­ties that in­hibit the ini­ti­a­tion and de­vel­op­ment of den­tal car­ ies. The in­verse as­so­ci­a­tion be­tween the abun­dance of oral com­men­sal bac­te­ria such as S. san­gui­nis and S. gordonii from the mitis group (as­so­ ci­ated with health) and that of S. mutans (linked with dis­ease) has been doc­u­mented, im­ply­ing that mitis group strep­to­cocci may have the abil­ity to con­trol S. mutans growth and ac­cu­mu­la­tion. Many strep­to­cocci from the mitis group pro­duce co­pi­ous amounts (in the millimolar range) of
H2O2, which in­hib­its the growth of S. mutans and other car­i­o­genic bac­ te­ria. Interestingly, per­ox­ide also serves as a sig­nal­ing molecule, and finetuned production regulates biofilm formation by commensal bacteria.

Acidogenic Interactions
Within the highly acidic den­tal plaque as­so­ci­ated with car­ies, be­sides mu­ tans group strep­to­cocci, other acidogenic and ac­id-tolerant bac­te­ria are fre­quently de­tected. Such bac­te­ria in­clude strains of low-pH non-mutans group streptococcal spe­cies, actinomyces, lac­to­ba­cilli, bifidobacteria, and
Scardovia spe­cies, which may also con­trib­ute to path­o­gen­e­sis by en­hanc­ ing acid­i­fi­ca­tion of the biofilm mi­lieu. Oral lac­to­ba­cilli have long been rec­og­nized as a car­i­o­genic path­o­gen and were con­sid­ered the ma­jor car­i­o­genic bac­te­rium prior to the dis­cov­ery of S. mutans. Lactobacilli are not ef­fi­cient oral col­o­niz­ers, but the pres­ence of S. mutans and GtfB and the pro­duc­tion of sticky glu­cans lay the foun­da­tion for lac­to­ba­cilli to col­ o­nize the oral biofilm. Moreover, the oral bio­films en­coun­tered in car­ies le­sions are acidic, an­aer­o­bic, and rich in car­bo­hy­drates, which is the ideal niche for op­por­tu­nis­tic lac­to­ba­cilli to re­side and thrive. In ad­di­tion to the pro­duc­tion of ac­ids, lac­to­ba­cilli also pos­sess two en­zymes po­ten­tially im­ por­tant in sugar me­tab­o­lism. One be­longs to the gly­co­side hy­dro­lase fam­ily 70 (GH70) glucansucrases (sim­i­lar to the S. mutans Gtfs), which syn­the­size α-glucan-type ex­tra­cel­lu­lar po­ly­sac­cha­rides from su­crose.
The other en­zyme is a starch-acting al­pha-amylase, which pro­cesses starch to isomalto-/malto-polysaccharides. The pres­ence of these two en­zymes al­lows lac­to­ba­cilli to ef­fi­ciently uti­lize dif­fer­ent di­e­tary sug­ars to pro­ duce hy­brid ex­tra­cel­lu­lar po­ly­mers that may con­trib­ute to car­i­o­genic bio­films.
Another ex­am­ple of an or­gan­ism that par­tic­i­pa­tes in acidogenic in­ter­ ac­tions is Veillonella, an ob­li­ga­tely an­aer­o­bic Gram-negative bac­te­rium.
While Veillonella does not uti­lize car­bo­hy­drates as a source of en­ergy, it uti­lizes lac­tic acid, py­ru­vate, and pep­tone. Such nu­tri­tional pref­er­ences ren­der this or­gan­ism de­pen­dent on the pres­ence of lac­tic acid bac­te­ria pro­ duced by the strep­to­cocci. In fact, Veillonella of­ten coaggregates with oral strep­to­cocci, and, not sur­pris­ingly, it has been iden­ti­fied as a sig­na­ture or­ gan­ism in car­ies-active sub­jects. Given the abil­ity of Veillonella to me­tab­ o­lize lac­tic acid, it has long been per­ceived that the pres­ence of Veillonella in plaque neu­tral­izes the acidic pH of the oral biofilm, thereby pre­vent­ing enamel de­min­er­al­iza­tion. However, ev­i­dence from hu­man microbiome stud­ies dem­on­strates that both Veillonella and S. mutans are as­so­ci­ated with car­ies le­sions. Alkalinogenic Interactions
In con­trast to car­i­o­genic bac­te­ria, other bac­te­rial spe­cies found in oral bio­ films can counter the del­e­te­ri­ous ef­fects of acid­i­fi­ca­tion by pro­duc­ing large quan­ti­ties of am­mo­nia us­ing sal­i­vary sub­strates such as ar­gi­nine (S. gordonii and S. san­gui­nis) via the ADS and urea (S. salivarius and Actinomyces naes­ lundii) via ure­ase en­zymes. Presumably, al­ka­li-producing bac­te­ria pro­tect against plaque acid­i­fi­ca­tion and fur­ther growth/dominance of bac­te­ria that thrive in acidic con­di­tions, while help­ing to pre­vent the dam­ag­ing ef­fects of acid de­min­er­al­iza­tion. Notably, ADS and ure­ase ex­pres­sion/activity is en­ hanced in re­sponse to in­creased ar­gi­nine or urea lev­els and low pH val­ues and even in in­ter­spe­cies in­ter­ac­tions (be­tween S. gordonii and A. naeslun­ dii). These mech­a­nisms can pro­vide al­ka­li-producing bac­te­ria with a com­ pet­i­tive ad­van­tage when biofilm en­vi­ron­ments be­come too acidic, po­ten­tially re­sult­ing in low­ered car­ies risk.

Bacterial-Fungal Interactions
Results from sev­eral clin­i­cal stud­ies have re­vealed that the fun­gus C. albi­ cans is fre­quently de­tected in high num­bers in plaque biofilms from tod­dlers with early child­hood car­ies (ECC) and has also been de­tected in deep-seated car­i­ous le­sions. In con­trast, C. albicans is ei­ther ab­sent or de­tected only spo­ rad­i­cally in plaque bio­films from healthy, car­ies-free chil­dren. In the oral cav­ity, C. albicans has been long rec­og­nized to form mixed mi­cro­bial com­ mu­ni­ties on mu­co­sal and pros­thetic sur­faces. However, re­cent stud­ies have shown that C. albicans in­ter­acts with S. mutans on tooth sur­faces in the pres­ence of su­crose. The or­gan­isms ap­pear to have de­vel­oped a sym­bi­otic re­la­tion­ship that is me­di­ated through the in­flu­ence of Gtf exo­en­zymes. More spe­cif­i­cally, the S. mutans GtfB en­zyme binds with ex­cep­tional avid­ity to the sur­face of C. albicans cells, pro­duc­ing large amounts of glu­cans on the fun­ gal sur­face when su­crose is avail­able. Furthermore, the pres­ence of C. albi­ cans within mixed-species bio­films was shown to in­duce gtf gene ex­pres­sion and en­hance sugar me­tab­o­lism. Thus, this unique in­ter­spe­cies in­ter­ac­tion can boost the abil­ity of both or­gan­isms to col­o­nize tooth sur­faces, dra­mat­i­ cally in­creas­ing the amount of po­ly­sac­cha­ride in the biofilm ma­trix and syn­ er­gis­ti­cally en­hanc­ing vir­u­lence. This cross-kingdom re­la­tion­ship has been shown to lead to the on­set of ram­pant den­tal car­ies (sim­i­lar to le­sions in
ECC) in an an­i­mal model of the dis­ease. Further in­ves­ti­ga­tion into the mech­ a­nisms by which EPS ma­trix is en­hanced and other met­a­bolic path­ways that are influ­enced when these spe­cies are to­gether may of­fer ad­di­tional in­sights into the dis­ease pro­cess.

Thus, un­der­stand­ing the im­pact of di­ets on mi­cro­bial com­mu­nity bal­ance in the con­text of den­tal car­ies will also be cru­cial for the de­vel­op­ment of ef­fec­tive pre­ven­tion strat­e­gies to de­ter this dis­ease. Understanding how the path­o­gens in­ter­act with other oral spe­cies and di­e­tary fac­tors as well as other host-derived mol­e­cules may lead to the dis­cov­ery of new ther­a­peu­ tic tar­gets. Furthermore, the use of small mol­e­cules to probe the com­plex in­ter­ac­tions and de­velop agents that in­ter­fere with the col­lec­tive mi­cro­ bial car­i­o­genic be­hav­ior, acid pro­duc­tion, and ex­tra­cel­lu­lar ma­trix as­sem­ bly can lead to new ther­a­peu­tic ap­proaches to pre­vent car­ies.

The path­o­log­i­cal pro­cess that leads to den­tal car­ies in­volves the as­sem­bly of a car­i­o­genic biofilm that com­prises an ex­tra­cel­lu­lar po­ly­meric matrix, acidogenic-aciduric microbiotas, and an acidic en­vi­ron­ment due to pro­duc­tion of or­ganic ac­ids (mostly lac­tic acid) and ex­tra­cel­lu­lar po­ly­meric sub­stance (EPS; such as glu­cans) by bac­te­ria from di­e­tary car­bo­hy­drates. The or­ganic ac­ids con­fined within bio­films lower the pH at the tooth sur­face to the point where the hy­droxy­ap­a­tite of enamel de­min­er­al­izes.

Among car­i­o­genic bac­te­ria, S. mutans is par­tic­u­larly ad­ept at col­o­niz­ing and ac­cu­mu­lat­ing within bio­films. S. mutans uti­lizes di­e­tary su­crose to pro­duce EPS such as glu­can (built from mono­mers of glu­cose) and fructan (built from mono­mers of fruc­tose) through the ac­tion of glucosyltransferases (Gtfs) and a fructosyltransferase (Ftf), while be­ing both acidogenic and aciduric.

Insoluble glu­can po­ly­mers help at­tach the bac­te­rial cells to the tooth sur­face through the ac­tiv­i­ties of glu­can-binding pro­teins pres­ent on the bac­te­rial cell sur­faces. Moreover, glu­cans form the core of the car­i­o­genic biofilm ma­trix that em­beds the cells and cre­ates a dif­fu­sion-modulating mi­lieu, pro­vid­ing pro­tec­tion, and spa­tial/chemical het­ero­ge­ne­ity, while fa­cil­i­tat­ing the de­vel­op­ment of acidic mi­cro­en­vi­ron­ments. Glucan and fructan can also be used as re­serves of sug­ars for me­tab­o­lism and growth.

In gen­eral, car­i­o­genic bac­te­ria are acid pro­duc­ers and highly aciduric; i.e., they can re­sist the ad­verse effects of the low pH that re­sults from their own met­a­bolic ac­tiv­i­ties. Mechanisms of acid re­sis­tance in­clude pump­ing pro­tons out­ of the cell, in­creas­ing the pro­por­tion of un­sat­u­rated mem­brane fatty ac­ids, and rais­ing the in­tra­cel­lu­lar pH via am­mo­nia-generating sys­tems.
