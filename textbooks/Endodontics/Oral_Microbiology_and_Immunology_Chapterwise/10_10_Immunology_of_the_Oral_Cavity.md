INTRODUCTION
The mu­co­sal sur­faces of the body con­sti­tute a ma­jor in­ter­face with the mi­ cro­bial world, and the ma­jor­ity of path­o­gens that in­fect hu­mans in­vade through mu­co­sal routes. The mouth forms the en­try to the al­i­men­tary tract and is in con­ti­nu­ity with the phar­ynx. Therefore, the phys­i­cal and func­tional in­teg­rity of the oral mu­cosa is im­por­tant both for oral and sys­temic health.
In the oral cav­ity, soft shed­ding mu­co­sal sur­faces co­ex­ist with hard non­ shedding tooth sur­faces, both of which are pro­tected by in­nate and adap­tive im­mune mech­a­nisms (Fig. 1). A par­tic­u­larly sus­cep­ti­ble area, how­ever, is the junc­tion be­tween the teeth and the gin­giva, which po­ten­tially con­sti­tutes a
“breed­ing ground” for peri­odon­tal bac­te­ria. The im­mune mech­a­nisms and mol­e­cules of the oral cav­ity de­rive from either the se­cre­tory or the sys­temic im­mune sys­tem. The for­mer en­ter the oral cav­ity through the se­cre­tions of the ma­jor and mi­nor sal­i­vary glands, whereas the lat­ter en­ter through the gin­gi­val crevicular fluid (GCF) (Fig. 1). The GCF is a se­rum ex­u­date that orig­i­na­tes in the gin­gi­val capil­lar­ies and flows into the gin­gi­val crev­ice, car­ ry­ing lo­cally pro­duced me­di­a­tors of in­nate and adap­tive im­mu­nity. This chap­ter is di­vided into two sec­tions—“Oral Secretory Immunity” and
“Subgingival Immunity”—to bet­ter re­flect the pe­cu­li­ar­i­ties of the re­spec­ tive an­a­tom­i­cal ar­eas and the im­mune mech­a­nisms op­er­at­ing there­in.

ORAL SECRETORY IMMUNITY
Every day, an es­ti­mated 0.5 to 1.5 li­ters of sa­liva is pro­duced and se­creted in the oral cav­ity by the paired ma­jor (pa­rotid, sub­lin­gual, and sub­man­dib­u­ lar) and the nu­mer­ous mi­nor sal­i­vary glands through­out­ the mouth. About
65% of the whole sa­liva is pro­duced by the sub­man­dib­u­lar gland, 23% by the pa­rotid gland, and 4% by the sub­lin­gual gland. The mi­nor sal­i­vary glands col­lec­tively con­trib­ute the re­main­ing 8% of the sal­i­vary pro­duc­tion.
The ba­sic se­cre­tory unit of sal­i­vary glands is rep­re­sented by the acinus, a clus­ter of ep­i­the­lial cells that se­crete a fluid com­prised of wa­ter, elec­tro­lytes, mu­cins, and pro­teins, in­clud­ing en­zymes. There are two ba­sic types of ac­i­ nar ep­i­the­lial cells: the se­rous cells, which pro­duce a wa­tery fluid de­void of mu­cus ma­te­rial, and the mu­cous cells, which pro­duce a mu­cus-rich se­cre­tion.

From the ac­ini, se­cre­tions are ini­tially col­lected into small col­lect­ing ducts, which lead to larger ducts and fi­nally to a sin­gle large duct that se­cretes the sal­i­vary con­tents into the oral cav­i­ty. Cationic Antimicrobial Peptides
Cationic antimicrobial peptides are small pep­tides (gen­er­ally be­tween 12 and 50 amino ac­ids) with a net pos­i­tive charge, ow­ing to an ex­cess of ba­sic amino ac­ids, such as ar­gi­nine, ly­sine, and his­ti­dine. At least 50% of the amino ac­ids in these mol­e­cules are hy­dro­pho­bic. This prop­erty al­lows them to in­ter­act with bac­te­rial mem­branes, which con­sti­tutes their com­mon tar­ get, de­spite dif­fer­ent modes of ac­tion. Proteins with Bacterial Agglutination Properties

MUCIN-7
A rel­a­tively small sal­i­vary gly­co­pro­tein (357 amino acid res­i­dues), mu­cin-7 is pro­duced by the mu­cous ac­i­nar cells of the sal­i­vary glands. It has been shown to pro­mote bac­te­rial ag­glu­ti­na­tion and pre­ven­tion of HIV pen­e­ tra­tion into the host tis­sue. Its con­cen­tra­tion in stim­u­lated sa­liva is re­duced in pa­tients with peri­odon­ti­tis.

SALIVARY AGGLUTININ
Salivary agglutinin is a large gly­co­pro­tein (340 kDa) that is de­rived from the pa­rotid se­cre­tion and can ag­glu­ti­nate a large num­ber of oral bac­te­ria (in­clud­ing car­i­o­genic strep­to­cocci) via its mul­ti­ple scav­en­ger re­cep­tor cys­te­ ine-rich re­peats. It is iden­ti­cal to the scav­en­ger re­cep­tor gp340 pres­ent in bronchoalveolar fluid. In hu­man sa­liva, gp340 binds HIV-1 gp120 and in­ hib­its vi­ral in­fec­tion. Gene po­ly­mor­phisms of the sal­i­vary ag­glu­ti­nin have been as­so­ci­ated with a high in­ci­dence of car­ies.

SURFACTANT PROTEIN A
A mem­ber of the collectin fam­ily, sur­fac­tant pro­tein A is a ma­jor pro­tein com­po­nent of lung sur­fac­tant. It is also ex­pressed in hu­man sal­i­vary glands and can ag­glu­ti­nate bac­te­ria and neu­tral­ize the in­flu­enza vi­rus via its si­alic acid res­i­dues. Its lev­els in the sal­i­vary glands are upregulated in pa­tients with chronic sialadenitis, sug­gest­ing a role in the in­nate host de­fense of sal­ i­vary glands.

PROLINE-RICH PROTEINS
Proline-rich proteins (PRPs) are sal­i­vary pro­teins pro­duced by pa­rotid and sub­man­dib­u­lar glands and con­sti­tute nearly 70% of the to­tal pro­tein of hu­man sa­liva. Proline ac­counts for about 25 to 40% of the amino acid con­ tent of PRPs. The larger PRPs bind to tooth sur­faces and pro­mote bac­te­rial ad­her­ence, whereas the smaller PRPs ag­glu­ti­nate bac­te­ria in the fluid phase, thereby an­tag­o­niz­ing bac­te­rial ad­her­ence and hence col­o­ni­za­tion. PRPs on the tooth sur­face can be de­graded into pep­tides hav­ing po­ten­tial pro­tec­tive ef­fects. For in­stance, the an­ti­mi­cro­bial pen­ta­pep­tide (Arg-Gly-Arg-Pro-Gln) is de­rived from PRPs af­ter pro­te­ol­y­sis by com­men­sal Streptococcus and Actinomyces spp. This pen­ta­pep­tide was shown to in­hibit the su­crose-induced de­crease of den­tal plaque pH in vi­tro.

FIBRONECTIN
Fibronectin a large gly­co­pro­tein ex­pressed by many cells, in­clud­ing he­pa­ to­cytes and ep­i­the­lial cells. It is also found in sa­liva, where it me­di­ates bac­ te­rial ag­glu­ti­na­tion and con­trols bac­te­rial col­o­ni­za­tion. Fibronectin binds to the fim­briae of P. gingivalis and in­hib­its the fim­bria-induced ex­pres­sion of in­flam­ma­tory cy­to­kines in mac­ro­phages. Reduced lev­els of fi­bro­nec­tin are cor­re­lated with peri­odon­ti­tis in adults and with high lev­els of S. mutans in chil­dren.

Metal Ion Chelators

CALGRANULIN A AND B (CALPROTECTIN)
Calgranulin A (S100A8) and calgranulin B (S100A9) are metal ion-binding pro­teins that in­hibit bac­te­rial growth by scav­eng­ing di­va­lent cat­ions, such as Mn2+ and Zn2+. The di­mer of calgranulin A and B, termed calprotectin, is ex­pressed in the cy­to­sol of var­i­ous cell types (neu­tro­phils, mono­cytes, and keratinocytes) and has host de­fense ac­tiv­ity. For in­stance, calprotectin pro­tects ep­i­the­lial cells from in­va­sion by P. gingivalis and other bac­te­ria.
The ex­pres­sion of calprotectin is upregulated in the GCF of pa­tients with peri­odon­tal dis­ease and in sa­liva of pa­tients with can­di­di­a­sis, im­ply­ing a host de­fense mech­a­nism to con­trol in­fec­tion.

LACTOFERRIN
Lactoferrin is an 80-kDa gly­co­pro­tein pro­duced by mu­co­sal ep­i­the­lial cells and neu­tro­phils and found in all­ ma­jor mu­co­sal se­cre­tions, in­clud­ing sa­liva and the GCF. Lactoferrin binds iron (Fe3+) in as­so­ci­a­tion with bi­car­bon­ate and, there­fore, can de­prive mi­cro­or­gan­isms (bac­te­ria, vi­ruses, fungi and par­a­sites) of this es­sen­tial nu­tri­ent. Apo-lactoferrin (the iron-free form of lactoferrin) is bac­te­rio­static. On the other hand, iron-saturated lactoferrin in­hib­its the bind­ing of A. actinomycetemcomitans to buc­cal ep­i­the­lial cells, sug­gest­ing that low iron lev­els might en­hance col­o­ni­za­tion by this oral bac­ te­rium. Bactericidal ac­tiv­ity, in­de­pen­dent of iron bind­ing, has been re­ported for lactoferrin and prob­a­bly in­volves in­creased mem­brane per­me­abil­ity in tar­geted bac­te­ria. Protease Inhibitors

CYSTATINS
Cystatins constitute a fam­ily of pro­teins in­volv­ing at least 14 genes. Seven of these genes ex­press sal­i­vary pro­teins that are con­trib­uted by the sub­man­ dib­u­lar and sub­lin­gual sal­i­vary glands. Their an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tion in­volves in­hi­bi­tion of bac­te­rial cys­te­ine pro­te­ases, such as the gingipains of P. gingivalis. Bacterial pro­te­ases play a ma­jor role in nu­tri­ent ac­qui­si­tion, and their in­hi­bi­tion by cystatins can lead to sup­pres­sion of bac­te­rial growth. Cys­ tatins also in­hibit hu­man ly­so­somal ca­thep­sins, which un­der in­flam­ma­tory con­di­tions con­trib­ute to peri­odon­tal tis­sue de­struc­tion.

SECRETORY LEUKOCYTE PROTEASE INHIBITOR
Secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI) is an 11.7-kDa cat­ionic pro­ tein ex­pressed by gin­gi­val keratinocytes and other mu­co­sal ep­i­the­lial cells. SLPI is pres­ent in mu­co­sal se­cre­tions, in­clud­ing sa­liva. It ex­erts an­ti­ bac­te­rial, an­ti­fun­gal, and anti-inflammatory prop­er­ties through its ser­ine pro­te­ase in­hib­i­tor prop­er­ties. SLPI can block the en­try of HIV into host cells, al­though this ac­tion is in­de­pen­dent of its pro­te­ase in­hib­i­tor ac­tiv­ity.
SLPI blocks HIV-1 in­fec­tion of mac­ro­phages and pri­mary T cells at con­ cen­tra­tions (1 to 10 μg/ml) that oc­cur phys­i­o­log­i­cally in sa­liva, sug­gest­ing that SLPI may in part be re­spon­si­ble for the low rate, or es­sen­tial ab­sence, of oral trans­mis­sion of HIV-1 by sa­li­va.

SKIN-DERIVED ANTILEUKOPROTEINASE/ELAFIN
A 12-kDa sal­i­vary pro­tein ex­pressed in the hu­man sub­man­dib­u­lar gland, skin-derived antileukoproteinase (SKALP)/elafin acts as a transglutamin­ ase sub­strate by vir­tue of its ami­no-terminal do­main and has antielastase ac­tiv­ity as­so­ci­ated with its car­boxy-terminal do­main. The pro­tein can kill both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bac­te­ria, ac­tiv­ity that re­quires the pres­ence of both do­mains. Although P. gingivalis upregulates the ex­pres­ sion of elafin in gin­gi­val ep­i­the­lial cells, it can also de­grade the pro­duced pro­tein through the ac­tion of its gingipains.

Enzymes Acting against Bacterial Cell Walls

LYSOZYME
Lysozyme is a 14.6-kDa pro­tein pres­ent in all­ ma­jor body flu­ids, in­clud­ ing sa­liva, milk, lac­ri­mal flu­ids, nasal secretions, and bron­chial se­cre­tions.
Salivary ly­so­zyme is de­rived from the sal­i­vary glands and the GCF. In terms of en­zy­matic ac­tiv­ity, ly­so­zyme is a muramidase that hydrolyzes the β-1,4 gly­co­sidic bond be­tween N-acetylmuramic acid and N-acetylglucosamine res­i­dues in the pep­ti­do­gly­can of the cell wall of Gram-positive bac­te­ria.
This ac­tion may lead to os­motic break­age of the cell mem­brane. Although few spe­cies of bac­te­ria are di­rectly lysed by ly­so­zyme, the cell walls of oral strep­to­cocci, weak­ened by cleav­age of pep­ti­do­gly­can, be­come sus­cep­ti­ble to ly­sis by the ad­di­tion of mono­va­lent an­ions, such as bi­car­bon­ate, fluo­ride, thio­cy­a­nate, and chlo­ride, which are abun­dant in sa­liva. PEPTIDOGLYCAN RECOGNITION PROTEINS 3 AND 4
This fam­ily of large (90 to 115 kDa) an­ti­mi­cro­bial pro­teins is also found in in­sects. Four of these pep­ti­do­gly­can rec­og­ni­tion pro­teins are pres­ent in hu­mans, and mem­bers 3 and 4 are ex­pressed in the sal­i­vary glands. Their mech­a­nism of ac­tion is un­clear, as they bind to the cell wall pep­ti­do­gly­ cans with­out­ permeabilizing the bac­te­rial mem­brane. They can be ei­ther bacteriocidal or bac­te­rio­static, de­pend­ing on bac­te­rial tar­get.

The Peroxidase System
Peroxidases are found in all­ mu­co­sal se­cre­tions. The per­ox­i­dase sys­tem of sa­liva in­cludes sal­i­vary per­ox­i­dase (which is sim­i­lar to lactoperoxidase found in milk) and myeloperoxidase ex­pressed in neu­tro­phils. These en­ zymes cat­a­lyze the ox­i­da­tion of thio­cy­a­nate ions (SCN–) by H2O2 to
OSCN–, which is cy­to­toxic to bac­te­ria. SCN– is a nor­mal com­po­nent in sa­liva, plaque fluid, and GCF, while H2O2 is pro­duced dur­ing the aer­o­bic me­tab­o­lism of glu­cose by bac­te­ria. Therefore, the avail­abil­ity of H2O2 is a ma­jor lim­it­ing fac­tor for SCN– per­ox­i­da­tion in hu­man sa­liva. OSCN– ox­i­ dizes bac­te­rial en­zymes in the gly­co­lytic path­way and, there­fore, in­hib­its the growth of and acid pro­duc­tion by oral mi­cro­or­gan­isms, in­clud­ing strep­ to­cocci, lac­to­ba­cilli, and fungi. H2O2 is also toxic to eu­kary­otic cells, and its re­duc­tion to H2O by sal­i­vary per­ox­i­dase pro­tects the oral mu­cosa. The per­ox­i­dase sys­tem seems to in­ter­act syn­er­gis­ti­cally with other an­ti­mi­cro­bial mol­e­cules, such as ly­so­zyme, lactoferrin, and se­cre­tory IgA (S-IgA).

Biological Activities of S-IgA Antibodies
S-IgA is a rel­a­tively sta­ble im­mu­no­glob­u­lin that can main­tain its an­ti­body ac­tiv­ity for a long time in the pro­teo­lytic en­vi­ron­ment of the oral cav­ity.
The ma­jor de­fense func­tion of S-IgA in­volves im­mune ex­clu­sion of sol­u­ble and par­tic­u­late an­ti­gens, which is an ef­fec­tive anti-inflammatory mech­a­nism to pro­tect mu­co­sal sur­faces. In this re­spect, mo­re­over, S-IgA does not ac­ti­vate the com­ple­ment sys­tem and there­fore is con­sid­ered to be an anti-inflammatory im­mu­no­glob­u­lin. By bind­ing and block­ing mi­cro­bial adhesins, S-IgA an­ti­ bod­ies can in­hibit mi­cro­bial col­o­ni­za­tion and, fur­ther­more, can neu­tral­ize the ac­tion of mi­cro­bial en­zymes or tox­ins (Fig. 3). For these de­fense func­ tions, S-IgA an­ti­bod­ies are con­sid­er­ably more ef­fi­cient than mo­no­meric an­ ti­bod­ies, such as IgG. Intriguingly, pIgA an­ti­bod­ies can neu­tral­ize vi­ruses by in­ter­cept­ing them in­side se­cre­tory ep­i­the­lial cells dur­ing their pIgR-mediated exo­cy­to­sis. S-IgA also synergizes with, and pro­motes the bac­te­rio­static ef­fect of, lactoferrin and the per­ox­i­dase sys­tem.
S-IgA is ab­sent in the sa­liva of new­borns. However, its lev­els emerge rap­idly in the post­na­tal pe­riod and by ap­prox­i­ma­tely 1 to 2 years of age, reach­ing adults lev­els at 4 to 7 years of age. Besides age, there are other im­por­tant fac­tors that can in­flu­ence the sal­i­vary lev­els of S-IgA, in­clud­ing the sal­i­vary flow rate, cig­a­rette smok­ing, preg­nancy, and other stress­ors.
Interestingly, most of S-IgA comes from the sub­man­dib­u­lar glands, which con­tain ap­prox­i­ma­tely two times more IgA-secreting plasma cells per tis­ sue unit than the pa­rotid gland. The mi­nor sal­i­vary glands also con­tain nu­mer­ous IgA-secreting plasma cells (three times more cells are found in the la­bial glands per tis­sue unit com­pared to the pa­rotid glands) and con­ trib­ute about 30 to 35% of the to­tal S-IgA in sa­li­va.
Although S-IgA an­ti­bod­ies ex­ert im­por­tant de­fense func­tions, the re­ sults of stud­ies that at­tempted to cor­re­late lev­els of S-IgA an­ti­bod­ies with den­tal car­ies have not been con­clu­sive. Indeed, the lev­els of S-IgA an­ti­bod­ ies to mutans group strep­to­cocci are var­i­ously cor­re­lated pos­i­tively or neg­a­tively with past ex­pe­ri­ence of den­tal car­ies (as mea­sured by in­di­ces of de­cayed, miss­ing, and filled teeth). Therefore, the re­sults could be in­ter­ preted in dif­fer­ent ways to sug­gest ei­ther that the an­ti­bod­ies could con­fer pro­tec­tion, or con­versely, that the an­ti­bod­ies sim­ply re­flected pre­vi­ous ex­po­ sure to mutans strep­to­cocci. Nevertheless, mu­co­sal ap­pli­ca­tion of car­ies vac­ cines in ro­dents in­duces pro­tec­tive S-IgA an­ti­body re­sponses that sup­press the col­o­ni­za­tion of tooth sur­faces by orally in­oc­u­lated mutans strep­to­cocci as well as the for­ma­tion of car­i­ous le­sions, even in an­i­mals fed a high-sucrose diet. SUBGINGIVAL IMMUNITY
The junc­tion be­tween the teeth and the gin­gi­val mu­cosa forms an in­ter­face be­tween the mu­co­sal and sys­temic im­mune sys­tems. The GCF, which bathes the gin­gi­val crev­ice and ac­cu­mu­lates around the necks of the teeth, also en­ ters the oral cav­ity and mixes with sa­liva at an es­ti­mated di­lu­tion of 1:500 to 1:1,000. In this con­text, hu­moral an­ti­mi­cro­bial mol­e­cules and an­ti­bod­ies pres­ent in the GCF may im­pact the bac­te­rial col­o­ni­za­tion of the teeth, at least in the vi­cin­ity of the gin­gi­val mar­gins. However, the tran­si­tion from the iso­tonic gin­gi­val crev­ice to hy­po­tonic sa­liva is not con­du­cive to the nor­mal func­tion of im­mune cells, such as phago­cytic cells or lym­pho­cytes, if they en­ter the oral cav­ity. On the other hand, the GCF and the im­mune mech­a­nisms op­er­at­ing in the gin­gi­val crev­ice are in­ti­mately as­so­ci­ated with peri­odon­tal health or dis­ease.

Innate and Adaptive Immune Players be­low the Gum Line
In a clin­i­cally healthy periodontium, the GCF rep­re­sents a slow-flowing tran­su­date of plasma pro­teins. However, the flow of GCF into the gin­gi­val crev­ice in­creases con­sid­er­ably if the biofilm is left un­dis­turbed for 2 to
4 days (i.e., time re­quired for the ini­ti­a­tion of gin­gi­vi­tis). This is be­cause the biofilm ex­tends into the subgingival re­gion (by pro­lif­er­a­tion and spread­ing or by re­lo­ca­tion of dis­lodged bac­te­ria), pro­vok­ing a ro­bust in­flam­ma­tory re­sponse. The ini­tial host re­sponse to the ma­tur­ing biofilm in­volves in­ creased vas­cu­lar per­me­abil­ity (of the subepithelial blood ves­sels) and flow of in­flam­ma­tory ex­u­date, as well as che­mo­tac­tic re­cruit­ment of in­flam­ma­ tory phago­cytes, such as neu­tro­phils and, to a lesser ex­tent, mac­ro­phages.
The mi­gra­tion of leu­ko­cytes into the crev­ice oc­curs through the junc­tional ep­i­the­lium, which, un­der in­flam­ma­tory con­di­tions, is largely oc­cu­pied (by about 60%) by traf­fick­ing neu­tro­phils.

GCF, and the im­me­di­ate sur­round­ings (oral sulcular ep­i­the­lium, junc­tional ep­i­the­lium, subgingival tooth sur­face with the at­tached biofilm) form a com­plex net­work of im­mune play­ers. The in­nate im­mune play­ers in­clude phago­cytes, ep­i­the­lial cells, and sol­u­ble in­flam­ma­tory me­di­a­tors, such as cy­to­kines and com­ple­ment ac­ti­va­tion prod­ucts. Features of the Subgingival Environment and Their Impact on Host Response
Although it is a cliché, noth­ing could be truer in the field of im­mu­nol­ogy than that the host im­mune re­sponse is a dou­ble-edged sword. An ex­ag­ger­ ated host re­sponse to the subgingival biofilm can in­flict col­lat­eral dam­age upon the peri­odon­tal tis­sues. On the other hand, im­mune de­fi­cien­cies can lead to sim­i­lar un­fa­vor­able out­comes, as ev­i­denced by the in­creased sus­ cep­ti­bil­ity to peri­odon­ti­tis of in­di­vid­u­als with neu­tro­phil de­fi­cien­cies with re­gard to ab­so­lute num­bers and traf­fick­ing ca­pac­ity (see Chapter 15 for de­tails). In con­trast, peri­odon­tal health rep­re­sents a dy­namic bal­anced state where proinflammatory and an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tiv­i­ties are op­ti­mally reg­u­lated to pre­vent harm­ful host re­ac­tions while con­trol­ling the lo­cal micro­ biota. Certain an­a­tom­i­cal and phys­i­o­log­i­cal fac­tors ren­der peri­odon­tal tis­ sue ho­meo­sta­sis a del­i­cate and chal­leng­ing task for the host. To bet­ter un­der­stand the pe­cu­li­ar­i­ties of the subgingival en­vi­ron­ment, it would be in­struc­tive to con­trast it with the in­tes­tine.
The in­tes­tine is of­ten cited as an ex­am­ple of an or­gan that has op­ti­ mally evolved to ac­com­mo­date ben­e­fi­cial host-microbe in­ter­ac­tions. In­ deed, al­though the in­tes­ti­nal im­mune sys­tem is faced with an un­usu­ally high mi­cro­bial bur­den com­pared to most other tis­sues or or­gans, hu­mans (and mam­mals in gen­eral) are nor­mally healthy and free of clin­i­cally sig­nif­ i­cant gut in­flam­ma­tion. Important mech­a­nisms that con­trib­ute to the con­trol of ad­verse in­flam­ma­tory re­sponses in the in­tes­tine in­clude the pro­duc­tion of a thick mu­cus layer, which cov­ers the in­tes­ti­nal ep­i­the­lium, and the in­duc­tion of S-IgA an­ti­bod­ies. Toll-Like Receptors and Coreceptors
Sentinel cells (e.g., neu­tro­phils, mac­ro­phages, and den­dritic cells) sense bac­te­ria through con­served, germ-line en­coded re­cep­tors termed pat­tern recognition re­cep­tors (PRRs). When PRRs bind mi­cro­bial li­gands, they in­duce in­tra­cel­lu­lar sig­nal­ing path­ways that ac­ti­vate an­ti­mi­cro­bial and in­ flam­ma­tory re­sponses aim­ing to con­trol or erad­i­cate a mi­cro­bial threat. A ma­jor PRR fam­ily is rep­re­sented by the Toll-like re­cep­tors (TLRs), each mem­ber of which senses dis­tinct types of con­served mi­cro­bial struc­tures (mi­crobe-associated mo­lec­u­lar pat­terns [MAMPs]), thus en­dow­ing the in­nate re­sponse with a de­gree of spec­i­fic­ity (see Chapter 2 for de­tails).
Clinically healthy gin­gi­vae con­tain a va­ri­ety of TLR-expressing cells, in­clud­ing pro­fes­sional phago­cytes, gin­gi­val ep­i­the­lial cells and fi­bro­blasts, and peri­odon­tal lig­a­ment cells. TLR ac­ti­va­tion in re­sponse to lo­cal bac­te­ria is thought to be im­por­tant in that it can sup­port ho­meo­static im­mu­nity, in­ clud­ing pro­duc­tion of an­ti­mi­cro­bial pep­tides and che­mo­kines for nor­mal re­cruit­ment of neu­tro­phils and other im­mune cells. However, in­hi­bi­tion of che­mo­kine pro­duc­tion, termed “lo­cal che­mo­kine pa­ral­y­sis,” by bac­te­ria such as P. gingivalis can po­ten­tially de­lay the in­flux of neu­tro­phils, al­low­ ing suf­fi­cient time for the bac­te­ria to es­tab­lish col­o­ni­za­tion. In den­dritic cells, TLR ac­ti­va­tion en­ables them to ex­press costimulatory mol­e­cules (e.g.,
CD80 and CD86) that are re­quired for ef­fec­tive an­ti­gen presentation and ac­ti­va­tion of T cells, thereby link­ing in­nate to adap­tive im­mu­nity. Neutrophils
Neutrophils are pro­fes­sional phago­cytes that are swiftly re­cruited from the cir­cu­la­tion to pe­riph­eral tis­sues in re­sponse to in­fec­tion, in­jury, or in­ flam­ma­tion. They can be found in great num­bers in the gin­gi­val crev­ice, where they com­prise ≥95% of to­tal leu­ko­cytes. The ex­trav­a­sa­tion of neu­ tro­phils is me­di­ated by a cas­cade of low- and high-affinity ad­he­sive in­ter­ ac­tions be­tween the neu­tro­phils and the en­do­the­lium lin­ing the blood ves­sels. Complement
Complement is an an­ti­mi­cro­bial en­zyme sys­tem pres­ent in se­rum and in­ flam­ma­tory ex­u­dates, such as the GCF. In ad­di­tion to the clas­sic se­rum pro­teins (C1 to C9), the in­te­grated com­ple­ment sys­tem com­prises pat­ternrecognition mol­e­cules, convertases and other pro­te­ases, reg­u­la­tors, and re­cep­tors for in­ter­ac­tions with im­mune me­di­a­tors. The trig­ger­ing of com­ ple­ment pro­ceeds via dis­tinct path­ways (namely, the clas­si­cal, lec­tin, and alternative pathways) and in­volves se­quen­tial ac­ti­va­tion and pro­teo­lytic cleav­age of a se­ries of pro­teins. All three path­ways con­verge at the third com­po­nent of com­ple­ment (C3), which is ac­ti­vated by path­way-specific
C3 convertases and leads to the gen­er­a­tion of ef­fec­tor mol­e­cules that me­ di­ate re­cruit­ment and ac­ti­va­tion of in­flam­ma­tory cells (via the anaphyla­ toxins C3a and C5a that ac­ti­vate spe­cific G-protein-coupled re­cep­tors on leu­ko­cytes), mi­cro­bial opsonization and phago­cy­to­sis (through op­so­nins such as C3b and iC3b), and di­rect ly­sis of tar­geted sus­cep­ti­ble bac­te­ria (by means of the C5b-9 mem­brane at­tack com­plex). Moreover, com­ple­ ment has func­tions above and be­yond its tra­di­tional role of tag­ging and elim­i­nat­ing mi­crobes. It is now well ap­pre­ci­ated that the ac­tiv­i­ties of com­ple­ment are not re­stricted to a lin­ear cas­cade of events but rather in­ volve a net­work of in­ter­ac­tions with other sys­tems, which to­gether co­or­ di­nate the host im­mune re­sponse to in­fec­tion, tis­sue in­jury, or other types of in­sult. These in­ter­ac­tions of com­ple­ment can am­plify in­nate im­mune and in­flam­ma­tory re­sponses through syn­ergy with TLRs, pro­vide a bar­rier against the spread of in­vad­ing bac­te­ria by po­ten­ti­at­ing lo­cal clot­ting, re­ plen­ish the im­mune sys­tem through mo­bi­li­za­tion of he­ma­to­poi­etic stem/ progenitor cells from the bone mar­row, and reg­u­late the ac­ti­va­tion and dif­fer­en­ti­a­tion of B cells and T-cell sub­sets.
Under in­flam­ma­tory con­di­tions, the GCF con­tains com­ple­ment at up to 70 to 80% of its con­cen­tra­tion in se­rum. However, the se­rum is not the only source of peri­odon­tal com­ple­ment, since it is also pro­duced lo­cally.
For in­stance, re­cruited leu­ko­cytes and es­pe­cially mac­ro­phages con­sti­tute a source of lo­cal pro­duc­tion of com­ple­ment com­po­nents. The peri­odon­tal pock­ets con­tain a func­tional com­ple­ment sys­tem, as at­tested by an­a­ly­ses of col­lected GCF sam­ples show­ing ro­bust com­ple­ment-dependent he­mo­lytic ac­tiv­ity. Therefore, the peri­odon­tal bac­te­ria con­stantly en­coun­ter ac­tive com­ple­ment, and as­so­ci­ated de­fense mech­a­nisms may con­trib­ute to hostmicrobe ho­meo­sta­sis in the healthy periodontium. In the set­ting of chronic peri­odon­ti­tis, where bac­te­rial growth is un­checked, it can be rea­soned that the dis­ease-associated bac­te­ria em­ploy mech­a­nisms to re­sist the an­ti­mi­cro­ bial ac­tions of com­ple­ment. This topic is dealt with in Chapter 15.

Adaptive Immunity in the Periodontium
As dis­cussed above, the im­mune sys­tem pro­ac­tively pa­trols the periodon­ tium, which is in con­stant con­tact with the tooth-associated microbiota.
Adaptive im­mu­nity is also in­volved in the main­te­nance of peri­odon­tal tis­ sue ho­meo­sta­sis, and the healthy hu­man gin­giva con­tains a pre­dom­i­nantly
T-cell-rich in­flam­ma­tory in­fil­trate and APCs, mostly den­dritic cells. CD4+
Th cells form the ma­jor­ity of the T cells in healthy gin­gi­vae. These helper lym­pho­cytes com­prise dis­tinct ef­fec­tor and reg­u­la­tory sub­sets, namely,
Th1, Th2, Th17, and reg­u­la­tory T cells (Tregs). As de­tailed in Chapter 2, these sub­sets are in­duced by dis­tinct dif­fer­en­ti­a­tion pro­grams and are clas­ si­fied on the ba­sis of dis­tinct cy­to­kine pro­duc­tion pat­terns and func­tions.
Briefly, Th1 cells pro­duce gamma interferon (IFN-γ) and are pri­mar­ily re­ spon­si­ble for cell-me­di­ated im­mu­nity to in­tra­cel­lu­lar path­o­gens (bac­te­ria, pro­to­zo­ans, vi­ruses). Th2 cells pro­duce IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 and sup­port hu­moral im­mu­nity. The rel­a­tively re­cently de­scribed Th17 sub­set pro­duces
IL-17, IL-21, and IL-22 and me­di­ates re­sponses that re­in­force neu­tro­phil and in­nate im­mu­nity against ex­tra­cel­lu­lar bac­te­ria and fungi. Tregs pre­vent ex­ces­sive in­flam­ma­tion by sup­press­ing ef­fec­tor func­tions of Th1, Th2, and
Th17, in part through the pro­duc­tion of IL-10 and trans­form­ing growth fac­tor-β (TGF-β). Within the CD4+ T-cell com­part­ment of the healthy gin­ giva, about 10 to 15% of the cells are Tregs.
Th17 cells con­sti­tute the ma­jor source of IL-17 in both healthy and dis­eased hu­man gin­gi­vae. IL-17 is a key cy­to­kine that links T-cell ac­ti­va­ tion to neu­tro­phil mo­bi­li­za­tion and ac­ti­va­tion for pro­tec­tion against ex­ tra­cel­lu­lar bac­te­ria and fun­gal path­o­gens. Neutrophils, which are also found in the healthy gin­giva, can po­ten­ tially cross talk with adap­tive im­mune cells. In this re­gard, neu­tro­phils were shown to re­lease the che­mo­kines CCL2 and CCL20, which can se­ lec­tively re­cruit Th17 cells. Therefore, neu­tro­phils and Th17 cells can en­gage in in­ter­ac­tions for re­cip­ro­cal re­cruit­ment to the periodontium. Moreover, neu­tro­phils, as well as ep­i­the­lial cells, re­lease the cy­to­kines B-lymphocyte stim­u­la­tor (BLyS) and a pro­lif­er­a­tion-inducing li­gand (APRIL) that can en­hance the sur­vival, pro­lif­er­a­tion, and mat­u­ra­tion of B cells into plasma cells. The ex­pres­sion and re­lease of BLyS and APRIL can be in­duced by sev­eral mol­e­cules (e.g., gran­u­lo­cyte col­o­ny-stimulating fac­tor, LPS and other TLR li­gands, C5a, and im­mune com­plexes), the lev­els of which are con­sid­er­ably in­creased in in­flamed gin­gi­vae. This might, at least in part, ex­plain why the pres­ence of B cells (and plasma cells) is not prom­i­nent in a healthy periodontium as com­pared to dis­eased tis­sue. Consequently,
IgG an­ti­body lev­els are typ­i­cally el­e­vated in peri­odon­ti­tis pa­tients rel­a­tive to healthy con­trols, al­though the role of an­ti­bod­ies in peri­odon­tal health and dis­ease is un­cer­tain (more in Chapter 15).
A unique fea­ture of the periodontium com­pared with other mu­co­sal sites, such as the gas­tro­in­tes­ti­nal and re­spi­ra­tory tracts, is that it con­sists of both mu­co­sal and bone tis­sue. Thus, it is clear that the cel­lu­lar and mo­ lec­u­lar cross talk be­tween the peri­odon­tal im­mune and bone sys­tems can play a crit­i­cal role in the reg­u­la­tion of bone ho­meo­sta­sis. In gen­eral, what con­sti­tutes pro­tec­tive or ho­meo­static im­mu­nity against peri­odon­ti­tis has not yet been ad­e­quately elu­ci­dated. On the other hand, at least some as­ pects of the host re­sponse may re­sult in more harm than good, ow­ing to col­lat­eral re­lease of in­flam­ma­tory me­di­a­tors, an­ti­gen-antibody com­ plexes, tis­sue-destructive en­zymes, and cy­to­toxic sub­stances from ac­ti­ vated in­nate and adap­tive leu­ko­cytes (e.g., re­ac­tive ox­y­gen spe­cies), as well as the gen­er­a­tion of cleav­age prod­ucts of com­ple­ment ac­ti­va­tion that have chemo­attractant and cell-activating prop­er­ties. If the mi­cro­bial chal­ lenge is con­trolled by these mech­a­nisms, then the in­flam­ma­tion is re­solved and tis­sue heal­ing en­sues. In such cases, some ini­tial tis­sue dam­age would be a small price to pay. Intriguingly, sta­ble gin­gi­vi­tis, al­though tech­ni­cally a dis­ease, likely rep­re­sents a pro­tec­tive host re­sponse that pre­ vents tran­si­tion to peri­odon­ti­tis. If the in­flam­ma­tory re­sponse, how­ever, does not con­trol the dysbiotic in­sult, then the stage is set for per­sis­tent chronic in­flam­ma­tion and overt peri­odon­ti­tis, a topic that is com­pre­hen­ sively dis­cussed in Chapter 15.

K E Y  P O I N T S

The im­mune mech­a­nisms and mol­e­cules of the oral cav­ity de­rive from either the se­cre­tory or the sys­temic im­mune sys­tem. The for­mer en­ter the oral cav­ity through the se­cre­tions of the ma­jor and mi­nor sal­i­vary glands, whereas the lat­ter en­ter through the gin­gi­val crevicular flu­id.

The oral cav­ity con­tains an ar­ray of in­nate an­ti­mi­cro­bial fac­tors, se­creted by sal­i­vary glands and also by ep­i­the­lial cells and leu­ko­ cytes. These an­ti­mi­cro­bial mol­e­cules can kill or in­hibit the growth of mi­cro­or­gan­isms and have broad-spectrum an­ti­bac­te­rial, an­ti­fun­gal, and an­ti­vi­ral prop­er­ties.

The sin­gle most im­por­tant hu­moral me­di­a­tor of spe­cific oral mu­co­sal im­mu­nity is se­cre­tory IgA (S-IgA), which en­ters the oral cav­ity through the sal­i­vary se­cre­tions. Antibodies of this isotype bind to spe­cific mi­cro­bial tar­gets and thereby con­trol mi­cro­bial ad­her­ence to mu­co­sal sur­faces or neu­tral­ize tox­ins or en­zymes.

The in­duc­tion of B-cell re­sponses, which can lead to pro­duc­tion of
S-IgA an­ti­bod­ies in sa­liva, is ini­ti­ated at spe­cial­ized in­duc­tive sites of the mu­co­sal im­mune sys­tem, such as the in­tes­ti­nal Peyer’s patches and the ton­sils and ad­e­noids that form the Waldeyer’s pha­ryn­geal ring.

The ini­tial host re­sponse to the ma­tur­ing subgingival biofilm in­volves com­ple­ment ac­ti­va­tion, in­creased vas­cu­lar per­me­abil­ity and flow of in­flam­ma­tory ex­u­date (gin­gi­val crevicular fluid [GCF]), as well as che­mo­tac­tic re­cruit­ment of in­flam­ma­tory phago­cytes, mostly neu­tro­phils. As adap­tive im­mu­nity be­comes in­volved, ad­di­tional play­ers in­clude lym­pho­cytes (T cells, B cells, and plasma cells), which are largely con­fined to the un­der­ly­ing con­nec­tive tis­sue, and their prod­ucts, such as cy­to­kines and an­ti­bod­ies (pro­duced by plasma cells), which can tran­su­date to the crev­ice.

The co­or­di­nated and reg­u­lated re­cruit­ment of neu­tro­phils is cru­cial for peri­odon­tal tis­sue ho­meo­sta­sis, as ev­i­denced by the in­creased sus­cep­ti­bil­ity to, and se­ver­ity of, peri­odon­ti­tis in in­di­vid­u­als with neu­tro­phil de­fi­cien­cies with re­gard to ab­so­lute num­bers or traf­fi ck­ing ca­pac­ity. Conversely, when their re­cruit­ment is dysregulated and they are pres­ent in ex­ces­sively high num­bers, neu­tro­phils may cause col­lat­eral tis­sue dam­age through the re­lease of in­flam­ma­tory and toxic sub­stances, as well as tis­suedegrading en­zymes.

In ad­di­tion to form­ing a me­chan­i­cal bar­rier, gin­gi­val ep­i­the­lial cells con­trib­ute to host de­fense by ex­press­ing an­ti­mi­cro­bial pep­tides (e.g., β-defensins) or im­mu­no­reg­u­la­tory mol­e­cules (cy­to­kines and che­mo­kines). The in­duc­tion of these host de­fense mol­e­cules de­pends largely on the ac­ti­va­tion of ep­i­the­lial Toll-like re­cep­tors (TLRs) by oral bac­te­ria.

Healthy hu­man gin­gi­vae con­tain a pre­dom­i­nantly T-cell-rich in­flam­ma­tory in­fil­trate and a net­work of an­ti­gen-presenting cells (APCs; den­dritic cells and mac­ro­phages) that or­ches­trate lo­cal im­mu­nity. In con­trast, min­i­mal num­bers of B cells and plasma cells are pres­ent in a healthy periodontium, and their pres­ence in high num­bers sig­ni­fies clin­i­cally overt in­flam­ma­tion (gin­gi­vi­tis or peri­odon­ti­tis).

Periodontal health rep­re­sents a dy­namic bal­anced state where the proinflammatory and an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tiv­i­ties of the host are op­ti­mally reg­u­lated to pre­vent un­war­ranted host re­ac­tions. In peri­odon­tally healthy sub­jects, the col­lec­tive ac­tion of in­nate and adap­tive im­mune re­sponses es­tab­lishes a sym­bi­otic re­la­tion­ship be­tween the host and the microbiota. Periodontal dis­ease may oc­cur in sus­cep­ti­ble in­di­vid­u­als when this ho­meo­static bal­ance is dis­rupt­ed.
