INTRODUCTION
The de­vel­op­ment of re­com­bi­nant DNA tech­nol­ogy in the 1970s and ar­ ray tech­nol­o­gies in the 1990s proved sig­nif­i­cant in ad­vanc­ing our knowl­ edge of the bio­chem­i­cal, phys­i­o­log­i­cal, and vir­u­lence prop­er­ties of oral mi­cro­or­gan­isms. The past de­cade has ush­ered in the “mul­ti-omics” rev­o­ lu­tion, which has dra­mat­i­cally re­shaped our un­der­stand­ing of host-­microbe in­ter­ac­tions and has pro­vided new in­sights into the mech­a­nisms by which bac­te­ria in­ter­act with and col­o­nize the host and cause dis­ease. These ad­ vances have also led to the de­vel­op­ment of mo­lec­u­lar di­ag­nos­tic pro­to­cols for more rapid iden­ti­fi­ca­tion of oral mi­cro­bial taxa and ap­pro­pri­ate mo­lec­ u­lar ther­a­peu­tic tar­gets.

The first two com­plete ge­nome se­quences for the oral bac­te­ria Strepto­ coccus mutans and Fusobacterium nucleatum were pub­lished in 2002.
Since then, the nu­cle­o­tide se­quences of the en­tire ge­nomes of a range of oral bac­te­ria have been ob­tained, in­clud­ing those of Porphyromonas gin­ givalis, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Treponema denticola,
Actinomyces naeslundii, Prevotella intermedia and can­di­date di­vi­sion

GENOMICS
Genomics is the study of all­ genes, in­clud­ing their func­tions and reg­u­la­tory el­e­ments, pres­ent in the ge­nomes of or­gan­isms. The ge­nome of an or­gan­ism con­sists of its chro­mo­somes and any ex­tra­chro­mo­somal el­e­ments that may be pres­ent. Most bac­te­rial ge­nomes have a sin­gle cir­cu­lar mol­e­cule of DNA and typ­i­cally only a sin­gle or­i­gin of rep­li­ca­tion per cir­cu­lar chro­mo­some.
While there are var­i­a­tions be­tween spe­cies, the or­i­gin of rep­li­ca­tion se­ quences share some com­mon char­ac­ter­is­tics, such as a high AT con­tent (AT re­peats are easier to sep­a­rate than GC re­peats). Determination of the nu­cle­ o­tide base se­quence of an or­gan­ism’s com­plete ge­nome is a ma­jor goal of ge­no­mics and has been made pos­si­ble by rapid ad­vances in au­to­mated
DNA se­quenc­ing and bioinformatics. The com­pleted ge­nome se­quences of a mul­ti­tude of mi­cro­or­gan­isms are avail­able for anal­y­sis through the internet (e.g., http://www.​tigr.​org/​ and http://www.​ncbi.​nlm​.​nih.​gov/​bioproject/​).
Given the ease with which ge­nomes may now be se­quenced, many thou­ sands of bac­te­rial ge­nomes will be se­quenced over the next few years.
In ad­di­tion to ge­no­mic DNA sequences, 16S rRNA se­quences can be used as iden­tity sig­na­tures to study the evo­lu­tion­ary re­lat­ed­ness of bac­te­ ria. The 16S rRNA genes are highly con­served be­tween dif­fer­ent spe­cies due to the slow rate of evo­lu­tion. The genes en­cod­ing the 5S, 16S, and
23S rRNAs of bac­te­ria are mostly or­ga­nized in op­er­ons. The copy num­ ber of rRNA op­er­ons per bac­te­rial ge­nome var­ies from 1 to 15. Transcription of the rRNA op­er­ons cor­re­sponds to the avail­abil­ity of nu­tri­tional re­sources and can rep­re­sent al­most 70% of to­tal tran­scrip­tion dur­ing pe­ri­ ods of fast growth.
A bac­te­rial spe­cies is de­fined by its pan-genome, which in­cludes the full com­ple­ment of genes in a par­tic­u­lar spe­cies of bac­te­ria. The pan-genome con­sists of a core ge­nome and a dis­pens­able/variable ge­nome. The core ge­ nome con­tains the set of genes re­quired for ba­sic cel­lu­lar func­tions, and these are pres­ent in all­ strains of the same bac­te­rial spe­cies. The ma­jor­ity of genes mak­ing up the core ge­nome be­long to groups with house­keep­ing func­tions, many of which are es­sen­tial genes, for ex­am­ple, those en­cod­ing pro­teins in­volved in cell en­ve­lope/cell wall me­tab­o­lism, nu­tri­ent uti­li­za­tion, and global gene reg­u­la­tion. These genes are evo­lu­tion­arily more con­served than the rest of the ge­nome. Core genes are not sub­ject to fre­quent hor­i­zon­ tal trans­fer and gen­er­ally do not con­trib­ute to the adap­tive evo­lu­tion of bac­te­rial path­o­gens. Identifying the core com­ple­ment of genes in a bac­te­rial spe­cies is of­ten the first step in pop­u­la­tion ge­no­mics stud­ies. The var­i­able ge­nome in­cludes the genes that are dif­fer­en­tially pres­ent be­tween in­di­vid­ ual strains or unique to a given strain. Some genes of the var­i­able ge­nome may play a role in ad­ap­ta­tion to spe­cial growth con­di­tions, such as those in­volved in the col­o­ni­za­tion of new eco­log­i­cal niches, sym­bi­o­sis, host cell in­ter­ac­tions, and path­o­ge­nic­ity. In ad­di­tion, many of the strain-specific genes en­code hy­po­thet­i­cal or un­known func­tion pro­teins. Gene Organization and Regulation
A bac­te­rial ge­nome usu­ally con­tains thou­sands of genes, which are not all­ ex­pressed at the same time. Most of these genes have spe­cific roles and are ex­pressed only in cer­tain met­a­bolic states (e.g., en­zymes needed for amino acid syn­the­sis) or in re­sponse to a spe­cific en­vi­ron­men­tal con­di­tion (e.g., overexpression of some spe­cific genes in P. gingivalis un­der ox­i­da­ tive stress con­di­tions). However, some genes, also known as house­keep­ing genes, are con­sti­tu­tively ex­pressed, as their prod­ucts are re­quired to main­ tain crit­i­cal func­tions such as en­ergy pro­duc­tion, DNA rep­li­ca­tion, and tran­scrip­tion.

In bac­te­ria, genes can be tran­scribed in­di­vid­u­ally or or­ga­nized into op­er­ons. Each in­di­vid­u­ally tran­scribed gene has its own pro­moter (a nu­ cle­o­tide se­quence where RNA po­ly­mer­ase binds to ini­ti­ate tran­scrip­tion) and other up­stream reg­u­la­tory DNA se­quences. An op­eron is a group of genes, lo­cated next to each other on a stretch of DNA, that are cotranscribed un­der the con­trol of one pro­moter. This re­sults in mRNA that will trans­late into mul­ti­ple pro­teins. In ad­di­tion to the pro­moter, con­trol of gene tran­scrip­tion of­ten in­volves an op­er­a­tor, a DNA se­quence lo­cated ei­ther just next to the pro­moter or within the pro­moter, that is the bind­ ing site for re­pres­sor pro­teins. The gene en­cod­ing the re­pres­sor pro­tein may be lo­cated near the op­eron or else­where in the chro­mo­some. Some op­er­ons are in­duc­ible in that they can only be turned on or ex­pressed in the pres­ence of a spe­cific mol­e­cule known as in­ducer. Others are re­press­ ible, mean­ing that their ex­pres­sion can be downregulated or turned off once the spe­cific re­pres­sor pro­tein is pres­ent. Such tightly reg­u­lated gene ex­pres­sion sys­tems en­sure that pro­teins are ex­pressed only when needed for rel­e­vant func­tions.
The clas­sic ex­am­ple of an in­duc­ible op­eron is the lac op­eron in Esch­ erichia coli dis­cov­ered by No­bel lau­re­ates François Ja­cob and Jacques
Monod. The lac op­eron con­tains three struc­tural genes that en­code pro­ teins re­quired for lac­tose trans­port and me­tab­o­lism, namely, lacZ, lacY, and lacA. The lacZ gene en­codes β-galactosidase, which con­verts lac­tose into glu­cose and ga­lac­tose; the lacY gene en­codes β-galactoside per­me­ ase, which trans­ports lac­tose into the cell; and the lacA gene codes for
β-galactoside transacetylase. The transacetylase is not cru­cial for me­tab­ o­lism of lac­tose but plays a role in the de­tox­i­fi­ca­tion of com­po­nents that may come in­side the cell dur­ing lac­tose trans­port. So to­gether, these gene prod­ucts first trans­port lac­tose into the cell from the lo­cal en­vi­ron­ment and then break it down to mono­sac­cha­rides for use as an en­ergy source.
The lac op­eron also has sev­eral fea­tures to en­sure its co­or­di­nated reg­u­la­ tion. These in­clude a sin­gle pro­moter up­stream of three lac genes, an op­er­a­tor se­quence be­tween the pro­moter and struc­tural genes that helps de­ter­mine if tran­scrip­tion will take place, and a ter­mi­na­tor that is lo­ cated down­stream of lac struc­tural genes and tells the tran­scrip­tional ma­chin­ery to ter­mi­nate tran­scrip­tion. One more im­por­tant reg­u­la­tory el­e­ment is the lac reg­u­la­tory gene (lacI), which is found just out­side the lac op­eron and has its own pro­moter and ter­mi­na­tor se­quences. Expression of the lac op­eron is reg­u­lated by the pres­ence of lac­tose it­self. In the ab­sence of lac­tose (Fig. 2A), the re­pres­sor pro­tein en­coded by the lacI gene binds to the lac op­er­a­tor. This pre­vents RNA po­ly­mer­ase from bind­ing to the pro­moter, thus in­hib­it­ing the tran­scrip­tion of lac genes.
When lac­tose is pres­ent in the sur­round­ing me­dium (Fig. 2A), a lac­tose me­tab­o­lite named allolactose (gen­er­ated through β-galactosidase ac­tiv­ity pres­ent be­fore in­duc­tion) binds to the re­pres­sor pro­tein. Now the re­pres­ sor-allolactose com­plex is not ­able to bind to the op­er­a­tor, thus al­low­ing
RNA po­ly­mer­ase to bind to lac pro­moter and tran­scribe the lac op­eron genes (de­re­pres­sion).
The lac op­eron also has a sec­ond con­trol mech­a­nism that is in re­sponse to glu­cose con­cen­tra­tion. E. coli only uses lac­tose when the en­vi­ron­men­tal glu­cose con­cen­tra­tion is low. In con­trast, the E. coli trp op­eron, which reg­u­lates the bio­syn­the­sis of tryp­to­phan from the pre­cur­sor chorismic acid, has been used as a model to un­der­stand the mech­a­nism of a re­press­ible op­eron. The trp op­eron con­ tains a pro­moter, an op­er­a­tor lo­cated at the 3′ end of the pro­moter, along with five struc­tural genes—trpE, trpD, trpC, trpB, and trpA—which are re­quired for the bio­syn­the­sis of tryp­to­phan. The re­pres­sor TrpR pro­tein is en­coded by the trpR reg­u­la­tor gene, which is not part of the trp op­eron and is lo­cated else­where in the bac­te­rial chro­mo­some. When tryp­to­phan (the co­re­pres­sor) is pres­ent (Fig. 2B), it binds to the re­pres­sor pro­tein, caus­ing a change in the re­pres­sor struc­ture and al­low­ing the re­pres­sor to bind to the op­er­a­tor. This in­hib­its RNA po­ly­mer­ase bind­ing to the pro­ moter and there­fore pre­vents tran­scrip­tion of trp genes. When tryp­to­phan is de­pleted and needs to be syn­the­sized, the free re­pres­sor pro­tein in its in­ac­tive con­for­ma­tion is un­able to bind to the op­er­a­tor (Fig. 2B), thus al­ low­ing RNA po­ly­mer­ase to bind the trp pro­moter and lead­ing to the syn­ the­sis of Trp pro­teins.
Control of tran­scrip­tion in bac­te­ria is achieved at many lev­els, in­clud­ ing reg­u­la­tion of tran­scrip­tion ini­ti­a­tion and ter­mi­na­tion, as well as the avail­abil­ity and sta­bil­ity of mRNAs. While some of these mech­a­nisms are tightly con­trolled, tran­scrip­tion ter­mi­na­tion is some­times an in­ef­fi­cient pro­cess and the RNA po­ly­mer­ase reads through the ter­mi­na­tor se­quence.
In fact, bac­te­ria al­ways have an­ti­ter­mi­na­tion mech­a­nisms that in­hibit the func­tions of the ter­mi­na­tor and ter­mi­na­tion fac­tors re­sult­ing in in­creased ex­pres­sion of down­stream genes. Two clas­ses of reg­u­la­tors help RNA po­ ly­mer­ase to read through ter­mi­na­tors. The larger first class acts di­rectly on a ter­mi­na­tor but does not af­fect RNA po­ly­mer­ase, whereas in the sec­ ond class, RNA po­ly­mer­ase by­passes the oth­er­wise func­tional ter­mi­na­tion sig­nals. In the sec­ond case, RNA po­ly­mer­ase reads through many sub­se­ quent ter­mi­na­tors. Why such a cen­tral pro­cess should be in­ef­fi­cient is not clear, al­though sev­eral in­stances of tran­scrip­tional readthrough have been re­ported to ben­e­fit the cell. A well-cited ex­am­ple of tran­scrip­tion an­ti­ter­ mi­na­tion is the trp op­eron in E. coli, where an­ti­ter­mi­na­tion oc­curs by a stalled ri­bo­some. In most Gram-negative bac­te­ria, the ri­bo­some con­trols ex­pres­sion of amino acid bio­syn­the­sis op­er­ons in re­sponse to the avail­ abil­ity of the cog­nate amino acid by sens­ing the level of charged tRNA. Two-component reg­u­la­tory sys­tems (TCS) (see Chapter 1) reg­u­late global gene ex­pres­sion in bac­te­ria in re­sponse to their en­vi­ron­ment. TCS al­low bac­te­ria to sense the phys­i­cal and/or chem­i­cal changes in their en­vi­ ron­ment and then trans­fer the sig­nal through the cy­to­plasm to the bac­te­rial nu­cle­oid where genes are reg­u­lated ac­cord­ingly. In a num­ber of cases, it has been found that the TCS re­sponse reg­u­la­tor (RR) pro­teins are not as­so­ci­ ated with a sen­sor ki­nase. These are known as “or­phan re­sponse regulators,” as they lack a cog­nate his­ti­dine ki­nase as a phos­phor­y­la­tion part­ner.
The re­sponse mech­a­nism for or­phan RRs is not yet un­der­stood. There is ev­i­dence in­di­cat­ing that some or­phan RRs may as­so­ci­ate with other his­ ti­dine ki­nases in the ge­nome. However, other re­ports sug­gest that or­phan
RRs may be con­trolled by phos­phor­y­la­tion at ser­ine, thre­o­nine, or ty­ro­ sine res­i­dues. Orphan RRs are in­volved in var­i­ous cel­lu­lar func­tions, such as ox­i­da­tive stress re­sponses, in biofilm for­ma­tion and in vir­u­lence.

Transcriptional Initiation
Most bac­te­rial gene reg­u­la­tion takes place at the tran­scrip­tional level (see
Table 1). During tran­scrip­tion, RNA po­ly­mer­ase chem­i­cally syn­the­sizes
RNA through four ba­sic steps: pro­moter rec­og­ni­tion, chain ini­ti­a­tion, chain elon­ga­tion, and ter­mi­na­tion. Promoter rec­og­ni­tion in­volves bind­ing of RNA po­ly­mer­ase to DNA near to a struc­tural gene. Sigma fac­tors (e.g., house­keep­ing sigma fac­tor, ơ70 in E. coli) di­rect RNA po­ly­mer­ase to spe­cific pro­moter se­quences in the DNA. In the ca­non­i­cal E. coli sys­tem, pro­mot­ ers have four dif­fer­ent se­quence el­e­ments nec­es­sary for proper rec­og­ni­ tion. Measurement of Gene Expression
How a gene is reg­u­lated (e.g., in­duc­tion or re­pres­sion) in re­sponse to en­ vi­ron­men­tal sig­nals can shed light on its func­tion. For ex­am­ple, genes that en­code iron-scavenging pro­teins are usu­ally upregulated in re­sponse to low en­vi­ron­men­tal iron con­cen­tra­tions. However, the ac­tiv­i­ties of many bac­te­rial gene prod­ucts are gen­er­ally dif­fi­cult to as­say or de­tect, un­less they have eas­ily mea­sur­able en­zy­mic ac­tiv­ity or a spe­cific an­ti­body to the protein product is available. Thus, sur­ro­gate genes, re­ferred to as “re­ porter genes,” which en­code well-studied and eas­ily as­sayed en­zymes, are of­ten used in reg­u­la­tion stud­ies. To as­sess the ef­fects of spe­cific en­vi­ron­ men­tal sig­nals (or any pa­ram­e­ter) on the ex­pres­sion of a gene of in­ter­est, a con­struct is de­signed such that a re­porter gene is in­cor­po­rated im­me­di­ ately down­stream of (and at times as a re­place­ment for) the gene of in­ter­ est. The re­sult is a tran­scrip­tional fu­sion in which the reg­u­la­tory re­gion of the gene of in­ter­est now con­trols the ex­pres­sion of the re­porter gene. This type of fu­sion is pro­duced by clon­ing a promoterless re­porter gene down­ stream of the pro­moter of the gene of in­ter­est. One of the more com­mon re­porter genes used to study gene reg­u­la­tion in oral mi­cro­or­gan­isms is lacZ (which en­codes β-galactosidase; see above). When a chro­mo­genic sub­strate is pro­vided in place of the nor­mal sub­strate lac­tose, LacZ ac­tiv­ity can be mea­sured by a sim­ple col­or-change as­say.
Another com­monly used re­porter gene is cat, which en­codes chlor­am­ phen­i­col acetyltransferase, an en­zyme that in­ac­ti­vates the an­ti­bi­otic chlor­ am­phen­i­col via the ad­di­tion of an ace­tyl group to the mol­e­cule. The re­porter is par­tic­u­larly use­ful be­cause the ac­tiv­ity of the en­zyme can be mea­sured di­rectly or by de­ter­min­ing the level of an­ti­bi­otic re­sis­tance.

GENOMIC STRUCTURE
Knowledge of the con­tig­u­ous DNA se­quence of a bac­te­ri­um’s chro­mo­ some pro­vi­des im­por­tant in­sights into its ge­netic makeup. Interpretation of com­pleted ge­nome se­quences is ac­com­plished by first iden­ti­fy­ing the pre­dicted pro­tein cod­ing se­quences or ORFs. Small Noncoding RNAs
Bacterial non­cod­ing small RNAs (sRNAs) in­clude a large group of RNA mol­e­cules that are not trans­lated to a pro­tein prod­uct. On av­er­age, some­ where be­tween 100 to 200 sRNAs have been dis­cov­ered per sin­gle bac­te­ rial ge­nome. Most of these sRNAs are en­coded in the 5′ and 3′ un­trans­lated re­gions, as well as in intergenic re­gions of the ge­nome; they lack pro­teincoding se­quences and con­tain in­de­pen­dent pro­mot­ers and ter­mi­na­tors.
sRNAs gen­er­ally range in size from 40 to 500 nu­cle­o­tides (nt). Recently,
RNA-Seq (global high-throughput se­quenc­ing of RNA; see be­low) has pro­vided a new tool for global de­tec­tion of po­ten­tial sRNAs. As more at­ ten­tion has been fo­cused upon de­ter­min­ing the roles of sRNAs in bac­te­ rial phys­i­ol­ogy, it is be­com­ing clear that many of these RNAs are likely to be riboregulatory RNAs in­volved in post­tran­scrip­tional con­trol of gene ex­pres­sion.

Generally, sRNAs re­ press gene ex­pres­sion by in­hib­it­ing tar­get mRNA trans­la­tion. For ex­am­ ple, sRNA may in­hibit ex­pres­sion of mRNA through base pairing of its 3′ hair­pin loops with the tar­get mRNAs, or through base pair­ing with the 5′ end to the cod­ing se­quence of the tar­get mRNA (Fig. 3A). The toxin and the an­ti­toxin were tran­scribed across di­rect tan­dem re­peats, pro­vid­ing a re­gion of per­ fect com­ple­men­tar­ity for both RNAs that fa­cil­i­tate seed pair­ing in­hi­bi­ tion of toxin trans­la­tion.

CRISPR
CRISPR are es­sen­tial com­po­nents of a bac­te­rial nu­cleic ac­id-based adap­ tive im­mune sys­tem and pro­vide ge­netic mem­ory of in­fec­tion against in­ vad­ing for­eign el­e­ments (such as vi­ruses, plas­mids, and trans­po­sons) via
RNA-guided DNA cleav­age. CRISPR loci con­sist of a se­ries of di­rect re­ peats sep­a­rated at reg­u­lar in­ter­vals by unique spacer se­quences. Spacers are short se­quences orig­i­nat­ing from for­eign (or some­times self) nu­cleic ac­ids that re­flect the past in­fec­tions and events in­volv­ing in­tro­duc­tion of nu­cleic ac­ids to the bac­te­rial cell. By add­ing new spac­ers, new for­eign DNA can be rec­og­nized; there­fore, spac­ers are used as rec­og­ni­tion el­e­ments to find match­ing for­eign ge­nomes and de­stroy them. CRISPRs were orig­i­nally iden­ti­fied in the E. coli ge­nome in 1987 and have since been found in about
45% of all­ bac­te­rial spe­cies.
CRISPRs func­tion to­gether with cas genes that typ­i­cally flank CRISPR loci in the ge­nome, and the en­tire path­way is con­se­quently re­ferred to as
CRISPR-Cas. CRISPR-Cas-mediated adap­tive im­mu­nity pro­ceeds in three stag­es: ac­qui­si­tion of for­eign DNA, CRISPR RNA bio­gen­e­sis, and in­ter­ fer­ence. During ac­qui­si­tion, new spac­ers from an in­vad­ing vi­rus or plas­ mid (protospacer) are in­te­grated pref­er­en­tially at the leader end of the
CRISPR lo­cus. Protospacers are flanked by a short mo­tif called the protospacer ad­ja­cent mo­tif (PAM) lo­cated on the 3′ or 5′ end of for­eign DNA. In next step, the CRISPR lo­cus (with for­eign protospacer and PAM) gets tran­ scribed and pro­cessed into short ma­ture CRISPR-derived RNAs (crRNAs).
In type II sys­tems, a trans-acting an­ti­sense RNA (tracrRNA) with com­ ple­men­tar­ity to the CRISPR RNA re­peat se­quence forms an RNA du­plex that is rec­og­nized and cleaved by RN­ase III. This cleav­age in­ter­me­di­ate is fur­ther pro­cessed at the 5′ end, re­sult­ing in a ma­ture, ∼40-nt crRNA.
In the fi­nal step, crRNAs as­so­ci­ates with Cas pro­teins to form a large
­ri­bo­nu­cleo­pro­tein-targeting com­plex that can rec­og­nize in­vad­ing nu­cleic ac­ids. The Cas en­do­nu­cle­ase then cleaves tar­get nu­cleic ac­ids that are marked for deg­ra­da­tion via com­ple­men­tary base pair­ing be­tween the crRNA spacer se­quence and a com­ple­men­tary se­quence from the for­eign DNA.
CRISPR-Cas im­mune sys­tems can dis­crim­i­nate be­tween self and non­self se­quences dur­ing in­ter­fer­ence via PAM rec­og­ni­tion. Targets found in for­ eign DNA con­tain a PAM and are tar­geted, whereas match­ing tar­gets in the CRISPR lo­cus it­self, from which the crRNA is tran­scribed, do not con­tain a PAM and are avoid­ed.

NATIVE Cas9-MEDIATED GENOME EDITING
CRISPR/Cas9 has been mod­i­fied to edit ge­nomes (Fig. 4). By de­liv­er­ing the
Cas9 nu­cle­ase com­plexed with a syn­thetic guide RNA (sgRNA) into a cell, the cell’s ge­nome can be cut at a de­sired lo­ca­tion, al­low­ing ex­ist­ing genes to be re­moved and/or new ones added. Cas9-mediated ge­nome ed­it­ing
­de­pends on two se­quen­tial steps. First, ge­no­mic DNA is cleaved by Cas9 at a spe­cific site de­ter­mined by the 20-nt tar­get rec­og­ni­tion se­quence on gRNA. Second, dou­ble-stranded breaks (DSBs) gen­er­ated by Cas9 are li­gated by na­tive DNA re­pair sys­tems, na­tive non­ho­mol­o­gous end join­ing (NHEJ), or tem­plate-dependent ho­mol­o­gy-directed re­pair (HDR). Because
NHEJ is an er­ror-prone re­pair path­way that re­sults in small in­ser­tions or de­le­tions, the ORF is dis­rupted and the gene be­comes in­ac­ti­vated. When an ed­it­ing tem­plate that bears ho­mol­ogy to se­quences flank­ing the tar­ geted site is used, the DSBs can be pre­cisely re­paired by HDR, gen­er­at­ing de­fined de­le­tions, in­ser­tions, and nu­cle­o­tide sub­sti­tu­tions. The use of mul­ ti­ple sgRNAs si­mul­ta­neously al­lows mul­ti­ple tar­get­ing.

Tn-Seq
Random trans­po­son mu­ta­gen­e­sis is a pow­er­ful tech­nique for dis­sect­ing the ge­netic ba­sis of bac­te­rial func­tions (e.g., the vir­u­lence of bac­te­rial path­o­gens). With the in­ven­tion of next-generation se­quenc­ing, ge­no­mic data be­came in­creas­ingly avail­able, and knowl­edge of gene func­tion is now the lim­it­ing fac­tor in our un­der­stand­ing of the role of genes. By cou­ pling fun­da­men­tal trans­po­son mu­ta­gen­e­sis to next-generation se­quenc­ ing, re­search­ers have de­vel­oped a widely ap­pli­ca­ble high-throughput tool for gene dis­rup­tion, named Tn-Seq, for trans­po­son se­quenc­ing. This has great po­ten­tial for elu­ci­dat­ing gene func­tion, link­ing ge­no­types to phe­no­types, and re­solv­ing com­plex path­ways in mi­cro­or­gan­isms. Tn-Seq cre­ates a large pool of highly sat­u­rated mu­tant li­brar­ies, and the com­par­ a­tive con­tri­bu­tions of bac­te­rial genes can be as­sessed fol­low­ing se­lec­tion for fit­ness in dif­fer­ent en­vi­ron­ments, through mas­sive par­al­lel se­quenc­ ing. The ap­proach is based on the con­struc­tion of a sat­u­rated Mariner trans­po­son (Himar 1 C9) in­ser­tion li­brary. The only pref­er­ence for Mariner trans­po­si­tion is in­ser­tion of the trans­po­son DNA at “TA” sites, which are abun­dant through­out­ all­ ge­nomes. Upon growth of the li­brary un­der a test con­di­tion of in­ter­est (in vi­tro or in vi­vo), in­ser­tion mu­tants with a lower fit­ness de­crease in fre­quency in the pop­u­la­tion, while other mu­ tants, de­pend­ing on the ef­fect of the trans­po­son in­ser­tion, re­main the same or in­crease in fre­quency. Changes in fre­quency are de­ter­mined by se­quenc­ing the trans­po­son-flanking re­gions en masse. Subsequently, fit­ ness of ev­ery in­di­vid­ual gene knock­out­ in a bac­te­rial ge­nome can be cal­cu­lat­ed.
In ad­di­tion to its util­ity in de­ter­min­ing the fit­ness con­tri­bu­tion of genes, Tn-Seq is well suited for sub­se­quently iden­ti­fy­ing ge­nome-wide ge­ netic in­ter­ac­tions. To iden­tify in­ter­ac­tions be­tween a gene of in­ter­est (query gene) and the rest of the ge­nome, the query gene is de­leted, and sub­se­quently a sat­u­rated trans­po­son li­brary is in­tro­duced in this query gene de­le­tion back­ground. The fit­ness of each dou­ble mu­tant is de­ter­mined by Tn-Seq and com­pared to the ex­pected fit­ness value, which is the prod­uct of the in­di­ vid­ual fit­ness val­ues pre­vi­ously de­ter­mined us­ing Tn-Seq in the wild-type back­ground. A dou­ble mu­tant fit­ness value that de­vi­ates from the ex­pected value re­veals a ge­netic in­ter­ac­tion. PROTEOMICS
In some in­stances, genes may be tran­scribed but not trans­lated into pro­ tein. Furthermore, num­bers of mRNA cop­ies may not nec­es­sar­ily re­flect the num­ber of func­tional pro­tein mol­e­cules, or their ac­tiv­ity. Thus, in­sights into such fac­tors as the rel­a­tive abun­dance of a pro­tein prod­uct, post­trans­ la­tional mod­i­fi­ca­tion, sub­cel­lu­lar lo­cal­i­za­tion, turn­over, and in­ter­ac­tion with other pro­teins can only be mon­i­tored by anal­y­sis of the pro­tein pro­file.
The tech­nol­ogy for mon­i­tor­ing pro­tein ex­pres­sion is named pro­te­o­mics, de­fined as the iden­ti­fi­ca­tion and quan­ti­ta­tion of the com­plete set of pro­ teins, in both space and time, that is syn­the­sized un­der a given set of con­ di­tions.
There are two main ap­proaches to pro­te­o­mics. One is the ex­pres­sion model in which all­ pro­teins are an­a­lyzed, and the other is the cell map model in which only a se­lected set of pro­teins, like com­plexes, are stud­ied. In the ex­pres­sion model, changes in the pat­terns of ex­pres­sion in re­sponse to dif­fer­ent con­di­tions, or in a mu­tant strain, are ob­served quan­ti­ta­tively for a large num­ber of pro­teins within a cell. This makes it pos­si­ble to iden­tify dif­fer­en­tially ex­pressed pro­teins on a global scale. It also al­lows de­duc­tions of en­tire reg­u­la­tory net­works by iden­ti­fy­ing pro­teins that may un­dergo co­or­di­nated changes of ex­pres­sion. One goal of the cell map model is to iden­tify pro­teins that in­ter­act with or form com­plexes with other pro­teins.
By doc­u­ment­ing the phys­i­cal in­ter­ac­tion of pro­teins, the as­so­ci­a­tions of pro­teins in par­tic­u­lar path­ways can be de­duced.

Methods for Protein Identification
The anal­y­sis of ge­nome-encoded pro­teins re­lies on the use of sev­eral tech­ nol­o­gies. One of the more com­monly used tech­niques is two-dimensional gel elec­tro­pho­re­sis. This tech­nique pro­vi­des higher res­o­lu­tion than onedimensional so­dium dodecyl sul­fate-polyacrylamide gel elec­tro­pho­re­sis (PAGE) anal­y­sis, as it sep­a­rates pro­teins both in terms of their iso­elec­tric points and mo­lec­u­lar mass. Methods of pro­tein iden­ti­fi­ca­tion in­clude im­ mu­no­blot­ting, pep­tide se­quenc­ing fol­low­ing Edman deg­ra­da­tion, de­ter­mi­na­ tion of amino acid com­po­si­tion, ma­trix-assisted la­ser de­sorp­tion/ionization mass spec­trom­e­try, and electrospray ion­i­za­tion. The last two meth­ods, which rely on the com­par­i­son of pep­tide mass fin­ger­prints, are fast and re­ quire only pi­co­mole amounts of pro­teins. The pep­tide se­quences ob­tained can be com­pu­ta­tion­ally matched with the pre­dicted amino acid se­quences of the ge­no­mic ORFs to iden­tify the pro­tein spot.
Mass spec­trom­e­try is one of the ver­sa­tile meth­ods of study­ing pro­teins in a whole cell. This method avoids two-dimensional gels al­to­gether and can de­ter­mine the whole pro­tein com­ple­ment of the cell, in­clud­ing iden­ti­ fi­ca­tion, mod­i­fi­ca­tion, quan­ti­fi­ca­tion, and lo­cal­i­za­tion of pro­teins. After whole cells or cel­lu­lar frac­tions are subjected to pro­teo­lytic di­ges­tion (usu­ally tryp­sin), the re­sult­ing pep­tide mix­tures are sep­a­rated by liq­uid chro­ma­tog­ra­phy and then electrosprayed for tan­dem mass spec­tro­met­ric anal­y­sis, al­low­ing de­ter­mi­na­tion of the amino acid se­quence. Tens of thou­sands of pep­tides elute over a rel­a­tively short time, and mass spec­tro­ m­e­ters have been de­vel­oped to pro­vide higher sen­si­tiv­ity, se­quenc­ing speed, and res­o­lu­tion.

Posttranslational Modifications
Posttranslational mod­i­fi­ca­tions are pro­cess­ing events in the cells that can al­to­gether change the prop­er­ties of a pro­tein by co­va­lent ad­di­tion of an ad­di­tional func­tional com­pound such as phos­phate. Mass spec­tros­copy is a very im­por­tant tool that can iden­tify and an­a­lyze the ex­pressed pro­teins with post­trans­la­tional mod­i­fi­ca­tions, such as al­kyl­ation, gly­co­syl­a­tion and phos­phor­y­la­tion, that play im­por­tant reg­u­la­tory roles in the cel­lu­lar en­vi­ ron­ment. It is al­ways chal­leng­ing to an­a­lyze pep­tide mix­tures de­rived from com­plex pro­teins, and af­fin­i­ty-based meth­ods are es­pe­cially use­ful to re­duce the pep­tide com­plex­i­ties. For ex­am­ple, phosphopeptides can be se­ lec­tively cap­tured through their neg­a­tively charged phosphogroup on im­ mo­bi­lized-metal af­fin­ity (IMAC) col­umns. This method can also be used in com­bi­na­tion with phosphotyrosine pro­tein af­fin­ity pu­ri­fi­ca­tion.
However, the iden­ti­fi­ca­tion and char­ac­ter­iza­tion of post­trans­la­tional mod­ i­fi­ca­tions constitute a daunt­ing task due to lim­ited knowl­edge of un­der­ly­ing en­zy­matic re­ac­tions and fi­nal im­pact on pro­tein dy­nam­ics over­all. Hence, bioinformatics are of­ten used in con­junc­tion with em­pir­i­cal data col­lec­tion.
There are cur­rently nu­mer­ous bioinformatics tools for pre­dic­tion of post­ trans­la­tional mod­i­fi­ca­tions, and these can be broadly cat­e­go­rized into four types. The first cat­e­gory in­volves gen­eral post­trans­la­tional modification-­ related re­sources that per­form rapid reg­u­lar ex­pres­sion pat­tern searches in or­der to pre­dict eukaryotic linear motifs (ELMs) in pro­tein se­quences. Category 2 cov­ers dif­fer­ent neu­ral net­work pre­dic­tion tools, which in­clude phos­phor­y­la­tion-related pre­dic­tion serv­ers (like NetPhos and NetPhosK),

Protein Expression Levels
Another an­a­lyt­i­cal ap­proach to pro­tein quan­ti­ta­tion is the use of la­bel­ing tags. Proteins in met­a­bol­i­cally ac­tive bac­te­ria can be dif­fer­en­tially la­beled by in­cor­po­ra­tion of 15N or 13C, which can then be dis­crim­i­nated from un­ la­beled pep­tides through the in­cre­men­tal in­crease in mass. Alternatively, sta­ble iso­topes can be in­tro­duced into pro­teins by chem­i­cal de­riv­a­ti­za­tion of ei­ther the car­boxyl or amino ter­mi­nus; for ex­am­ple, 18O can be in­cor­ po­rated into the car­boxyl ter­mi­nus of pep­tides by pro­te­ase-catalyzed cleav­age of the am­ide bond. Isobaric tags are com­pounds with the same mass but which frag­ment dif­fer­ently to yield re­porter ions of dif­fer­ent masses. The re­sults ob­tained with tags can show the rel­a­tive abun­dance of the whole pro­te­ome of the path­o­gen un­der var­i­ous ex­per­i­men­tal con­ di­tions. At the same time, they can also iden­tify host pro­teins that may be up- or downregulated. The rel­a­tive quan­ti­fi­ca­tion of pro­teins pro­duced by microbes and by hosts aids in the un­der­stand­ing of the in­fec­tious pro­cess and of host cell re­sponses. With the in­creas­ing avail­abil­ity of com­plete ge­ nome se­quences for oral bac­te­ria, global pro­te­o­mics an­a­ly­ses are be­com­ ing stan­dard in the lab­o­ra­to­ry.

METABOLOMICS AND METABONOMICS

Metabolic Fingerprint
Ultimately the func­tion, role, and in­ter­ac­tions of a bac­te­rium in an eco­ sys­tem are dic­tated by the prod­ucts of its met­a­bolic ac­tiv­ity. Therefore, the study of the met­a­bolic ca­pa­bil­i­ties of bac­te­ria is of great im­por­tance. The metabolome is the com­plete set of small-molecule me­tab­o­lites found within a bi­o­log­i­cal sam­ple. This usu­ally re­fers to a sin­gle or­gan­ism un­der a de­fined set of con­di­tions, al­though it can also ap­ply to com­mu­ni­ties of or­gan­isms, for ex­am­ple, a subgingival polymicrobial biofilm. Metabolites in­clude met­a­bolic in­ter­me­di­ates and end prod­ucts, sig­nal­ing mol­e­cules, and sec­ond­ary me­tab­o­lites; they are usu­ally de­fined as any mol­e­cule less than 1 kDa in size. The de­ter­mi­na­tion of the me­tab­o­lites within an or­gan­ ism is known as its metabolomic fin­ger­print, and in some in­stances this can be used, like hu­man fingerprints, to iden­tify the bac­te­rium. The ma­jor chal­lenges in metabolome an­a­ly­ses are the com­plex­ity and va­ri­ety of me­ tab­o­lites, which lead to dif­fi­cul­ties in iden­ti­fi­ca­tion and in­ter­pre­ta­tion.
Metabolomics, in con­junc­tion with the rapid ad­vances in ge­no­mics, transcriptomics, and pro­tein pre­dic­tion soft­ware, now en­ables the con­ struc­tion of the met­a­bolic path­ways em­ployed by bac­te­ria un­der a spe­cific set of en­vi­ron­men­tal con­di­tions. Metabolite pro­fil­ing ap­proaches have also been used to iden­tify novel or un­an­tic­i­pated me­tab­o­lites and met­a­ bolic path­ways in bac­te­ria. Oral bac­te­ria have rel­a­tively small ge­nomes that usu­ally en­code 1,500 to 3,000 pro­teins and as such have rel­a­tively lim­ited met­a­bolic flex­i­bil­ity. Metabolic footprinting (or exometabolomics), as op­posed to fin­ger­ print­ing, is the study of the ex­tra­cel­lu­lar me­tab­o­lites pro­duced by a de­ fined or­gan­ism or com­mu­nity of or­gan­isms. Metabolic footprinting de­fi­nes the range of mol­ e­cules that the bac­te­rium uses to in­ter­act, ei­ther pos­i­tively or neg­a­tively, with the host. The spe­cies/genetic com­po­si­tion of com­mu­ni­ties can have sig­nif­i­cant in­flu­ences on the over­all end prod­ucts of me­tab­o­lism.

Metabonomics
Distinct from metabolomics, metabonomics is de­fined as “the quan­ti­ta­tive mea­sure­ment of the dy­namic multiparametric met­a­bolic re­sponse of liv­ ing sys­tems to path­o­phys­i­o­log­i­cal stim­uli or ge­netic mod­i­fi­ca­tion.” The sub­tle dif­fer­ence be­tween metabolomics and metabonomics is still a cause of con­fu­sion; how­ever, metabonomics ex­tends met­a­bolic fin­ger­print­ing to in­clude in­for­ma­tion about per­tur­ba­tions of me­tab­o­lism. Metabonomics is an ex­cel­lent tool for de­ter­min­ing the ef­fects of ge­netic ma­nip­u­la­tion, such as gene de­le­tion, in­ac­ti­va­tion, or in­ser­tion. For ex­am­ple, in tan­dem with trans­po­son mu­ta­gen­e­sis where a li­brary of mu­tant strains with in­ac­ti­vated genes are pro­duced, metabonomic anal­y­sis can pre­dict the func­tion of un­ known genes by com­par­i­son of the metabolomes of the mu­tant strains with the wild-type.
There are two ma­jor tech­ni­cal ap­proaches to met­a­bolic fin­ger­print­ing, footprinting, and metabonomics. The first is the use of mass ­spec­trom­e­try (MS), usu­ally cou­pled with a high-pressure liq­uid ­chro­ma­tog­ra­phy (LC) sep­a­ra­tion step to deconvolute the sam­ples. Indeed, given the large num­ ber of me­tab­o­lites in many sam­ples, two sep­a­ra­tion steps are uti­lized (tandem LC). The first sep­a­ra­tion can be based on charge or charge and size us­ing an an­ion/cation ex­change col­umn and the sec­ond based on hy­dro­pho­bic/hydrophilic char­ac­ter­is­tics of the mol­e­cule us­ing re­versedphase LC prior to iden­ti­fi­ca­tion on a mass spec­trom­e­ter. The sec­ond ap­proach to met­a­bolic an­a­ly­ses is the use of nu­clear mag­netic res­o­nance (NMR) spec­tros­copy. NMR does not rely on sep­a­ra­tion of the me­tab­o­ lites, mak­ing sam­ple prep­a­ra­tion sim­pler than for MS an­a­ly­ses; as a fur­ther ad­van­tage, the sam­ple can be re­cov­ered for fur­ther an­a­ly­ses. A wide range of me­tab­o­lites can be mea­sured si­mul­ta­neously. The main dis­ad­van­tage of NMR is rel­a­tively in­sen­si­tiv­ity com­pared with MS-based tech­niques.

An op­eron is a set of genes lo­cated con­tig­u­ously on a DNA stretch tran­scribed un­der the con­trol of one pro­moter. Operons may be in­duc­ible (can be in­duced to ex­press in the pres­ence of an in­ducer mol­e­cule) or re­press­ible (can be turned off in the pres­ence of a re­pres­sor pro­tein).

The ac­tiv­ity of a gene (the amount of mRNA pro­duced) can be mea­sured by clon­ing the pro­moter re­gion (the bind­ing site for RNA po­ly­mer­ase), along with up­stream reg­u­la­tory se­quences next to a re­porter gene, and re­turn­ing this con­struct to the chro­mo­some.
The re­porter gene gen­er­ally en­codes an en­zyme for which sub­strate ac­cu­mu­la­tion is eas­ily mea­sured. Bacterial strains con­tain­ing such pro­mot­er-reporter con­structs can be tested in an­i­mal mod­els of dis­ease, al­low­ing the de­tec­tion of genes that are ex­pressed in vi­vo.
