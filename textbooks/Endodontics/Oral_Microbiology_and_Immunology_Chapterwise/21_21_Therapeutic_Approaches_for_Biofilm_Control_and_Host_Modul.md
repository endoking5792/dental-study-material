INTRODUCTION
The mouth is one of the most com­plex en­vi­ron­ments in na­ture, where a myr­iad of bi­o­log­i­cal in­ter­ac­tions and phys­i­co­chem­i­cal events oc­cur among a va­ri­ety of bio­mol­e­cules and mi­cro­or­gan­isms and biotic or abi­otic host sur­faces. It har­bors a com­plex com­po­si­tion of host-derived bio­mol­e­cules (e.g., sal­i­vary gly­co­pro­teins), hard and soft tis­sue sur­faces (e.g., teeth and gin­gi­val/mucosal tis­sues), and a di­verse microbiota. In par­tic­u­lar, an en­vi­ ron­ment ex­ists that is con­du­cive to se­lec­tive ad­he­sion and biofilm de­vel­ op­ment of spe­cific mi­cro­bial com­mu­ni­ties on nonshedding nat­u­ral or in­tro­duced sur­faces and in­ter­faces, such as enamel, re­stor­ative ma­te­ri­als, den­tures, and den­tal im­plants.

Oral sur­faces are coated with sal­i­vary pel­li­cle to which par­tic­u­lar oral mi­cro­or­gan­isms known as “early col­o­niz­ers” bind av­idly. Biofilm for­ma­ tion oc­curs as these early col­o­niz­ers coadhere with other spe­cies to de­ velop com­plex com­mu­ni­ties. Depending on the diet, the ge­netic and im­mune sta­tus of the host, and var­i­ous sys­temic fac­tors, this ini­tial col­o­niz­ing com­ mu­nity can tran­si­tion from a health-associated state to a highly path­o­genic state. Our knowl­edge of the phys­i­co­chem­i­cal prop­er­ties of bac­te­ria-surface in­ter­ac­tions and the mech­a­nisms by which sal­i­vary mol­e­cules sub­stan­tially af­fect these bi­o­log­i­cal prop­er­ties is in­creas­ing. These ad­vances are pro­vid­ ing valu­able new in­sights into the pro­cesses of bac­te­rial ad­he­sion and biofilm for­ma­tion, which can en­able ap­proaches to mod­u­late the ef­fi­cacy of ther­a­peu­tic agents.

In this chap­ter, we fo­cus on cur­rent and pro­spec­tive ther­a­peu­tic ap­ proaches to mod­u­late host, path­o­gen, and ex­ter­nal fac­tors in­volved in oral biofilm for­ma­tion and de­vel­op­ment and de­scribe the lat­est strat­e­gies to con­trol den­tal car­ies and peri­odon­tal dis­eases, the two most prev­a­lent and costly oral mi­crobe-driven dis­eases world­wide.

Bacterial ad­he­sion takes place af­ter a sal­i­vary con­di­ tion­ing film or ac­quired pel­li­cle has formed. This film, which de­vel­ops im­me­di­ately upon ex­po­sure to sa­liva, con­sists of a thin acel­lu­lar or­ganic layer, with a hy­drated thick­ness of about 0.07 and 0.10 μm. The in­tro­ duc­tion of re­stor­ative and im­plant ma­te­ri­als into the oral cav­ity cre­ates an ad­di­tion to the oral sur­face area and ex­tends the va­ri­ety of sur­faces for oral mi­crobes to in­ter­act with. These sur­faces have dif­fer­ing phys­i­co­chem­ i­cal prop­er­ties and con­se­quently show al­tered in­ter­ac­tions with oral mi­ crobes, as well as with tis­sues and im­mune cells. Biomaterials such as den­tal im­plants and ti­ta­nium fix­a­tion plates are also sub­ject to ad­sorp­tion of plasma pro­teins and other bio­mol­e­cules from gin­gi­val crevicular fluid. The bac­te­rial ad­he­sion pro­cess is mod­u­lated by the var­ied prop­er­ties of these coated sur­faces, both biotic and abi­otic. In ad­di­tion, the host im­mune de­fense sys­tem in­ter­acts dif­fer­ently with bac­te­ri­ally col­o­nized for­eign ma­ te­ri­als com­pared to tis­sue in­fec­tions.
Oral bio­films, clin­i­cally termed den­tal plaque on tooth sur­faces, form on all­ sur­faces ex­posed in the hu­man oral cav­ity. Biofilm for­ma­tion, through bac­te­rial ad­he­sion and the pro­duc­tion of ex­tra­cel­lu­lar po­ly­meric sub­stances (EPS), is con­sid­ered to be a sur­vival mech­a­nism, pro­tect­ing the mi­cro­or­gan­isms against an­ti­mi­cro­bial at­tack and me­chan­i­cal de­tach­ment.
This pro­cess oc­curs re­gard­less of the sur­face to which oral bac­te­ria ad­ here, rais­ing the ques­tion as to how bac­te­ria ac­tu­ally “know” they are on a sur­face and start their adap­tive re­sponse to a ses­sile life style. It has been ar­gued that, in the ab­sence of vi­sual, au­di­tory, and ol­fac­tory per­cep­tion, ad­ her­ing bac­te­ria be­come aware of their ses­sile state by sens­ing mem­brane stresses aris­ing from mi­nor cell wall de­for­ma­tion.
Bacterial at­tach­ment oc­curs through ei­ther non­spe­cific or highly spe­cific li­gand re­cep­tor bind­ing (e.g., via adhesin-pellicle re­cep­tor in­ter­ac­tions; see
Chapter 5). This bind­ing cre­ates ad­he­sion forces lead­ing to de­for­ma­tion of the bac­te­rial cell wall, stress­ing the mem­brane and al­low­ing the or­gan­ ism to “sense” the sur­face to which it ad­heres (mechanosensing). Current lit­er­a­ture sug­gests that three ad­he­sion force re­gimes dic­tate bac­te­rial re­ sponse to a sub­stra­tum (Fig 1). In the plank­tonic re­gime, ad­he­sion forces are ex­tremely weak. Weakly ad­her­ing bac­te­ria do not de­tect that they are on a sur­face and do not adapt their plank­tonic phe­no­type (Fig. 1, left panel).
In the in­ter­ac­tion re­gime (Fig. 1, cen­ter panel), the strength of ad­he­sion forces is var­i­able and bac­te­ria re­spond to en­vi­ron­men­tal stresses by en­cas­ing them­selves in a pro­tec­tive biofilm, with EPS pro­duc­tion help­ing to gen­er­ate bal­ance be­tween ad­he­sion forces and pre­vail­ing en­vi­ron­men­tal shear. This re­gime rep­re­sents the pro­tec­tive biofilm mode of growth of bac­te­ria. In the le­thal re­gime (Fig. 1, right panel), strong ad­he­sion forces in­duce mem­ brane de­for­ma­tion that causes stress de­ac­ti­va­tion of the ad­her­ing bac­te­ ria, lead­ing to re­duced growth and cell death, as oc­cur­ring on pos­i­tively charged, al­kyl­ated sur­fac­es.
Further to their role in me­di­at­ing bac­te­rial re­sponse to at­tach­ment to a sur­face, these force re­gimes may ex­tend to in­ter­ac­tions be­tween bac­te­ria and thus even­tu­ally con­trol the tran­si­tion from the ini­tial ad­her­ing bac­te­ria to a ma­ture biofilm. In ad­di­tion to in­ter­ac­tions with the sub­strate, biofilm de­vel­op­ment is equally de­pen­dent upon in­ter­ac­tions be­tween or­gan­isms,

However, this means of intermicrobial com­mu­ni­ca­tion is lim­ited by dif­fu­sion of auto­ inducers through a biofilm. QS is thus most ef­fi­cient when com­mu­ni­cat­ing bac­te­ria are in close proximity. Oral mi­cro­or­gan­isms also re­lease DNA as a pro­tec­tive mech­a­nism to sta­bi­lize the ar­chi­tec­tural in­teg­rity of bio­films and to fa­cil­i­tate for­ma­tion of mul­ti­spe­cies pop­u­la­tions.

ANTIBIOFILM APPROACHES TO CONTROL ORAL DISEASES
Around 60% of hu­man in­fec­tions treated by phy­si­cians are caused by bac­ te­rial bio­films. This has pro­found im­pli­ca­tions for treat­ment mo­dal­i­ties, as le­thal or in­hib­it­ing con­cen­tra­tions of an­ti­bi­ot­ics against biofilm bac­te­ria are sig­nif­i­cantly in­creased, by up to 1,000-fold. One con­trib­u­tory fac­tor for this may be fail­ure to pen­e­trate into EPS, al­low­ing bac­te­ria re­sid­ing in the depths of a biofilm to sur­vive an­ti­bi­otic treat­ment. However, more im­por­ tantly, an­ti­mi­cro­bial re­sis­tance is de­vel­op­ing in many spe­cies of path­o­genic mi­cro­or­gan­isms be­cause of pro­longed an­ti­bi­otic us­age and ge­netic se­lec­ tion for re­sis­tant forms. This is fa­cil­i­tated by ex­change of an­ti­bi­otic re­ sis­tance genes be­tween mi­cro­or­gan­isms. Biofilms pro­vide an ex­cel­lent en­vi­ron­ment for mi­cro­bial DNA ex­change, as cells are in close prox­im­ity and DNA can be trapped within the ex­tra­cel­lu­lar ma­trix. Antibiotic re­sis­ tance traits can also be dis­sem­i­nated through interbacterial DNA trans­fer, mak­ing bac­te­rial bio­films a source of an­ti­mi­cro­bial re­sis­tance de­vel­op­ment.
Efforts are there­fore be­ing made to ex­plore new av­e­nues for ef­fec­tive kill­ ing of biofilm bac­te­ria by meth­ods that do not nec­es­sar­ily in­volve the use of con­ven­tional an­ti­bi­ot­ics. These char­ac­ter­is­ti­cally in­hibit spe­cific cel­lu­lar met­a­bolic path­ways but are ren­dered less ef­fec­tive by the com­plex­ity of biofilm struc­ture, com­po­si­tion, and mi­cro­en­vi­ron­men­tal and phys­i­o­log­i­cal het­ero­ge­ne­ity.
Many var­ied strat­e­gies to con­trol or in­hibit the dif­fer­ent phases of biofilm for­ma­tion have been put for­ward. Here we fo­cus on strat­e­gies di­rected to­wards in­hib­it­ing the ini­tial ad­he­sion of bac­te­ria to pre­vent bio­films from form­ing. Such strat­e­gies can tar­get sub­stra­tum sur­faces, mi­cro­bial cell sur­ faces, or their in­ter­play. One means to ac­com­plish this is to uti­lize syn­thetic or nat­u­ral bio­mol­e­cules as in­hib­i­tors. Other ap­proaches in­clude vac­cines to gen­er­ate an­ti­bod­ies that tar­get bac­te­rial sur­face com­po­nents and pre­vent ad­he­sion and biofilm ini­ti­a­tion and strat­e­gies that mod­u­late host re­sponses to be more ef­fec­tive against oral path­o­gens.

Oral Surface Modification
A wide range of oral health care prod­ucts are avail­able con­tain­ing che­ mo­ther­a­peu­tic agents, in­clud­ing an­ti­mi­cro­bi­als to fa­cil­i­tate con­trol of biofilm for­ma­tion. The abil­ity of an­ti­mi­cro­bial agents to be re­tained in the oral cav­ity (substantivity) is im­por­tant for long-lasting clin­i­cal ef­fec­tive­ ness. Some agents are ­able to ad­sorb to oral soft tis­sues, and, with a low de­sorp­tion rate, their ac­tiv­i­ties in sa­liva are more pro­longed. Also, biofilm re­main­ing on oral sur­faces af­ter brush­ing with an­ti­bac­te­rial tooth­pastes will act as a hous­ing for the an­ti­mi­cro­bial agents and con­trib­ute to their substantivity and slow re­lease be­tween oral care ses­sions.
The broad-spectrum an­ti­mi­cro­bial chlor­hex­i­dine (CHX) can ad­sorb to oral sur­faces and in­hibit bac­te­ria fol­low­ing re­lease and is com­monly used in clin­i­cal prac­tice. However, oral biofilm bac­te­ria with re­duced sus­ cep­ti­bil­ity to CHX have emerged, and these can also be re­sis­tant to mul­ti­ ple antimicrobials. Resistant spe­cies, when cul­ti­vated in vi­tro, re­mained vi­a­ble af­ter ex­po­sure to un­di­luted com­mer­cial CHX mouth­wash. When for­eign ma­te­ri­als, in­clud­ing osseointegratable oral im­plants, are im­planted into the body, there is com­pe­ti­tion be­tween bac­te­ria and tis­sue to ad­here to the ma­te­rial. Ideally, bio­ma­te­rial im­plants or de­vices should be ­able to re­tain host im­mune sys­tem com­po­nents com­pe­tent in clear­ance, fa­cil­i­tate host tis­sue in­te­gra­tion, pre­vent mi­cro­bial ad­he­sion and growth, and kill any at­tached mi­cro­or­gan­isms. Moreover, for ef­fec­tive in­fec­tion pro­tec­tion, this func­tion­al­ity should last for a clin­i­cally rel­e­vant du­ra­tion.
However, de­sign­ing sur­faces to both re­pel and kill bac­te­ria while at the same time pro­mot­ing tis­sue cell ad­he­sion is chal­leng­ing. A trend has emerged, there­fore, to­wards the de­sign of sur­faces with mul­ti­ple func­tion­al­i­ties that re­li­ably se­lect host cells over mi­crobes. Surface mod­i­fi­ca­tions of bio­ma­te­ ri­als to min­i­mize sus­cep­ti­bil­ity to biofilm ac­cu­mu­la­tion have been widely ex­plored in ex­per­i­men­tal mod­els in vi­tro. Developments in­clude de­sign of du­al-function coat­ings that uti­lize unique sur­face chem­is­tries and to­po­ log­i­cal pat­terns to com­prise both ad­he­sive and non­ad­he­sive sites in den­ si­ties and con­fig­u­ra­tions that se­lec­tively en­cour­age tis­sue cell at­tach­ment, yet im­pede ad­he­sion of much smaller mi­cro­or­gan­isms. In ad­di­tion to the com­pe­ti­tion be­tween bac­te­rial ad­he­sion and tis­sue in­te­gra­tion on an im­ planted bio­ma­te­rial sur­face, in­ter­ac­tions also ex­ist be­tween pro­teins and bac­te­ria.
One such di­rec­tion for pre­vent­ing bac­te­rial re­ten­tion on a sur­face in­ volves al­ter­ing the physiocochemical prop­er­ties of the sub­stra­tum to in­ flu­ence pro­tein ad­sorp­tion. Some pro­teins can form spe­cific li­gand re­cep­tor bonds with cer­tain bac­te­rial strains while oth­ers in­ter­act only weakly (non­spe­cif­i­cally). To this end, bio­ma­te­rial sur­faces can there­fore be mod­i­ fied to block the spe­cific bind­ing be­tween path­o­genic bac­te­ria and their in­ter­act­ing pro­teins and/or hin­der non­spe­cific ad­he­sion by de­po­si­tion of a pre­se­lected pro­tein layer on the ma­te­rial prior to its place­ment into the body. These strat­e­gies also need to con­sider the re­quire­ment for ad­sorbed pro­teins to re­duce fric­tion be­tween ar­tic­u­lat­ing sur­faces in the oral cav­i­ty.
Another prom­is­ing area has been the de­vel­op­ment of non-leaching ster­ ile sur­faces mod­i­fied with co­va­lently at­tached al­kyl­ated polycations. These com­pounds can kill a broad range of air­borne or wa­ter­borne mi­cro­or­gan­ isms, in­clud­ing bac­te­ria and fungi. Quaternary am­mo­nium com­pounds (QAC), for ex­am­ple, are com­monly used in an­ti­sep­tics, and their mech­a­ nism of ac­tion is il­lus­trated in Fig. 2. Polymers con­tain­ing QAC pos­sess the unique fea­ture of kill­ing bac­te­ria upon con­tact. Flexible po­ly­mers with long-chained, mod­er­ately hy­dro­pho­bic im­mo­bi­lized polycations can reach across the mi­cro­bial cell en­ve­lope, in­sert­ing their al­kyl chains into the plasma mem­brane, sub­se­quently pro­duc­ing leak­age, au­tol­y­sis, and bac­te­ rial death. This con­fers an ad­van­tage over an­ti­mi­cro­bi­al-releasing coat­ ings, which are widely used to yield high ini­tial an­ti­bi­otic con­cen­tra­tions around an im­plant or de­vice. These have the draw­back of a high-burst re­ lease im­me­di­ately upon in­ser­tion, fol­lowed by a lengthy tail-end re­lease that can ex­tend to sev­eral years and that has been as­so­ci­ated with the de­vel­op­ ment of an­ti­bi­otic re­sis­tance.

EPS Inhibition
The EPS ma­trix is a mul­ti­func­tional scaf­fold that sup­ports and pro­tects bac­te­ria em­bed­ded in bio­films. It is crit­i­cal for both the life­style and drug tol­er­ance of bio­films. The EPS ma­trix, pro­duced by the mi­cro­or­gan­isms, can con­tain exopolysaccharides, pro­teins, lip­ids, nu­cleic ac­ids, lipoteichoic ac­ids, and even li­po­po­ly­sac­cha­rides. Microbial EPS can be sur­face as­so­ ci­ated or se­cret­ed––that is, EPSs are pro­duced lo­cally or are de­pos­ited onto abi­otic and biotic sur­faces, thereby dy­nam­i­cally me­di­at­ing mi­cro­bial ad­ her­ence to the sur­face, cell-cell ad­he­sion, and the for­ma­tion of cell clus­ ters or microcolonies. In ad­di­tion to pro­mot­ing mi­cro­bial ad­he­sion, the ma­trix also pro­vi­des struc­tural in­teg­rity, co­he­sive­ness, and me­chan­i­cal sta­bil­ity. The EPS ma­trix can also mod­ify and de­lin­eate path­o­genic en­vi­ ron­ments (such as acidic pH and hyp­oxia) that are at­tri­butes as­so­ci­ated with many in­fec­tious dis­eases and med­i­cal com­pli­ca­tions. Because the
EPS pro­vi­des an im­por­tant struc­tural back­bone and per­forms es­sen­tial func­tions, tar­get­ing the EPS rep­re­sents an ex­is­ten­tial threat to bio­films.
Dissolution of the EPS ren­ders bac­te­ria with­out­ the pro­tec­tion con­ferred by ag­gre­ga­tion and the sur­round­ing ma­trix and makes them vul­ner­a­ble to host ef­fec­tors and an­ti­mi­cro­bial ther­a­pies. Thus, tar­get­ing of EPS by in­hib­it­ing pro­duc­tion and de­grad­ing the pro­duced EPS in es­tab­lished bio­ films (see be­low) could be an ef­fec­tive antibiofilm strat­e­gy.
Detailed char­ac­ter­iza­tion of path­ways reg­u­lat­ing biofilm EPS ma­trix gene ex­pres­sion has iden­ti­fied sev­eral ex­tra­cel­lu­lar and in­tra­cel­lu­lar sig­nal­ ing net­works as well as nonsignaling mech­a­nisms for ac­ti­va­tion of EPS pro­duc­tion. Overall, cyclic di-GMP and cyclic di-AMP have been shown to con­trol var­i­ous EPS-producing exo­en­zymes, po­ly­sac­cha­rides, and adhesins that are po­ten­tial tar­gets. These nu­cle­o­tide-signaling mol­e­cules have been shown to reg­u­late glu­can-producing exo­en­zymes (e.g., glucosyltransferase) in Streptococcus mutans, as well as the ag­gre­ga­tive exopolysaccharides in the Gram-negative bacterium Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Probiotic Strategies
As defined in a 2001 meeting convened by the Food and Agriculture Organization and the World Health Organization, probiotics re­fers to “live mi­cro­or­gan­isms, which, when ad­min­is­tered in ad­e­quate amounts, con­fer a health ben­e­fit on the host.” Increasing ev­i­dence fa­vors pro­bi­otic ther­ apy in the main­te­nance of oral health. Classic pro­bi­otic strains that be­long to the gen­era Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium have been tested in vi­vo and have been shown to re­duce ini­tial car­ies in chil­dren and to re­duce num­ bers of car­i­o­genic S. mutans organisms in young adults.
Probiotic bac­te­ria can hin­der the ad­he­sion of path­o­genic bac­te­ria by pas­sively oc­cu­py­ing the niche, ac­tively re­strict­ing the ad­he­sive ca­pa­bil­ity of path­o­gens to sur­faces or pro­duc­ing biosurfactants. Certain pro­bi­otic strains have been shown to in­hibit bac­te­rial ad­he­sion by mod­i­fy­ing the sal­i­vary con­di­tion­ing film and thus re­duc­ing the col­o­ni­za­tion ef­fi­ciency of path­o­genic bac­te­ria. Probiotic bac­te­ria may dis­play a higher af­fin­ity for oral sur­faces than oral path­o­gens and im­pede path­o­gen col­o­ni­za­tion through mod­u­la­tion of the mi­cro­bial biofilm com­mu­nity. Probiotic bac­te­ria that are
­able to out­com­pete path­o­gens for es­sen­tial nu­tri­ents or chem­i­cals re­quired for growth will con­trib­ute to a shift of the oral microbiome in a health­ier di­rec­tion. Probiotic bac­te­ria may also mod­u­late the host re­sponse (see
“Host Response Modulation in Oral Diseases”).
Despite the ap­peal­ing mul­ti­ple mech­a­nisms of ac­tion, in­sta­bil­ity of pro­bi­ot­ic-based prod­ucts due to their short shelf-life and com­plex stor­ age con­di­tions for live or­gan­isms have re­stricted prog­ress in the de­vel­op­ ment of these oral prod­ucts. Currently, ef­forts are di­rected at de­vel­op­ing novel tech­niques and prod­ucts that do not in­volve live or­gan­isms but achieve sim­i­lar pro­bi­otic ef­fects.

DISRUPTION OF BIOFILMS
Current chem­i­cal mo­dal­i­ties avail­able to dis­rupt oral bio­films are mostly com­prised of broad-spectrum an­ti­mi­cro­bi­als such as CHX and es­sen­tial oil-containing prod­ucts (e.g., Listerine). CHX is con­sid­ered the gold stan­ dard an­ti­mi­cro­bial agent for oral biofilm con­trol. Although CHX sup­ presses sal­i­vary lev­els of oral path­o­gens such as mutans group strep­to­cocci, it is only mod­er­ately ef­fec­tive against bio­films and con­se­quently has shown lim­ited use against oral dis­eases such as den­tal car­ies. CHX has ad­verse side ef­fects, in­clud­ing tooth stain­ing and pro­mot­ing cal­cu­lus for­ma­tion, and hence, it is not rec­om­mended for daily pre­ven­tive or ther­a­peu­tic use. Antimicrobial Peptides
Antibacterial ac­tiv­i­ties of an­ti­mi­cro­bial pep­tides (AMPs) are de­pen­dent on dif­fer­ent pep­tide prop­er­ties, in­clud­ing charge, hy­dro­pho­bic­ity, amphipathicity, sec­ond­ary struc­tures, and the pres­ence of ar­o­matic amino acid res­i­dues. Most AMPs pos­sess an over­all pos­i­tive net charge due to a high con­tent of ly­sine and ar­gi­nine res­i­dues, and this fa­cil­i­tates the in­ter­ ac­tion with neg­a­tively charged bac­te­rial mem­branes. Several stud­ies have dem­on­strated a clear cor­re­la­tion be­tween cationicity and an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tiv­i­ty.
The mode of ac­tion of AMPs is not fully un­der­stood, but it is be­lieved to be sim­i­lar to that of QAC in that their ma­jor tar­gets are the cy­to­plas­ mic mem­brane and in­tra­cel­lu­lar mol­e­cules. Antibacterial mech­a­nisms of
AMPs in­clude mem­brane dis­rup­tion, func­tional in­hi­bi­tion of pro­teins, and bind­ing to DNA. Interaction of AMPs with the EPS it­self is also of in­ter­est: cer­tain AMPs can be traf­ficked through pores formed in the lipid com­po­nent of the EPS, while oth­ers can dis­perse bio­films. In ad­di­tion to bind­ing with bac­te­rial mem­branes, cat­ionic AMPs can also bind to EPS ma­trix, which pre­vents their pen­e­tra­tion into the deeper lay­ers of the biofilm struc­ture.
AMPs can act ef­fec­tively in syn­ergy with con­ven­tional an­ti­bi­ot­ics against mul­ti­drug-resistant bac­te­ria. In ad­di­tion to co­ad­min­is­tra­tion of
AMPs and other an­ti­mi­cro­bial agents, a com­bi­na­tion of two AMPs and the us­age of chi­me­ric pep­tides showed en­hanced biofilm re­duc­tion, re­duced tox­ic­ity against hu­man eryth­ro­cytes, and a broad-spectrum an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tiv­ity both in vi­tro and in vi­vo. With re­gard to oral bio­films, com­bined treat­ment with CHX and the bac­te­rio­cin PsVP-10 pep­tide syn­er­gis­ti­cally re­duced bio­films of S. mutans. Lactoferrin, an iron-binding an­ti­mi­cro­bial pro­tein pres­ent in sa­liva and crevicular flu­ids, is in­volved in host de­fense against oral path­o­gens, in­clud­ing periodontopathogenic bac­te­ria. Lactoferrin can in­hibit ini­tial at­tach­ment of Streptococcus gordonii and S. mutans, plank­tonic growth of Porphyromonas gingivalis, and Prevotella intermedia and can sup­press biofilm for­ma­tion alone or in com­bi­na­tion with an­ti­ bi­ot­ics. In a small-scale clin­i­cal trial, pa­tients ad­min­is­tered bo­vine lactoferrin for 3 months showed a re­duc­tion of bac­te­rial num­bers within subgingival bio­films. These re­sults dem­on­strate the fea­si­bil­ity of uti­liz­ing AMPs for the treat­ment of sub­jects with peri­odon­tal dis­eases and as an ad­junct re­gime for oral hy­giene.
AMPs can mo­re­over be im­mo­bi­lized onto solid sur­faces (e.g., med­i­cal de­vices or im­plant sur­faces) to pre­vent nos­o­co­mial in­fec­tions. Chemical cou­pling via co­va­lent bond­ing of AMPs onto po­ly­mer brushes or into a hy­dro­gel net­work dem­on­strated good antiadhesive and bac­te­ri­cidal prop­ er­ties with­out­ ham­per­ing tis­sue com­pat­i­bil­ity. As with QACs, it is very dif­ fi­cult for bac­te­ria to de­velop re­sis­tance to AMPs, as they kill bac­te­rial cells through ac­tions on the en­tire cy­to­plas­mic mem­brane and act through a va­ri­ ety of mech­a­nisms af­fect­ing mul­ti­ple tar­gets. Although re­sis­tance to AMPs has been oc­ca­sion­ally re­ported, ac­qui­si­tion of re­sis­tance (e.g., by chang­ ing bac­te­rial sur­face charge or pro­teo­lytic cleav­age) is lim­ited and takes lon­ger to de­velop than re­sis­tance to con­ven­tional drugs.

EPS Matrix Degradation
Degradation of the EPS ma­trix of­fers the po­ten­tial to dis­rupt formed bio­ films with­out­ nec­es­sar­ily af­fect­ing the vi­a­bil­ity of the mi­cro­or­gan­isms.
However, this ap­proach needs to be coadministered with an­ti­mi­cro­bial ther­a­pies to kill dis­persed cells for max­i­mal ef­fi­cacy. Exopolysaccharidedegrading en­zymes such as glucanohydrolases (dex­tran­ase and mutanase), dispersin B, and gly­co­side hy­dro­lases are ca­pa­ble of dis­rupt­ing the ma­trix of var­i­ous path­o­genic bio­films, in­clud­ing those as­so­ci­ated with den­tal car­ ies. Similar to exopolysaccharide-de­grad­ing en­zymes, DN­ases can dis­ as­sem­ble bio­films by di­gest­ing the ex­tra­cel­lu­lar DNA (eDNA) pres­ent in bio­films of sev­eral dif­fer­ent bac­te­ria. Although ma­trix-degrading en­zymes can help dis­perse bac­te­ria in bio­films, the po­ten­tial syn­er­gis­tic in­ter­ac­tion, when com­bined with an­ti­mi­cro­bial agents, prom­ises to be a more ef­fec­tive ap­proach. Glucanohydrolases, glycoside hydrolases, and DN­ases can en­ hance an­ti­mi­cro­bial de­liv­ery and po­ten­ti­ate the kill­ing ef­fects of an­ti­bi­ot­ics or an­ti­mi­cro­bial pep­tides when used in com­bi­na­tion against pre­formed bio­films in vi­tro. Nevertheless, fur­ther stud­ies us­ing in vi­vo biofilm mod­els are re­quired to dem­on­strate en­hanced ef­fi­cacy of the com­bined ap­proach and to val­i­date the com­bined ap­proach clin­i­cal­ly.

Metabolic Strategies
In the in­ter­cel­lu­lar com­mu­ni­ca­tions in oral bio­films, nu­mer­ous fi­ne-tuned sig­nals are pro­duced that are evolved spe­cif­i­cally for the pur­pose of con­vey­ ing in­for­ma­tion from one cell to an­other. Chemical cues of these sig­nals are of­ten bac­te­ria of their own met­a­bolic prod­ucts. Oral bac­te­ria pres­ent in bio­films pro­vide their me­tab­o­lites as en­ergy sources for other mem­bers.
Metabolic com­mu­ni­ca­tions among oral bac­te­ria may oc­cur through the ex­cre­tion of a me­tab­o­lite by one or­gan­ism that can be sensed and sub­se­ quently used as a nu­tri­ent by a dif­fer­ent or­gan­ism, or through the break­ down of a sub­strate by the ex­tra­cel­lu­lar en­zy­matic ac­tiv­ity of one or­gan­ism that gen­er­ates bi­o­log­i­cally avail­able sub­strates for dif­fer­ent or­gan­isms.

Atmospheric-Pressure Nonthermal Plasma (Cold Plasma)
Plasma, the fourth phys­i­cal state, is a par­tially ion­ized gas formed by en­ergy sup­ply to a car­rier gas (e.g., ar­gon). The so-called cold plasma (CP) yields tem­per­a­tures around 40°C and is ap­plied at at­mo­spheric pres­sure. CP gen­ er­ates a range of ac­tive spe­cies, in­clud­ing re­ac­tive ox­y­gen spe­cies (ROS), re­ac­tive ni­tro­gen spe­cies (RNS), UV ra­di­a­tion, ions, elec­trons, ex­cited mol­e­ cules, and elec­tro­mag­netic fields––all­ of which can in­ac­ti­vate bac­te­ria.
The bac­te­rial re­sponse to CP is spe­cies-dependent, with Gram-negative or­gan­isms being more sus­cep­ti­ble to CP than Gram-positive or­gan­isms.
The ex­act mech­a­nisms of this pro­cess re­main un­clear. Three dif­fer­ent mecha­ nisms have been pro­posed: di­rect permeabilization of the cell mem­brane or wall, lead­ing to leak­age of cel­lu­lar com­po­nents, in­clud­ing po­tas­sium, nu­cleic ac­ids, and pro­teins; crit­i­cal dam­age of in­tra­cel­lu­lar pro­teins from ox­i­da­tive or nitrosative spe­cies; and di­rect chem­i­cal DNA dam­age. More re­cently, CP has been shown to in­ac­ti­vate es­tab­lished bio­films via in­hi­ bi­tion of QS-regulated vir­u­lence fac­tors, in­di­cat­ing yet an­other mode of ac­tion.

Antimicrobial nanomaterials have re­ceived in­creas­ing at­ten­tion due to their unique ad­van­tages com­pared to con­ven­tional an­ti­bi­otic agents, in­clud­ing tar­geted de­liv­ery, en­hanced cel­lu­lar in­ter­nal­i­za­tion, sol­u­bil­ity, and sus­tained drug release. Various in­or­ganic and or­ganic nanoconstructs are known to pos­sess an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tiv­i­ties used for the treat­ment and pre­ven­tion of in­fec­tious dis­eas­es.
Metals such as sil­ver, zinc, and cop­per and their ox­ides have been in­ cor­po­rated into a wide range of den­tal ma­te­rial ap­pli­ca­tions, with in­creas­ ing in­ter­est in the form of nano­scale metal pow­ders, due to the su­prem­acy of nano­par­ti­cles (NPs) in terms of in­creased sur­face area and pos­si­ble di­ rect in­ter­ac­tions with the bac­te­rial cell wall. TiO2 NPs are most com­monly used in food and in per­sonal care prod­ucts, as bright­en­ing agents. The in­ ges­tion of TiO2 via food is es­ti­mated to be rel­a­tively high, with half of this in nanoparticulate form. Zn and ZnO NPs have been sug­gested for use in den­tistry, but as yet with only lim­ited ap­pli­ca­tions due to its low ac­cep­ tance within the den­tal pro­fes­sion com­pared with other fields. SURFACE-ADAPTIVE, pH-RESPONSIVE STEALTH NANOCARRIERS
Permeation into bio­films is partly con­trolled by biofilm thick­ness. Diffusion works most ef­fi­ciently over small dis­tances of a few micrometers and is ex­po­nen­tially slower as par­ti­cle di­am­e­ter in­creases. While, the­o­ret­i­cally,
NPs may be small enough to per­me­ate into the po­rous min­eral struc­ture of the sal­i­vary film-enamel in­ter­face, it is likely that the biofilm formed on the sal­i­vary con­di­tion­ing film will be a sig­nif­i­cant bar­rier to NP dif­fu­sion.
Recently, a new type of po­ly­meric mi­cel­lar nanocarrier with stealth and sur­face-adaptive fea­tures was in­tro­duced. This novel mi­cel­lar nanocarrier shows en­hanced biofilm pen­e­tra­tion and ac­cu­mu­la­tion by elec­tro­static tar­get­ing at acidic pH to­ward neg­a­tively charged bac­te­rial cell sur­faces in a staph­y­lo­coc­cal biofilm. These com­bined func­tion­al­i­ties are re­al­ized by a mixed shell struc­ture, con­sist­ing of a hy­dro­philic po­ly(eth­yl­ene gly­col) (PEG) shell and pH-responsive po­ly(β-amino es­ter), which be­come pos­i­ tively charged only at low pH, such as at a car­i­o­genic biofilm site. Similar to the stealth nanocarrier, NPs with en­hanced se­lec­tiv­ity of drug ac­ti­va­tion or de­liv­ery to biofilm bac­te­ria can be constructed by incorporating stimulus-triggered mech­a­nisms, such as pH. Such ap­proaches would spare nor­mal tis­sues and the res­i­dent microbiota while tar­get­ing in­fec­tive agents within path­o­log­i­cal microniches. For ex­am­ple, iron ox­ide with en­zyme-like (cat­a­lytic) func­tion­al­ity show­ing po­tent ef­fects against oral bio­films has been de­vel­oped. Because iron ox­ide NP ac­ti­va­tion is trig­gered only in acidic con­di­tions, it pre­vents cat­a­lytic re­ac­tion at phys­i­o­log­i­cal (neu­tral) pH and un­mit­i­gated free-radical pro­duc­tion, thereby pro­vid­ing a bio­com­ pat­i­ble treat­ment. Rationale and Scope for Host Modulation Therapies in Periodontal Disease
As dis­cussed in Chapter 15, peri­odon­tal dis­ease rep­re­sents a dis­rup­tion of host-microbe ho­meo­sta­sis and has a com­plex eti­ol­ogy that acts at mul­ti­ ple lev­els: at the mi­cro­bial level, based on the pres­ence of dysbiotic mi­cro­ bial com­mu­ni­ties with po­ten­tial for de­struc­tive in­flam­ma­tion; at the host level, based on ge­netic fac­tors that may pre­dis­pose to or pro­tect from dis­ ease; and at the level of sys­temic health sta­tus and en­vi­ron­men­tal fac­tors (e.g., smok­ing, stress, diet) that mod­ify the host re­sponse in ei­ther a pro­tec­ tive di­rec­tion or de­struc­tive di­rec­tion. Whereas sus­cep­ti­bil­ity to peri­odon­ti­ tis can be re­duced if the un­der­ly­ing en­vi­ron­men­tal or ge­netic fac­tors can be dealt with (e.g., bone mar­row trans­plan­ta­tion can cure leu­ko­cyte ad­he­sion de­fi­cien­cy-associated peri­odon­ti­tis), these ap­proaches are out­side the scope of this sec­tion, which fo­cuses on the mod­u­la­tion of the host re­sponse us­ing vac­ci­na­tion and phar­ma­co­log­i­cal in­ter­ven­tions (Fig. 8).

Immunization against Periodontal Disease
Since the first es­sen­tial con­di­tion for vac­ci­na­tion against any bac­te­ri­ally in­ duced dis­ease is to de­fine the caus­a­tive agent(s), the con­cept of vac­ci­na­tion against peri­odon­ti­tis started to emerge only af­ter spe­cific mi­cro­or­gan­isms, such as P. gingivalis, Tannerella for­sythia, Treponema denticola, and Aggregatibacter actinomyctemcomitans were im­pli­cated as pu­ta­tive eti­o­logic agents in peri­odon­tal dis­eases in the late 1980s. Despite the im­por­tance of these spe­cies, re­cent ad­vance­ments have pro­vided ev­i­dence that peri­odon­ti­tis is ini­ti­ated by syn­er­gis­tic and dysbiotic mi­cro­bial com­mu­ni­ties rather than by specific periopathogens. Another chal­lenge in vac­cine de­vel­op­ment for peri­ odon­ti­tis is that, as stated above, the dis­ease pri­mar­ily re­sults from col­lat­ eral tis­sue dam­age of the host im­mune re­sponse rather than from di­rect bac­te­rial ac­tion. Therefore, key to suc­cess in de­vel­op­ing a vac­cine against this dis­ease is a good un­der­stand­ing of the mech­a­nisms that in­duce in­flam­ ma­tory tis­sue dam­age while gen­er­ally fail­ing to con­trol subgingival mi­cro­ bial com­mu­ni­ties. A vac­cine-induced an­ti­mi­cro­bial re­sponse should not cause sig­nif­i­cant dam­age to the periodontium.
The first at­tempts for vac­ci­na­tion against specific peri­odon­tal bac­te­ria (e.g., P. gingivalis) were per­formed in rats and uti­lized whole bac­te­rial cells or bro­ken-cell prep­a­ra­tions. However, due to the un­de­sir­able reactogenicity of these types of vac­cines, sub­se­quent ef­forts in an­i­mal mod­els have fo­cused on de­fined mi­cro­bial mol­e­cules or ge­net­i­cally en­gi­neered sub­units of them. Such sub­unit vac­cine ap­proaches have so far fo­cused mainly on
P. gingivalis vir­u­lence pro­teins, par­tic­u­larly its cys­te­ine pro­tein­ases (RgpA,

Some stud­ies have used Freund’s com­plete or in­com­ plete ad­ju­vant or chol­era toxin, while oth­ers have used ad­ju­vants al­ready ap­proved for hu­man use, such as monophosphoryl lipid A, ei­ther alone or sup­ple­mented with tre­ha­lose dicorynomycolate.

Strategies for Inhibition and Resolution of Periodontal Inflammation
The pur­pose of anti-inflammatory ther­a­pies is to break the self-sus­tained vi­cious circle that links mi­cro­bial dysbiosis and de­struc­tive in­flam­ma­tion and un­der­lies the chro­nic­ity of peri­odon­ti­tis (Fig. 8). An ef­fec­tive ther­apy can ide­ally re­store the bal­ance be­tween proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory me­di­a­tors, ar­rest the de­vel­op­ment of the dis­ease, and pro­mote an en­vi­ron­ ment that is con­du­cive to peri­odon­tal tis­sue re­pair. It would not be fea­si­ble to dis­cuss all­ pro­posed host re­sponse mod­u­la­tion ap­proaches here, most of which are at an ex­per­i­men­tal stage and have not yet been tested in clin­i­cal tri­als. Below is a brief sum­mary of some of the sys­temic and lo­cal ap­ proaches that have been tested or are cur­rently be­ing pur­sued as ad­junc­tive ther­a­pies in peri­odon­ti­tis (Fig. 8).
Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDS) block the cy­clo­ox­y­ gen­ase path­way of ar­a­chi­donic acid me­tab­o­lism. This leads to in­hi­bi­tion of the pro­duc­tion of pros­ta­glan­dins, in­clud­ing pros­ta­glan­din E2. Prostaglandin E2 is a va­so­di­la­tor and also pro­motes vas­cu­lar per­me­abil­ity and bone re­sorp­tion. It is pro­duced by var­i­ous res­i­dent and re­cruited cell types (e.g., fi­bro­blasts, mac­ro­phages, and neu­tro­phils) in the periodontium in re­sponse to li­po­po­ly­sac­cha­ride or proinflammatory cy­to­kines. Certain stud­ies have shown that NSAIDS could im­prove the clin­i­cal out­come of me­chan­i­cal peri­odon­tal treat­ment, al­though the re­sults de­cline rap­idly af­ter drug with­drawal. Overall, how­ever, equiv­o­cal re­sults have been re­ported for the sys­temic use of NSAIDS as an ad­junct to the treat­ment of hu­man peri­odon­ti­tis, and cur­rent for­mu­la­tions have se­ri­ous ad­verse ef­fects (e.g., prothrombotic side ef­fects and in­creased risk of gas­tro­in­ tes­ti­nal tract bleed­ing) that pre­clude their pro­longed use for peri­odon­tal ther­a­py.
Anticytokine treat­ments (i.e., to block the ac­tion of proinflammatory cy­to­kines such as tu­mor ne­cro­sis fac­tor [TNF], in­ter­leu­kin [IL]-1, or IL-17) have been tested in an­i­mal mod­els of peri­odon­ti­tis and have pro­duced prom­is­ing re­sults, which have thus con­firmed the in­volve­ment of these cy­to­kines in peri­odon­tal bone loss. Periodontitis shares com­mon in­flam­ma­ tory path­ways with rheu­ma­toid ar­thri­tis. In this con­text, a re­cent sys­tem­atic re­view an­a­lyzed the ef­fects of sev­eral an­ti­rheu­matic drugs on peri­odon­tal in­flam­ma­tion and re­lated bio­mark­ers in rheu­ma­toid pa­tients with peri­ odon­ti­tis. Such drugs in­clude infliximab (mono­clo­nal an­ti­body to TNF), etanercept (sol­u­ble form of TNF re­cep­tor), and anakinra (IL-1 re­cep­tor an­tag­o­nist). The study con­cluded that there is cur­rently lim­ited ev­i­dence to sug­gest that ex­ist­ing anticytokine ther­a­pies can re­duce peri­odon­tal in­ flam­ma­tion, at least in pa­tients with both peri­odon­ti­tis and rheu­ma­toid ar­thri­tis. The in­suf­fi­cient ev­i­dence was likely due to the small num­ber of stud­ies, which mo­re­over were not spe­cif­i­cally de­signed to ad­dress ef­fi­cacy in peri­odon­ti­tis.
The res­o­lu­tion of in­flam­ma­tion is a well-coordinated ac­tive pro­cess aim­ing to re­in­state tis­sue in­teg­rity and func­tion. In great part, in­flam­ma­ tion res­o­lu­tion de­pends upon in­hi­bi­tion of fur­ther in­flam­ma­tory leu­ko­cyte re­cruit­ment and clear­ance of ap­o­pto­tic neu­tro­phils by tis­sue phago­ cytes. These func­tions are pro­moted by spe­cial­ized proresolving lipid me­di­a­tors, which in­clude ar­a­chi­donic ac­id-derived lipoxins and ome­ga-3

Multiple stud­ ies have es­tab­lished the pro­tec­tive ef­fects of these com­pounds in an­i­mal mod­els of peri­odon­ti­tis, such as rab­bits, where re­gen­er­a­tion of re­sorbed bone was also ob­served. Human peri­odon­ti­tis is as­so­ci­ated with el­e­vated com­ple­ment ac­tiv­ ity. Consistent with this, ge­netic and phar­ma­co­log­i­cal stud­ies in ro­dents have im­pli­cated the cen­tral com­ple­ment com­po­nent C3 and down­stream sig­nal­ing path­ways in host-microbe in­ter­ac­tions that pro­mote dysbiosis and in­flam­ma­tory bone loss. Moreover, lo­cal treat­ment of non­hu­man pri­ma­tes with a C3 in­hib­i­tor (Cp40) was shown to block both lig­a­tureinduced and nat­u­rally oc­cur­ring chronic peri­odon­ti­tis. AMY-101, a clin­ i­cally de­vel­oped Cp40-based drug, is cur­rently un­der eval­u­a­tion as a po­ten­tial treat­ment of sev­eral in­flam­ma­tory con­di­tions. With re­gard to peri­odon­ti­tis, fu­ture clin­i­cal tri­als with AMY-101 will de­ter­mine whether com­ple­ment in­hi­bi­tion can be an ef­fec­tive ad­junc­tive ther­apy in this oral dis­ease.
Because com­ple­ment cross-talks with and am­pli­fies Toll-like re­cep­tor (TLR)-mediated in­flam­ma­tion, com­ple­ment in­hi­bi­tion also af­fects TLR path­ways, which are also po­ten­tial tar­gets of host mod­u­la­tion in peri­ odon­ti­tis (Fig. 8). TLR path­ways have been pre­dom­i­nantly tar­geted at the in­tra­cel­lu­lar sig­nal­ing level. For in­stance, phar­ma­co­logic in­hi­bi­tion of p38 mi­to­gen-activated pro­tein ki­nase was shown to sup­press peri­odon­tal in­ flam­ma­tion and bone loss in a rat model of peri­odon­ti­tis. TLR ac­ti­va­tion can be trig­gered also by en­dog­e­nous TLR li­gands (e.g., biglycan, hyaluronan frag­ments, and heparan sul­fate frag­ments) that are re­leased upon tis­ sue in­jury. Therefore, in­hi­bi­tion of TLR sig­nal­ing (or of com­ple­ment that am­pli­fies TLR-mediated in­flam­ma­tion) may also con­trib­ute to block the pro­gres­sion of peri­odon­ti­tis.
Developmental en­do­the­lial lo­cus-1 (Del-1) is a ver­sa­tile ho­meo­static pro­tein pro­duced by cer­tain cell types, in­clud­ing en­do­the­lial cells and os­te­o­ clasts. As a se­creted prod­uct of en­do­the­lial cells, Del-1 can block the LFA-1 integrin on neu­tro­phils and thereby sup­press their transendothelial mi­gra­ tion. The ex­pres­sion of Del-1 by os­te­o­clasts con­trols their dif­fer­en­ti­a­tion and re­sorp­tive func­tion by in­ter­act­ing, re­spec­tively, with the integrins
Mac-1 and αvβ3 on os­te­o­clasts. Moreover, Del-1 pro­motes the phago­cy­to­ sis of ap­o­pto­tic neu­tro­phils by tis­sue mac­ro­phages (by form­ing a mo­lec­u­ lar bridge be­tween phosphatidylserine on ap­o­pto­tic cells and αvβ3 integrin on mac­ro­phages), thereby con­trib­ut­ing to the res­o­lu­tion of in­flam­ma­tion (Fig. 8). Consistent with these prop­er­ties, lo­cal ad­min­is­tra­tion of re­com­bi­ nant hu­man Del-1 blocked peri­odon­tal in­flam­ma­tion and bone loss in non­ hu­man pri­ma­tes, sug­gest­ing its po­ten­tial for the treat­ment of the hu­man dis­ease.

T reg­u­la­tory cells (Tregs) downregulate in­duc­tion and pro­lif­er­a­tion of ef­fec­tor T cells and can be pro­tec­tive in in­flam­ma­tory and au­to­im­mune con­di­tions. Local treat­ment of mice or dogs with a Treg chemoattractant for­mu­la­tion (che­mo­kine CCL22 en­cap­su­lated in de­grad­able po­ly­mer) in­ duced the re­cruit­ment of T reg­u­la­tory cells and in­hib­ited ex­per­i­men­tal peri­odon­ti­tis in these mod­els.
Probiotic mi­cro­or­gan­isms (pro­bi­ot­ics) are be­ing con­sid­ered as a ther­a­ peu­tic strat­egy to ame­lio­rate hu­man in­flam­ma­tory dis­eases. The mech­a­ nisms of ac­tion of pro­bi­ot­ics are not well un­der­stood but ap­pear to mod­u­late both the microbiota (help main­tain a bal­anced com­po­si­tion com­pat­i­ble with health) and the host re­sponse (Fig. 8). In the lat­ter re­gard, pro­bi­ot­ics en­hance bar­rier func­tion, pro­mote the ac­tiv­ity of Tregs, and in­hibit proinflammatory re­sponses. In mice, top­i­cal ap­pli­ca­tion of pro­bi­otic Lactobacillus bre­vis CD2 in­hib­ited lig­a­ture-induced peri­odon­tal in­flam­ma­tion and bone loss and re­duced counts of Gram-negative bac­te­ria. A ma­jor mech­a­ nism by which L. bre­vis CD2 ex­erts anti-inflammatory ac­tion is through sup­pres­sion of ni­tric ox­ide syn­the­sis. The same pro­bi­otic had a mod­est ef­ fect in re­duc­ing signs of ex­per­i­men­tal gin­gi­vi­tis in hu­man vol­un­teers in com­ par­i­son to a con­trol group that re­ceived pla­cebo. In this study, CD2 or pla­cebo loz­enges were ap­plied in the mouth and al­lowed to slowly dis­solve.
Several other stud­ies us­ing the pro­bi­otic Lactobacillus reuteri showed that it could be an ef­fec­tive ad­junc­tive treat­ment to con­ven­tional peri­odon­tal ther­ apy (SRP). A re­cent meta-analysis of these stud­ies con­cluded that more stud­ ies, es­pe­cially long-term ones, are re­quired to strengthen the argument for the clin­i­cal ef­fi­cacy of pro­bi­ot­ics as an ad­junct to SRP.

Bisphosphonates are antiresorptive drugs that bind the min­eral com­po­nent of bone (hy­droxy­ap­a­tite crys­tals) and in­ter­fere with the ac­tion of os­te­o­clasts. They have been used for the pre­ven­tion and treat­ment of os­te­o­po­ro­sis. Studies in an­i­mal mod­els of peri­odon­ti­tis showed that sys­ tem­i­cally ad­min­is­tered bis­phos­pho­nates could pro­tect against al­ve­o­lar bone loss with­out­ af­fect­ing in­flam­ma­tion. Similarly, re­duced al­ve­o­lar bone loss and im­proved min­eral den­sity was ob­served in peri­odon­ti­tis pa­tients ad­min­is­tered bis­phos­pho­nates (alendronate or risedronate) as an ad­junct to SRP, al­though a sig­nif­i­cant im­prove­ment of clin­i­cal in­flam­ma­tory pa­ ram­e­ters was not con­sis­tently ob­served. For in­stance, alendronate did not sig­nif­i­cantly in­flu­ence the gin­gi­val in­dex but showed some trend for im­proved at­tach­ment level. It should be noted that osteonecrosis of the jaw is a se­ri­ous ad­verse ef­fect ob­served in a sub­set of pa­tients re­ceiv­ing bis­phos­pho­nates.
Another way to di­rectly in­hibit bone loss is to in­hibit the dif­fer­en­ti­a­ tion of os­te­o­clasts by block­ing the in­ter­ac­tion of RANKL with its re­cep­ tor RANK on the sur­face of os­te­o­clast pre­cur­sors. Proof of concept was ob­tained by show­ing that the nat­u­ral in­hib­i­tor of RANKL osteoprotegerin (ad­min­is­tered as a fu­sion pro­tein with the Fc por­tion of IgG) as well as an­ti-RANKL an­ti­body could in­hibit peri­odon­tal bone loss in rats. Denosumab is a hu­man­ized an­ti-RANKL mono­clo­nal an­ti­body that pre­vents the RANKL-RANK in­ter­ac­tion and has been used for the treat­ment of os­te­o­po­ro­sis. A re­cent study in post­men­o­pausal women re­ceiv­ing sys­temic denosumab and SRP showed im­prove­ment in clin­i­cal pa­ram­e­ters of peri­ odon­tal dis­ease and re­duc­tion in bone turn­over mark­ers, as com­pared with a group re­ceiv­ing pla­cebo and SRP. More stud­ies are re­quired to de­ ter­mine the ef­fi­cacy of denosumab in peri­odon­ti­tis pa­tients, keep­ing in mind that osteonecrosis of the jaw has also been re­ported in pa­tients treated with denosumab.

K E Y  P O I N T S

The bac­te­rial cell wall de­forms upon bac­te­rial ad­he­sion to a sub­stra­tum sur­face un­der the in­flu­ence of the ad­he­sion forces aris­ing from the sub­stra­tum. Bacterial cell wall de­for­ma­tion trig­gers mechanical and chem­i­cal force-sensing ac­cord­ing to a three-ad­he­sion-force re­gime: plank­tonic, in­ter­ac­tive, and le­thal, rep­re­sent­ing small, in­ter­me­di­ate, and ex­tremely high ad­he­sion forces, re­spec­tive­ly.

Strategies to pre­vent bac­te­rial ad­he­sion to limit num­bers of ad­her­ing bac­te­ria have had lim­ited suc­cess in the oral cav­ity.
Research ef­forts might bet­ter fo­cus on ways to con­trol bac­te­rial cell re­sponses to their ad­her­ing states and thus ren­der oral bio­films more sus­cep­ti­ble to oral an­ti­mi­cro­bi­als and me­chan­i­cal re­mov­al.

New gen­er­a­tions of smart sur­faces and stimulus-responsive/ activated nano­struc­tures en­hance se­lec­tiv­ity of an­ti­mi­cro­bial tar­get­ing. Drug re­lease or ac­ti­va­tion spe­cif­i­cally within the biofilm mi­cro­en­vi­ron­ment could spare nor­mal tis­sues and the res­i­dent microbiota from in­hib­i­tory ef­fects, while kill­ing in­fec­tive agents within the path­o­log­i­cal microniche. This

rep­re­sents a bio­com­pat­i­ble and pre­cise antibiofilm treat­ment reg­i­men.

Proof of principle has been dem­on­strated in ro­dent and pri­mate car­ies mod­els that im­mu­ni­za­tion against key an­ti­genic tar­gets of mutans group strep­to­cocci can elicit spe­cific an­ti­bod­ies lead­ing to sup­pres­sion of col­o­ni­za­tion and di­min­ished de­vel­op­ment of car­ies le­sions. A safe, ef­fec­tive, and af­ford­able car­ies vac­cine could have sig­nif­i­cant im­pact among un­der­served pop­u­la­tions, both within
Western na­tions and else­where. The greater com­plex­ity of path­o­genic mech­a­nisms in peri­odon­tal dis­ease com­pared to caries, including intricate host immune-bacteria interactions, com­pli­cates the de­vel­op­ment of a peri­odon­ti­tis vac­cine.

Host mod­u­la­tion ther­a­pies in peri­odon­ti­tis pri­mar­ily aim to in­hibit harm­ful in­flam­ma­tion and pro­mote its res­o­lu­tion. Promising re­sults have been ob­tained in pre­clin­i­cal mod­els or in ear­ly-stage hu­man stud­ies. An FDA-approved sys­tem­i­cally de­liv­ered col­la­ge­ nase in­hib­i­tor (doxy­cy­cline; Periostat) is in­tended as an ad­junc­tive ther­apy to scal­ing and root plan­ing.
