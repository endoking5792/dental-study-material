INTRODUCTION
Antibiotics are a type of an­ti­mi­cro­bial agent used to fight bac­te­rial in­fec­ tions by kill­ing bac­te­ria or in­hib­it­ing their growth. In con­trast to an­ti­sep­tics and dis­in­fec­tants, which are an­ti­mi­cro­bial agents ap­plied to the sur­faces of liv­ing tis­sue or in­an­i­mate ob­jects, an­ti­bi­ot­ics can be in­tro­duced into the cir­ cu­la­tion to com­bat sys­temic in­fec­tions. Antibiotics may ex­ert an­ti­mi­cro­bial ef­fects against some types of fungi but are not ac­tive against vi­ruses. The de­vel­op­ment of an­ti­bi­ot­ics is one of the most im­por­tant ad­vances in the his­ tory of med­i­cine. Antibiotics have made it pos­si­ble to treat po­ten­tially le­thal bac­te­rial in­fec­tions and ex­tend the av­er­age hu­man lifespan.

Paul Ehr­lich and Alexander Flem­ing both played im­por­tant roles in the de­vel­op­ment of an­ti­bi­ot­ics. Paul Ehr­lich, known as the “Father of Che­ motherapy,” be­lieved in the con­cept of the “magic bul­let” that can se­lec­ tively in­hibit dis­ease-causing mi­crobes with­out­ dam­ag­ing the host. In
1904, he ini­ti­ated a sys­tem­atic ap­proach to phar­ma­co­log­i­cal screening to eval­u­ate the ef­fi­cacy of var­i­ous syn­thetic chem­i­cals in the treat­ment of syph­i­lis, which was con­sid­ered an in­cur­able dis­ease at that time. In 1909, with as­sis­tance from chem­ist Al­fred Bertheim and bac­te­ri­ol­o­gist Sahachiro
Hata, Ehr­lich suc­cess­fully treated a syph­i­lis-infected rab­bit with the or­ ganoarsenic agent compound 606. Compound 606 was later re­leased for che­mo­ther­a­peu­tic use un­der the trade name Salvarsan. This agent and its less toxic al­ter­na­tive, Neosalvarsan, were the most com­monly used an­ti­ mi­cro­bial agents be­fore the in­tro­duc­tion of pen­i­cil­lin in the 1940s. Ehr­ lich’s sys­tem­atic screen­ing ap­proach influ­enced the search strat­egy for an­ti­mi­cro­bi­als in the fol­low­ing de­cades.

The dis­cov­ery of pen­i­cil­lin by Alexander Flem­ing in 1928 was per­haps the most im­por­tant event of the mod­ern an­ti­bi­otic age. Upon returning from a vacation, Fleming noted that one of several blood agar plates in­ oculated with Staphylococcus aureus had become contaminated with mold.
The plate now displayed a zone surrounding the mold colony that was devoid of any bacterial growth. Fleming de­duced that the mold had pro­ duced an an­ti­mi­cro­bial sub­stance that dif­fused into the me­dium and in­ hib­ited the growth of S. au­re­us. Later, it was used to treat a se­ri­ous Streptococcus pneumoniae in­fec­tion in Do­magk’s own daugh­ter. Prontosil turned out­ to be a pre­cur­sor com­pound (prodrug) that is en­zy­mat­i­cally con­verted in vi­vo into the an­ti­mi­cro­bial agent sul­fa­nil­amide. By the time sul­fa­nil­ amide had un­der­gone the test­ing re­quired for ap­proval, it was al­ready off-patent due to years of use in the dye in­dus­try. However, the low cost and ease of mod­i­fy­ing sul­fa­nil­amide fa­cil­i­tated fur­ther re­search that led to the de­vel­op­ment and mar­ket­ing of sul­fa­nil­amide de­riv­a­tives.
Of the first two com­mer­cially mar­keted an­ti­bac­te­rial agents, Prontosil and pen­i­cil­lin, only pen­i­cil­lin pos­sesses di­rect an­ti­bac­te­rial ac­tiv­ity (Prontosil re­quires en­zy­matic con­ver­sion). Therefore, most mi­cro­bi­ol­o­gists trace the or­i­gin of the mod­ern an­ti­bi­otic era ei­ther to the dis­cov­ery of pen­i­cil­lin or to the de­vel­op­ment of a method for its pu­ri­fi­ca­tion and mass pro­duc­tion.
Consistent with the or­i­gin of pen­i­cil­lin, most an­ti­bi­ot­ics in clin­i­cal use to­day ei­ther are nat­u­ral prod­ucts usu­ally pro­duced by mi­cro­or­gan­isms that have been found to have bac­te­rio­static or bac­te­ri­cidal ef­fects, or they are semi­ syn­thetic de­riv­a­tives of these prod­ucts. In this chap­ter, we dis­cuss the dif­ fer­ent types of an­ti­bi­ot­ics and their mech­a­nisms of ac­tion as well as how bac­te­ria de­velop an­ti­mi­cro­bial re­sis­tance at the cel­lu­lar level and within bio­films. In ad­di­tion, we pro­vide an over­view of the an­ti­bi­ot­ics cur­rently used in den­tistry to pre­vent or treat dif­fer­ent in­fec­tions.

ANTIBIOTICS: TYPES AND MECHANISMS OF ACTION
Antibiotics can be clas­si­fied by sev­eral ways ac­cord­ing to the following characteristics:

1.	 Chemical struc­ture. Drugs within the same struc­tural cat­e­gory usu­

ally share sim­i­lar mech­a­nism of ac­tion, pat­terns of tox­ic­ity, and allergenic po­ten­tial.
2.	 Type of ac­tiv­ity. Bactericidal agents kill bac­te­ria, while bac­te­rio­

static agents in­hibit their growth.
3.	 Antimicrobial spec­trum. Narrow-spectrum agents are ef­fec­tive against only a few spe­cies of bac­te­ria, while broad-spectrum agents are ef­fec­tive against a wide va­ri­ety of Gram-positive and Gramnegative bac­te­rial spe­cies.
4.	 Mechanism of ac­tion, or the mech­a­nism by which an an­ti­mi­cro­bial agent in­hib­its or ar­rests an es­sen­tial bac­te­rial met­a­bolic path­way.

Antibiotics may tar­get cell wall syn­the­sis, pro­tein syn­the­sis, nu­cleic acid syn­the­sis, a sub­strate in in­ter­me­di­ary me­tab­o­lism, and cell mem­brane in­teg­rity. Cell Wall-Active Agents
The cell wall is crit­i­cal for bac­te­rial sur­vival be­cause it pre­vents the cells from be­ing burst by in­tra­cel­lu­lar tur­gor pres­sure. Its main com­po­ nent is pep­ti­do­gly­can (PG), which is com­posed of a po­ly­sac­cha­ride back­bone with cross-links of al­ter­nat­ing N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) and N-acetylmuramic acid (Mur-NAc) sugar moi­e­ties. The en­ zymes in­volved in the bio­syn­the­sis of PG are re­garded as ideal an­ti­bi­otic tar­gets be­cause most of them are unique to this path­way. Since mam­ ma­lian cells do not have cell walls, cell wall-ac­tive agents gen­er­ally have low tox­ic­ity in hu­mans. The path­way lead­ing to syn­the­sis of PG is ini­ti­ated in the cy­to­plasm, con­tin­ues in the cy­to­plas­mic mem­brane, and fin­ishes in the peri­plas­mic space by po­ly­mer­i­za­tion of the sac­cha­ride groups men­tioned above. The next step in PG syn­the­sis con­sists of the for­ma­tion of two cy­to­ plas­mic mem­brane-bound lipid in­ter­me­di­ates, lip­ids I and II. UDP-MurNAc-pentapeptide is trans­formed to lipid I by an­other en­zyme, MraY, while MurG cat­a­lyzes the syn­the­sis of lipid II from UDP-Glc-NAc and lipid I. Lipid II un­der­goes a trans­lo­ca­tion from in­side to out­side the cy­to­ plas­mic mem­brane. At this lo­ca­tion on the outer sur­face, the di­sac­cha­ride pen­ta­pep­tide sub­units of lipid II serve as sub­strates for the en­zy­matic do­ mains of mem­brane-associated pen­i­cil­lin-binding pro­teins (PBPs). PBPs are a fam­ily of bac­te­rial pro­teins that pos­sess transpeptidase ac­tiv­ity or a com­bi­na­tion of transpeptidase and transglycosylase ac­tiv­i­ties. Transglycosylases cat­a­lyze the for­ma­tion of co­va­lent link­ages be­tween ad­ja­cent di­sac­ cha­rides (GlcNAc-MurNAc) and the re­lease of C55-undecaprenyl phos­phate from lipid II, while transpeptidases form co­va­lent link­ages be­tween ad­ja­ cent pen­ta­pep­tides. As a re­sult of the en­zy­matic ac­tions of PBPs, a ma­ture, rigid PG layer is formed in the peri­plas­mic space.
The β-lactam an­ti­bi­ot­ics, which in­clude pen­i­cil­lins, ceph­a­lo­spo­rins, monobactams, and carbapenems, share a com­mon four-membered β-lactam ring struc­ture that re­sem­bles the ter­mi­nus of the pen­ta­pep­tides that are nor­ mally cross-linked by the transpeptidase do­mains of PBPs. This struc­tural sim­i­lar­ity al­lows β-lactam an­ti­bi­ot­ics to bind to PBPs and block their transpeptidase ac­tiv­ity. This ul­ti­mately trig­gers cell ly­sis, be­cause PBPs are un­ able to add fur­ther cross-links to PG to main­tain the in­teg­rity of the cell wall. Nevertheless, some bac­te­ria de­velop re­sis­tance to these an­ti­bi­ot­ics by pro­duc­ing β-lactamases, which are ca­pa­ble of cleav­ing the ring through hy­dro­ly­sis and in turn in­ac­ti­vat­ing the an­ti­bac­te­rial ac­tiv­ity. Four dif­fer­ ent clas­ses of β-lactamases (A, B, C, and D), con­sist­ing of ser­ine en­zymes and zinc metalloenzymes, have been iden­ti­fied.
The first β-lactams to be used clin­i­cally were the nat­u­ral pen­i­cil­lins, which in­clude pen­i­cil­lin G and pen­i­cil­lin V. Penicillin G, which is acid la­bile, is cur­rently avail­able in par­en­teral for­ma­tions in the United States, while pen­i­cil­lin V is acid sta­ble and can be ad­min­is­tered orally. The ceph­a­lo­spo­rins are an­other ma­jor fam­ily of β-lactam an­ti­bi­ot­ics.

Five gen­er­a­tions of ceph­a­lo­spo­rins have been in­tro­duced into clin­i­cal prac­tice. Like pen­i­cil­ lins, the ceph­a­lo­spo­rins bind to PBPs and in­hibit bac­te­rial wall syn­the­sis. In gen­eral, they have a broader spec­trum of ac­tiv­ity than nat­u­ral pen­i­cil­lins and are sus­cep­ti­ble to in­ac­ti­va­tion by a dis­tinct class of β-lactamases. It is es­ti­mated that ap­prox­i­ma­tely 8 to 10% of in­di­vid­u­als who are treated with one mem­ber of the pen­i­cil­lin fam­ily will be­come al­ler­gic to all­ pen­i­cil­lins.
Although health pro­fes­sion­als are usu­ally taught to avoid ad­min­is­tra­tion of ceph­a­lo­spo­rins to pa­tients al­ler­gic to pen­i­cil­lins, the over­all cross-re­ac­tiv­ity rate is 1 to 10% with first-generation ceph­a­lo­spo­rins (mostly due to their amino acid R1 side chains). The rate of cross-re­ac­tiv­ity is no more than 3 to
5% with later gen­er­a­tions. Although ceph­a­lo­spo­rins can po­ten­tially be used in pa­tients al­ler­gic to pen­i­cil­lins, pre­scrib­ers should re­view the symp­toms of the pa­tient’s pre­vi­ous re­ac­tion (nau­sea, gas­tro­in­tes­ti­nal dis­tur­bance, or hives) and weigh the risk and ben­e­fits care­fully. If they de­cide to ad­min­is­ter a ceph­a­lo­spo­rin, the pre­scriber should be will­ing to ob­serve the pa­tient af­ter the first dose and be ca­pa­ble of man­ag­ing an al­ler­gic re­ac­tion (pos­si­bly in­ clud­ing ana­phy­laxis) in the of­fice.
First-generation ceph­a­lo­spo­rins, in­clud­ing cefazolin and ceph­a­lexin, are ac­tive against many Gram-positive path­o­gens (en­tero­cocci ex­cluded) and were ef­fec­tive against β-lactamase-producing S. au­re­us when first in­tro­ duced. As with the pen­i­cil­lins, re­sis­tance be­gan to emerge af­ter ceph­a­lo­spo­ rins be­gan to be widely pre­scribed. The sec­ond-generation ceph­a­lo­spo­rins, which in­clude cefuroxime, cefaclor, cephamycin, and cefoxitin, are un­af­ fected by cephalosporinases (a class of β-lactamases) pro­duced by Gramnegative rods and there­fore have bet­ter cov­er­age of Gram-negative or­gan­isms while main­tain­ing coverage of Gram-positive organisms similar to that of first-generation agents. They are use­ful for treat­ing mixed aer­o­ bic-anaerobic in­fec­tions. The third-generation ceph­a­lo­spo­rins, which in­ clude cefotaxime, ceftazidime, cef­tri­ax­one, ceftizoxime, and cefoperazone, have en­hanced ac­tiv­ity against Gram-negative or­gan­isms and bet­ter sta­bil­ ity against β-lactamases, but have re­duced ac­tiv­ity against Gram-positive bac­te­ria. They are used in the treat­ment of sex­u­ally trans­mit­ted diseases

Antiribosomal Agents
The ri­bo­some is the site of bac­te­rial pro­tein syn­the­sis, where mRNA is trans­lated in a tem­plate-dependent fash­ion into po­ly­pep­tides. Bacteria have 70S ri­bo­somes, which are com­posed of 30S and 50S ri­bo­somal sub­ units. The 30S sub­unit con­sists of 16S rRNA and ap­prox­i­ma­tely 20 pro­teins, while the 50S sub­unit con­sists of 5S and 23S rRNA plus ap­prox­i­ma­tely
30 pro­teins. The ri­bo­some is a log­i­cal tar­get for an­ti­bi­ot­ics due to its fun­ da­men­tal im­por­tance in bac­te­rial func­tion.
The pro­cess for ri­bo­somal pro­tein syn­the­sis can be di­vided into four ma­jor phas­es: ini­ti­a­tion, elon­ga­tion, ter­mi­na­tion, and re­cy­cling (Fig. 1B).
In the ini­ti­a­tion phase, a 70S ini­ti­a­tion com­plex is formed by the as­sem­bly of the 30S sub­unit and 50S sub­unit, with ac­cu­rate po­si­tion­ing of the mRNA start co­don (usu­ally AUG) of a gene (cis­tron) and the ini­ti­a­tor tRNA (a charged fMet-tRNA) sit­u­ated at the P (pep­tide) site. The ri­bo­somal P site is flanked on one side by the A site (ac­cep­tor for new amino acid) and on the other side by the E site (exit for pep­tide). Initiation fac­tors IF1, IF2, and
IF3 are re­spon­si­ble for the ef­fi­cient and pre­cise for­ma­tion of this 70S ini­ ti­a­tion com­plex.
In the elon­ga­tion phase, there is a re­peat­ing cy­cle of four ma­jor steps, which adds one sin­gle amino acid to the grow­ing pep­tide each time. The cy­cle be­gins with the de­liv­ery of an aminoacylated-tRNA (aa-tRNA) to the A site in the form of ter­nary com­plexes with elon­ga­tion fac­tor Tu (EF-Tu) and GTP. A pep­tide bond be­tween the amino ac­ids bound to the tRNAs in the P and A sites is formed, re­sult­ing in trans­fer of amino acid from the
P site to the A site. This pro­cess is cat­a­lyzed by the peptidyl trans­fer­ase ac­tiv­ity of the 50S ri­bo­somal sub­unit. The next pro­cess, known as trans­ lo­ca­tion, moves the tRNAs from the P and A sites to the E and P sites and cre­ates an empty spot in the A site for the next in­com­ing aa-tRNA.
Translocation is me­di­ated by elon­ga­tion fac­tor G (EF-G) with the nec­es­ sary en­ergy sup­plied by the hy­dro­ly­sis of GTP. The elon­gat­ing pep­tide chain passes through an exit tun­nel in the 50S sub­unit along with the re­ lease of un­charged prior tRNA and EF-G. The elon­ga­tion cy­cle re­peats un­til a stop co­don is en­coun­tered, at which point ter­mi­na­tion re­lease fac­ tor RF3, along with ei­ther RF1 or RF2 (which di­rectly rec­og­nize the stop co­don), will bind to the A site, form­ing a ter­mi­na­tion com­plex. The 50Smediated peptidyl trans­fer­ase causes re­lease of the po­ly­pep­tide chain from the 70S ri­bo­some through hy­dro­ly­sis of the peptidyl-tRNA bond. After this phase, three fac­tors, in­clud­ing ri­bo­somal re­cy­cling fac­tor, EF-G, and
IF3, work to­gether to dis­so­ci­ate the 70S ri­bo­some into its 50S and 30S sub­ units for the next round of pro­tein syn­the­sis.
Many antiribosomal an­ti­bi­ot­ics in­ter­fere with the elon­ga­tion phase of pro­tein syn­the­sis. The tet­ra­cy­clines in­hibit a broad spec­trum of Grampositive and Gram-negative bac­te­ria by bind­ing to the 16S rRNA of the 30S ri­bo­somal sub­unit and block­ing the de­liv­ery of aa-tRNA to the ri­bo­somal
A site. Bacteria have a more ef­fi­cient trans­port sys­tem for tak­ing up tet­ra­ cy­clines than hu­man cells, so tet­ra­cy­clines are se­lec­tively ac­tive against bac­ te­ria. Since its first clin­i­cal use in 1948, many bac­te­rial strains have be­come re­sis­tant to tet­ra­cy­cline. To over­come this is­sue, sec­ond-generation tet­ra­ cy­clines (in­clud­ing minocycline and doxy­cy­cline) and third-generation tet­ra­cy­clines (tigecycline) were de­vel­oped. Antifolate Agents
Agents tar­get­ing the fo­late bio­syn­the­sis path­ways can be use­ful in the treat­ment of can­cer and in­fec­tions by bac­te­ria and pro­to­zoa. This path­ way pro­vi­des tetrahydrofolate, which is an es­sen­tial co­fac­tor for the syn­ the­sis of dTMP, pu­rine nu­cle­o­tides, and the amino ac­ids his­ti­dine and me­thi­o­nine. Antifolate agents dis­rupt DNA/RNA syn­the­sis and re­pair, as well as pro­tein syn­the­sis. In hu­mans, fo­lic acid is ac­tively taken up by cells and re­duced by the en­zyme dihydrofolate re­duc­tase (DHFR), first to dihydrofolate and then tetrahydrofolate by the same en­zyme. On the other hand, bac­te­ria, fungi, and pro­to­zoa pos­sess a path­way for de no­vo bio­ syn­the­sis and do not rely on ex­og­e­nous fo­lic acid. The en­zyme dihydropteroate synthase (DHPS) first con­verts dihydropteroate di­phos­phate and pa­ra-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) to dihydropteroate. The en­zyme dihydrofolate synthase then adds glu­ta­mate to cre­ate dihydrofolate.
The sul­fon­amides are a class of an­ti­bi­ot­ics that has been in clin­i­cal use since the 1930s. Structurally, they all­ have a chem­i­cal nu­cleus that re­sem­ bles PABA, which al­lows them to func­tion as com­pet­i­tive in­hib­i­tors of
DHPS. Sulfonamides are broad-spectrum an­ti­bi­ot­ics, but un­for­tu­nately, re­sis­tance to sul­fon­amides has emerged since their in­tro­duc­tion. The most com­monly pre­scribed sulfa drug is sulfamethoxazole. It is usu­ally used in a syn­er­gis­tic com­bi­na­tion with tri­meth­o­prim for uri­nary tract in­fec­tion and op­por­tu­nis­tic in­fec­tions or for the pre­ven­tion or treat­ment of par­a­ sitic and ma­lar­ial in­fec­tions. Approximately 3% of pa­tients re­port ad­verse ef­fects, such as di­ar­rhea, nau­sea, and diz­zi­ness, with sul­fon­amides, al­though most of these are mild and self-lim­it­ing. Severe side ef­fects in­clude acute liver dam­age, Ste­vens-Johnson syn­drome, and toxic epi­der­mal necrolysis.
Trimethoprim is a syn­thetic antifolate that has a py­rim­i­dine-like struc­ ture re­sem­bling part of fo­lic acid. It binds to DHFR and pre­vents the re­ duc­tion of dihydrofolate to tetrahydrofolate. It can be used to in­hibit aer­o­bic Gram-negative bac­te­ria such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Enterobacter spe­cies. While it is of­ten com­bined with sulfamethoxazole, tri­meth­o­prim alone is some­times used to treat of uri­nary tract in­fec­tions. Rare but se­vere side ef­fects of tri­meth­o­prim in­clude meg­ a­lo­blas­tic ane­mia, aplas­tic ane­mia, and agran­u­lo­cy­to­sis.

Topoisomerase Inhibitors
DNA topo­isom­er­ases are en­zymes that are es­sen­tial for cell sur­vival. They play a spe­cial role in man­ag­ing changes in DNA to­pol­ogy dur­ing DNA rep­li­ca­tion, tran­scrip­tion, and re­com­bi­na­tion. Among these en­zymes,
DNA gyrase is a ma­jor ther­a­peu­tic tar­get. It is pres­ent in all­ bac­te­ria but is not found in higher eu­kary­otes. Currently, all­ an­ti­bi­ot­ics tar­get­ing bac­ te­rial topo­isom­er­ases af­fect ei­ther DNA gyrase only or DNA gyrase and
DNA topoisomerase IV. Both of these en­zymes are heterotetramers that con­tain sub­units that pos­sess ATPase ac­tiv­ity to pro­vide the en­ergy re­ quired to al­ter DNA to­pol­o­gy.

Miscellaneous Agents
Metronidazole is a nitroimidazole agent that is bac­te­ri­cidal against an­aer­ o­bic bac­te­ria and also ex­hib­its an­ti­par­a­sitic ac­tiv­i­ty. Metronidazole en­ters cells as a prodrug that has no an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tiv­ity un­til it is pro­cessed by spe­cific in­tra­cel­lu­lar en­zymes. Under an­aer­o­bic con­di­tions, the ni­tro group of met­ro­ni­da­zole is re­duced. The re­ac­tion pro­duces free rad­i­cals that bind to bac­te­rial DNA, lead­ing to im­paired DNA syn­the­sis, ac­cu­mu­ la­tion of DNA dam­age (break­age), and even­tu­ally cell death. In den­tistry, met­ro­ni­ da­zole is used to treat odontogenic in­fec­tions and has been ex­ten­sively used as an ad­junct to scal­ing and root plan­ing for treat­ment of ag­gres­sive and chronic peri­odon­ti­tis.

Antibiotic Combinations
Although sin­gle-agent an­ti­bi­otic ther­apy is usu­ally pre­ferred due to con­cerns about ad­verse ef­fects and cost, a com­bi­na­tion of two or more an­ti­bi­ot­ics can some­times be more ben­e­fi­cial. The po­ten­tial ben­e­fits in­clude the fol­low­ing:

1.	 Synergistic ac­tiv­ity against bac­te­ria. As an ex­am­ple, com­bin­ing a
β-lactam an­ti­bi­otic and an aminoglycoside can pro­duce an an­ti­ mi­cro­bial ef­fect that is greater than the sum of in­di­vid­ual ef­fects of these agents. In this spe­cific com­bi­na­tion, in­hi­bi­tion of cell wall syn­the­sis by the β-lactam agent en­hances cell per­me­abil­ity to the aminoglycoside.
2.	 Enhancement of the an­ti­mi­cro­bial spec­trum for treat­ment of mixed in­fec­tions. The com­bi­na­tion of flu­o­ro­quin­o­lone and met­ro­ni­da­zole is more ef­fec­tive than a sin­gle agent in treat­ing intra-abdominal in­fec­tions.
3.	 Prevention of an­ti­mi­cro­bial re­sis­tance. Antibiotic com­bi­na­tions (e.g., iso­ni­a­zid, ri­fam­pin, and eth­am­bu­tol) are com­monly used in the treat­ment of tu­ber­cu­lo­sis.
4.	 Empirical use of an­ti­bi­otic com­bi­na­tions in crit­i­cally ill pa­tients.
When treat­ing in­fec­tions that are se­vere and life-threatening, it is es­ sen­tial to ini­ti­ate ther­apy as early as pos­si­ble (usu­ally be­fore in­for­ma­ tion from cul­ture and sen­si­tiv­ity test­ing is avail­able). A com­bi­na­tion of agents may be ben­e­fi­cial for treat­ing a se­vere, com­plex in­fec­tion.

In con­trast, the use of an in­ap­pro­pri­ate com­bi­na­tion of an­ti­bi­ot­ics can pro­duce an­tag­o­nis­tic ef­fects. Antibiotic Adjuvants
In ad­di­tion to com­bin­ing dif­fer­ent clas­ses of an­ti­bi­ot­ics, an­ti­bi­ot­ics are also com­bined with an ad­ju­vant to im­prove ef­fi­cacy. An ex­am­ple is clavulanic acid, which binds to and in­hib­its β-lactamase, which is pro­duced by bac­te­ria to de­grade pen­i­cil­lin-class an­ti­bi­ot­ics (dis­cussed in greater de­ tail be­low). Agents that dis­perse bio­films or stim­u­late growth of dor­mant cells, thus ren­der­ing the bac­te­ria more sus­cep­ti­ble to an­ti­bi­ot­ics, may also be con­sid­ered ad­ju­vants.

Measuring the Effects of Antibiotics
The po­tency of an an­ti­bi­otic against bac­te­ria may vary from spe­cies to spe­ cies and from strain to strain within the same spe­cies. Potency may also vary with the bac­te­rial load and the phys­i­o­log­i­cal state of the bac­te­ria. A va­ri­ety of meth­ods, in­clud­ing mi­cro­bi­o­log­i­cal as­says, au­to­mated chem­i­cal as­says (e.g., liq­uid chro­ma­tog­ra­phy), and im­mu­no­as­says, have been used to as­sess an­ti­bi­otic po­tency, but mi­cro­bi­o­log­i­cal as­says are used most frequently due to sim­plic­ity and cost-ef­fec­tive­ness. In ad­di­tion, they pro­vide in­for­ma­tion about bi­o­log­i­cal ac­tiv­ity. Commonly used mi­cro­bi­o­log­i­cal as­says in­clude broth di­lu­tion as­says, agar dif­fu­sion as­says, and disk dif­fu­ sion as­says. These meth­ods are based on the abil­ity of an­ti­bi­ot­ics to in­ hibit bac­te­rial growth in vi­tro ac­cord­ing to stan­dard­ized ex­per­i­men­tal pro­to­cols. Results of these meth­ods can be quan­ti­ta­tive (MIC, or min­i­mal con­cen­tra­tion re­quired to in­hibit vis­i­ble bac­te­rial growth, usu­ally ex­pressed in mi­cro­grams per milliliter) or qual­i­ta­tive (sus­cep­ti­ble, in­ter­me­di­ate, or re­sis­tant). In ad­di­tion to MIC, other mea­sure­ments have been used, in­ clud­ing min­i­mum bac­te­ri­cidal con­cen­tra­tions (MBC, the low­est con­cen­ tra­tion re­quired to kill a bac­te­rium) and mu­ta­tion pre­ven­tion con­cen­tra­tion (MPC, the low­est con­cen­tra­tion re­quired to block the growth of the least sus­cep­ti­ble cell pres­ent in high-density bac­te­rial pop­u­la­tions). These tests have shown their value in se­lec­tion of an­ti­bi­ot­ics for par­tic­u­lar bac­te­rial in­fec­tions and mon­i­tor­ing of pos­si­ble drug re­sis­tance. Readers are re­ ferred to guide­lines from the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI; for­merly the National Committee for Clinical Laboratory Standards) for fur­ther de­tails.
Since an­ti­bi­otic sus­cep­ti­bil­ity test­ing meth­ods are mainly based on ef­ fects ob­served on bac­te­ria liv­ing in a plank­tonic state, the re­sults may not be ex­trap­o­lated to bac­te­ria iso­lated from biofilm-associated in­fec­tions. In gen­eral, higher an­ti­bi­otic con­cen­tra­tions are re­quired to kill or in­hibit bac­ te­ria liv­ing in bio­films due to re­stricted pen­e­tra­tion of an­ti­bi­ot­ics, the slow growth of bac­te­ria within bio­films, and ac­quired re­sis­tance (fur­ther dis­cussed be­low). While new phar­ma­co­dy­namic pa­ram­e­ters, such as min­i­mal biofilm in­hib­i­tory con­cen­tra­tion and biofilm bac­te­ri­cidal con­cen­tra­tion, have been pro­posed, their application is not yet stan­dard pro­ce­dure.

Nonantibiotic Antimicrobial Agents and Disinfectants
While sys­temic an­ti­bi­ot­ics are used to treat acute orofacial in­fec­tions or ag­ gres­sive peri­odon­tal in­fec­tions, an­ti­mi­cro­bial agents are more com­monly used to pre­vent or treat car­i­o­genic bio­films, gin­gi­vi­tis, and peri­odon­ti­tis in com­bi­na­tion with rou­tine and pro­fes­sional me­chan­i­cal clean­ing. Commonly used an­ti­mi­cro­bial agents in tooth­pastes and mouth­washes in­clude tri­clo­san, chlor­hex­i­dine glu­co­nate (CHX), ce­tylpyridinium chloride (CPC), es­sen­tial oils, and fluo­rides (stan­nous or amine fluo­ride). Triclosan, CHX, and CPC dis­rupt the bac­te­rial cell wall, caus­ing leak­age of cel­lu­lar com­po­ nents and up­set­ting the os­motic bal­ance within the cell. In ad­di­tion, they have been shown to dis­rupt res­pi­ra­tion and cell sig­nal­ing path­ways, thus hav­ing po­ten­tial ef­fi­cacy against biofilm de­vel­op­ment. Fluoride com­pounds have both an an­ti­car­ies in­flu­ence, since they pro­tect tooth enamel from acid dis­ so­lu­tion of the hy­droxy­ap­a­tite, and also an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tiv­ity, par­tic­u­larly stan­nous and amine fluoride or fluoride compounds com­bined with sil­ver (e.g., sil­ver di­amine fluo­ride). Glutaraldehyde, hy­dro­gen per­ox­ide, ox­i­diz­ers, and al­co­hols (ethyl, iso­pro­pyl) have been used as sur­ face dis­in­fec­tants in the den­tal of­fice, while so­dium hy­po­chlo­rite has been com­monly used to dis­in­fect end­odon­tic ca­nals (see Chapter 18).

BACTERIAL RESISTANCE TO ANTIBIOTICS

Resistance: Health Effects and Economic Costs
The dis­cov­ery of an­ti­bi­ot­ics was a turn­ing point in the his­tory of mod­ern med­i­cine. By pro­vid­ing an ef­fec­tive ap­proach for treat­ment of in­fec­tious dis­ eases, an­ti­bi­ot­ics have saved the lives of count­less in­di­vid­u­als and im­proved pub­lic health by con­trol­ling the spread of in­fec­tions. However, his­tory has re­peat­edly shown that newly in­tro­duced an­ti­bi­ot­ics lose much of their clin­i­ cal ef­fec­tive­ness soon af­ter they have been widely used. As an ex­am­ple, re­sis­ tance to meth­i­cil­lin, re­lated to bac­te­rial ex­pres­sion of β-lactamases and al­tered PBPs, was ob­served the year af­ter its in­tro­duc­tion in 1960. Bacteria adapt to the pres­ence of an­ti­bi­ot­ics by evolv­ing mech­a­nisms that al­low them to sur­vive, and they be­come less re­spon­sive to an­ti­bi­ot­ics over time. As a con­se­quence, pa­tients who are treated with an­ti­bi­ot­ics are more likely to de­ velop an in­fec­tion by an an­ti­bi­ot­ic-resistant strain of bac­te­ria. Moreover, com­mu­ni­ties or hos­pi­tals in which an­ti­bi­ot­ics are over­pre­scribed have a greater prob­lem with emer­gence of an­ti­bi­ot­ic-resistant bac­te­ria than those in which an­ti­bi­ot­ics are used ju­di­ciously. In ev­ery in­stance in which an an­ti­bi­otic is ad­min­is­tered, its ther­a­peu­tic ef­fect is ob­tained at the cost of an in­creased risk that path­o­gens as well as the com­men­sal microbiota will de­velop re­sis­tance.

Intrinsic and Acquired Resistance
A dis­tinc­tion can be made be­tween in­trin­sic and ac­quired re­sis­tance. Intrinsic re­sis­tance re­fers to a nat­u­ral form of re­sis­tance to a spe­cific an­ti­bi­otic that is shared by vir­tu­ally all­ mem­bers of a par­tic­u­lar bac­te­rial ge­nus, spe­ cies, or sub­spe­cies. The ba­sis for in­trin­sic re­sis­tance is usu­ally re­lated to struc­tural or bio­chem­i­cal fea­tures of the bac­te­ria that pre­vent the an­ti­bi­otic from pen­e­trat­ing the in­tra­cel­lu­lar en­vi­ron­ment and in­ter­act­ing with its tar­get, or be­cause the an­ti­bi­otic can­not rec­og­nize its tar­get be­cause of an al­ter­ation in tar­get struc­ture. For ex­am­ple, Pseudomonas aeruginosa is in­trin­si­cally re­sis­tant to sev­eral dif­fer­ent an­ti­bi­ot­ics be­cause of the low per­me­abil­ity of its cel­lu­lar en­ve­lope. Enterococci are in­trin­si­cally re­sis­tant to ceph­a­lo­spo­rins be­cause they do not have PBPs that can bind and un­ dergo in­hi­bi­tion by these β-lactam agents. An aware­ness of in­trin­sic re­sis­ tance is es­sen­tial in pre­vent­ing in­ap­pro­pri­ate pre­scrip­tions and ther­a­peu­tic fail­ures.
Bacteria can ac­quire re­sis­tance to an­ti­bi­ot­ics through spon­ta­ne­ous mu­ta­tion, es­pe­cially when the mu­tated gene af­fects cell per­me­abil­ity or the ex­pres­sion of mem­brane trans­port­ers, an­ti­bi­otic tar­gets, or en­zymes that mod­ify an­ti­bi­ot­ics. A mu­ta­tion can al­ter the struc­ture and func­tion of the pro­tein en­coded by a par­tic­u­lar gene. Bacteria are al­ways un­der­go­ing mu­ ta­tion, but the fre­quency is low (∼1 mu­ta­tion per 300 cy­cles of DNA rep­ li­ca­tion) un­der nor­mal con­di­tions. Bacteria sub­jected to stress tend to mu­tate more fre­quently. Since an­ti­bi­ot­ics in­duce stress in bac­te­ria, ex­po­ sure to an­ti­bi­ot­ics in­creases the prob­a­bil­ity that re­sis­tance will de­velop as the re­sult of a chro­mo­somal mu­ta­tion. Resistant bac­te­ ria have been iso­lated from an­cient caves, Arctic ice beds, and other re­mote sites that have never been ex­posed to com­mer­cial an­ti­mi­cro­bi­als. It is very likely that mech­a­nisms of re­sis­tance orig­i­nally evolved in an­ti­bi­ot­ic-producing bac­te­ria as a means of self-pro­tec­tion. It is also likely that many re­sis­tance genes later arose from other sources, per­haps through the mu­ta­tion and re­ com­bi­na­tion of genes in­volved in un­re­lated met­a­bolic func­tions (e.g., de­ tox­i­fi­ca­tion and house­keep­ing). Biofilm-Associated Drug Tolerance
Biofilms are sur­face-associated mi­cro­bial com­mu­ni­ties form­ing dense ag­ gre­gates that are en­meshed and held to­gether within a self-pro­duced ma­trix com­prised of ex­tra­cel­lu­lar po­ly­meric sub­stances (EPS) such as po­ly­sac­cha­ rides, pro­teins, lip­ids, nu­cleic ac­ids (ex­tra­cel­lu­lar DNA [eDNA]), among other bio­mol­e­cules. Biofilms are both tol­er­ant to an­ti­bi­ot­ics and an­ti­mi­cro­ bi­als and me­chan­i­cally dif­fi­cult to re­move completely from surfaces due to their viscoelastic properties, mak­ing them ex­tremely dif­fi­cult to treat once es­tab­lished.
The mech­a­nism of biofilm tol­er­ance (also termed re­cal­ci­trance) is mul­ti­ fac­to­rial, in­volv­ing the pres­ence of the EPS ma­trix and al­tered mi­cro­en­vi­ ron­ment, ge­netic changes, dor­mancy, and per­sister cells (Fig. 4). Genetic changes in­clude gene trans­fer, small-col­ony var­i­ants (SCVs), a slow-growing sub­set of the bac­te­rial pop­u­la­tion, and ge­netic di­ver­sity. The term “tol­er­ ance” is of­ten used rather than “re­sis­tance” when re­fer­ring to the in­ef­fec­tive­ ness of an­ti­bi­ot­ics or an­ti­mi­cro­bial agents to kill biofilm cells, since even strains that are susceptible to an­ti­bi­ot­ics, when as­sessed by con­ven­tional clin­i­cal de­ter­mi­na­tion of the min­i­mum in­hib­i­tory or bac­te­ri­cidal con­cen­tra­ tion, re­quire con­cen­tra­tions that are hun­dreds to thou­sands of times greater to be killed when grow­ing in a biofilm.

DORMANCY AND PERSISTER CELLS
Another mech­a­nism of tol­er­ance as­so­ci­ated with bio­films is the pres­ence of a small pop­u­la­tion of dor­mant per­sister cells. Persister cells have a wellde­fined phe­no­type and are not to be con­fused with “per­sis­tent cells” or
“per­sis­tence,” which can be used to im­ply te­nac­ity in a more gen­eral sense.
Some cells within a pop­u­la­tion be­come dor­mant de­spite the pres­ence of nu­tri­ents. Persister cells can be iso­lated by add­ing an­ti­bi­ot­ics to a cul­ture to kill the ac­tively grow­ing cells. The an­ti­bi­ot­ics can then be re­moved by wash steps and the re­main­ing per­sister cells cul­tured in nor­mal cul­ture me­ dia. Unlike SCVs un­der fa­vor­able growth con­di­tions, these cells grow nor­mally and are not an­ti­bi­otic re­sis­tant. A small pop­u­la­tion (i.e., 1 cell in a mil­lion) will be­come a per­sister cell in the new pop­u­la­tion. Persister cells are not unique to bio­films, but the biofilm mi­cro­en­vi­ron­ment ap­pears to be con­du­cive to their for­ma­tion and pro­vi­des a sta­ble en­vi­ron­ment within which they can be har­bored.
In sum­mary, bac­te­rial bio­films pres­ent mul­ti­ple mech­a­nisms for tol­er­ ance to an­ti­bi­ot­ics and an­ti­mi­cro­bial agents. Conventional an­ti­mi­cro­bi­als are se­lected on the basis of their abil­ity to kill rap­idly grow­ing plank­tonic cells, not to be ef­fec­tive in the unique con­di­tions of the biofilm mi­cro­en­vi­ ron­ment. Targeting bac­te­rial cells alone will likely not be enough; new agents and ther­a­peu­tics will need to be in­tro­duced that tar­get the biofilm in a ho­lis­tic sense as a dis­tinct en­tity. An emerg­ing ap­proach is to com­bine an­ti­bi­ot­ics and an­ti­mi­cro­bi­als with agents that cause bac­te­ria to nat­u­rally dis­perse from bio­films or phys­i­cally break down the biofilm by tar­get­ing struc­tural com­po­nents in the EPS ma­trix through en­zy­matic, sur­fac­tant, and ox­i­da­tive ac­tiv­ity. However, like the cell pop­u­la­tion within the biofilm, the EPS ma­trix is also chem­i­cally com­plex and het­ero­ge­neous and, as such, will likely re­quire the ap­pli­ca­tion of mul­ti­ple ther­a­peu­tic mo­dal­ i­ties to achieve com­plete erad­i­ca­tion.

Since the risk of de­vel­op­ing an­ti­bi­otic re­sis­tance in­creases with the fre­ quency and du­ra­tion of bac­te­rial ex­po­sure to an­ti­bi­ot­ics, the key to min­ i­miz­ing re­sis­tance is to use an­ti­bi­ot­ics se­lec­tively and spar­ingly, bal­anc­ing the risks of in­duc­ing re­sis­tance against the ben­e­fits that can be ob­tained by kill­ing or in­hib­it­ing bac­te­ria. This can be ac­com­plished by ad­her­ing to the fol­low­ing prac­tices:

1.	 When con­sid­er­ing the use of an­ti­bi­otic pro­phy­laxis to pre­vent an in­fec­tion, fol­low the most cur­rent guide­lines. Historically, many of the an­ti­bi­ot­ics pre­scribed by den­tists were ad­min­is­tered prior to den­tal work to pre­vent bac­ter­emias in­duced by den­tal treat­ment from caus­ing in­fec­tive en­do­car­di­tis or an in­fec­tion of an ar­ti­fi­cial joint. Over time, the bal­ance be­tween the risks and ben­e­fits of an­ ti­bi­otic pro­phy­laxis has been re­as­sessed in light of new ev­i­dence from clin­i­cal stud­ies. In some cases, the data have sug­gested that the risk of tak­ing an an­ti­bi­otic ex­ceeds the po­ten­tial ben­e­fit of pre­ vent­ing an in­fec­tion. Currently, the pro­por­tion of pa­tients for whom an­ti­bi­otic pro­phy­laxis is rec­om­mended prior to den­tal treat­ment is much smaller than in pre­vi­ous years.
2.	 Select an an­ti­bi­otic with a nar­row spec­trum. Narrow-spectrum agents af­fect a smaller pro­por­tion of bac­te­ria than broad-spectrum agents, which de­creases the odds of in­duc­ing emer­gence of an­ti­bi­ot­icresistant bac­te­ria. In ad­di­tion, it min­i­mizes the dis­tur­bance of nor­mal microbiota, es­pe­cially in the gut. Narrow-spectrum an­ti­bi­ot­ics are use­ful for man­ag­ing many sim­ple in­fec­tions. Alternatively, com­bi­na­ tions of mul­ti­ple nar­row-spectrum an­ti­bi­ot­ics could pro­vide broader cov­er­age (at least to in­clude both Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms). Ideally, broad-spectrum an­ti­bi­ot­ics should be re­served for treat­ing com­plex in­fec­tions.
3.	 Prescribe an ef­fec­tive dose of an­ti­bi­otic. When bac­te­ria are ex­

posed to an ap­pro­pri­ate dose, they are more likely to be killed or se­verely in­hib­ited. When they are ex­posed to a low con­cen­tra­tion, there is an in­creased risk that they will sur­vive and de­velop re­sis­ tance. Underdosing may come about as the re­sult of an er­ror on the part of the pre­scrib­ing doc­tor, but it can also oc­cur if the pa­tient fails to com­ply with the pre­scribed reg­i­men. It is ben­e­fi­cial to ad­vise pa­tients that they must fol­low the in­struc­tions that ac­com­pany the pre­scrip­tion. It is usu­ally eas­ier to ob­tain pa­tient com­pli­ance when the an­ti­bi­otic reg­i­men is not overly com­plex.
4.	 Treat for the short­est ef­fec­tive du­ra­tion. In pre­vi­ous years, many cli­ni­cians were taught that if the an­ti­bi­otic reg­i­men for an in­fec­ tion was ter­mi­nated early, there was an in­creased chance of de­vel­ op­ing a re­bound in­fec­tion and an in­creased risk of de­vel­op­ing an­ti­bi­otic re­sis­tance. However, re­cent stud­ies sug­gest that short­erduration reg­i­mens, when pre­scribed for oth­er­wise healthy pa­tients, can be just as ef­fec­tive as lon­ger courses of treat­ment and less likely to se­lect for bac­te­ria that are re­sis­tant to an­ti­bi­ot­ics.
5.	 The de­ci­sion to pre­scribe an an­ti­bi­otic should be based on ap­pro­

pri­ate di­ag­no­sis. Antibiotics are not use­ful for treat­ing some oral

USE OF ANTIBIOTICS IN DENTISTRY
In 2013, den­tists in the United States pre­scribed over 24 mil­lion courses of an­ti­bi­ot­ics, equiv­a­lent to 9% of all­ an­ti­bi­ot­ics pre­scribed in out­ patient set­tings. On av­er­age, each den­tist wrote four an­ti­bi­otic pre­scrip­ tions per week. Only pri­mary care phy­si­cians, nurse prac­ti­tion­ers, and phy­si­cian as­sis­tants pre­scribed an­ti­bi­ot­ics more fre­quently. Dentists use sys­temic an­ti­bi­ot­ics to treat sev­eral dif­fer­ent types of acute and chronic oral in­fec­tions and, in se­lected pa­tients, to pre­vent in­fec­tions that could po­ten­tially re­sult from bac­ter­emias caused by in­va­sive den­ tal pro­ce­dures. Most of the in­di­ca­tions for us­ing an­ti­bi­ot­ics in den­tistry are well de­fined. Antibiotics are ad­min­is­tered sys­tem­i­cally to sup­press the growth of path­o­genic bac­te­ria, en­cour­age the re­place­ment of path­ o­gens by host-compatible spe­cies, and al­low the host re­sponse to re­es­ tab­lish con­trol.
Odontogenic in­fec­tions, which orig­i­nate from bac­te­ria as­so­ci­ated with teeth, are ini­tially lo­cal­ized to the area ad­ja­cent to root api­ces (as periapical in­fec­tions). However, these in­fec­tions can spread be­yond the periapical area, exit the bone, and spread along fas­cial planes into other re­gions of the head and neck and into ad­ja­cent soft tis­sue to pro­duce cel­lu­li­tis or an ab­scess. Severe odontogenic in­fec­tions can be life-threatening and very dif­fi­cult to man­age. Considerations for use of an­ti­bi­ot­ics to treat odontogenic in­fec­tions are pre­sented in this sec­tion. While an­ti­ bi­ot­ics are not in­di­cated for treat­ing lo­cal­ized in­fec­tions, they can play a crit­i­cal role in the treat­ment of pro­gres­sively spread­ing in­fec­tions as­ so­ci­ated with signs of sys­temic in­volve­ment (e.g., fe­ver, mal­aise, or lymph­ ade­nop­a­thy).
Antibiotics can be used to treat cer­tain forms of peri­odon­ti­tis, a chronic in­fec­tion that breaks down the peri­odon­tal at­tach­ment and con­trib­utes to tooth loss. In this role, an­ti­bi­ot­ics are al­ways used as an ad­junct to con­ ven­tional me­chan­i­cal de­bride­ment (non­sur­gi­cal scal­ing and root plan­ing).
Antibiotics are most use­ful in the treat­ment of ag­gres­sive peri­odon­ti­tis or se­vere chronic peri­odon­ti­tis. Most chronic peri­odon­ti­tis cases of slight-tomoderate se­ver­ity can be treated suc­cess­fully with­out­ the use of an­ti­bi­ot­ ics. Guidelines for use of an­ti­bi­ot­ics in peri­odon­tal ther­apy are dis­cussed in de­tail be­low.
Lastly, sys­temic an­ti­bi­ot­ics can be used to sup­press oral bac­te­ria that are seeded into the blood­stream dur­ing in­va­sive den­tal pro­ce­dures, thereby pre­vent­ing them from in­fect­ing other parts of the body. In sus­cep­ti­ble pa­ tients with a high risk of post­treat­ment com­pli­ca­tions, pro­phy­lac­tic an­ti­ bi­ot­ics are ad­min­is­tered as a sin­gle-dose reg­i­men prior to den­tal pro­ce­dures that have the po­ten­tial to in­duce bac­ter­emia. Indications for use of an­ti­bi­ otic pro­phy­laxis are dis­cussed in this sec­tion. In re­cent years, the use of an­ti­bi­otic pro­phy­laxis has be­come more se­lec­tive. It should now only be

Treatment of Endodontic (Periapical) Infections
Endodontic in­fec­tions are trig­gered by bac­te­rial en­try into the den­tal pulp and root ca­nal sys­tem. Details on the eti­ol­ogy and path­o­gen­e­sis of end­odon­tic in­fec­tions as well as treat­ment op­tions are de­scribed in Chapter 18. Briefly, lo­cal­ized end­odon­tic in­fec­tions are treated by open­ing the tooth, de­brid­ing the root ca­nal sys­tem, dis­in­fect­ing the ca­nal, and man­ ag­ing pain with an ap­pro­pri­ate an­al­ge­sic. If pres­ent, lo­cal­ized soft tis­sue swell­ing should be in­cised and drained at the same time as the root ca­nal sys­tem is treated. This ap­proach is usu­ally ef­fec­tive for re­solv­ing the in­ fec­tion with­out­ the use of an­ti­bi­ot­ics. Treatment of odontogenic in­fec­ tions that have spread be­yond the al­ve­o­lar bone is de­scribed in the next sec­tion.

Treatment of Odontogenic Infections
An es­tab­lished periapical in­fec­tion will spread through the bone and into the ad­ja­cent tis­sues. Minor odontogenic in­fec­tions that spread be­yond the periapical re­gion are not un­com­mon and usu­ally re­spond to treat­ment with sur­gi­cal drain­age and an ap­pro­pri­ate an­ti­bi­otic reg­i­men. However, se­vere in­fec­tions oc­ca­sion­ally spread pos­te­ri­orly and in­fe­ri­orly through the fas­cial spaces of the head and neck, caus­ing ob­struc­tion of the pa­tient’s air­way. Severe in­fec­tions should be man­aged by a spe­cial­ist through a com­bi­na­tion of sur­gi­cal treat­ment (in­ci­sion and drain­age, ex­trac­tion, or end­odon­tic ther­apy) and an­ti­bi­otic treat­ment. Patients with se­vere in­fec­ tions may re­quire sup­port­ive med­i­cal care dur­ing treat­ment.
Odontogenic in­fec­tions typ­i­cally prog­ress through sev­eral char­ac­ter­is­ tic stages (Table 3), each of which dif­fers with re­spect to clin­i­cal signs and symp­toms and the pre­dom­i­nant bac­te­ria. The ini­tial source of these in­fec­ tions is typ­i­cally car­ies-associated aer­o­bic and fac­ul­ta­tive bac­te­ria, with strep­to­cocci play­ing a pre­dom­i­nant role. During the first 3 days, clin­i­cal signs of in­flam­ma­tion are rel­a­tively mild and the in­fec­tion is lim­ited in size. Swelling is dif­fuse, soft, and mildly ten­der and is as­so­ci­ated with edema rather than pus for­ma­tion. During the cel­lu­li­tis stage (3 to 7 days af­ter the start of the in­fec­tion), the swell­ing in­creases in size and be­comes hard and ten­der to pal­pa­tion. The hard­ness is caused by ex­trav­a­sa­tion of fluid from vas­cu­lar el­e­ments into the in­ter­sti­tial tis­sue. This pro­duces tis­ sue ten­sion, com­pres­sion of blood ves­sels, and re­stric­tion of the vas­cu­lar sup­ply to the area. Cellulitis is as­so­ci­ated with se­vere pain, el­e­vated tem­ per­a­ture, mal­aise, and the pres­ence of a mix­ture of aer­o­bic and an­aer­o­bic path­o­gens. A shift in the lo­cal ox­i­da­tion-reduction po­ten­tial fa­vors the pre­dom­i­nance of an­aer­o­bic bac­te­ria in the ab­scess stage, which be­gins 5 to
7 days af­ter ini­ti­a­tion of the in­fec­tion. During this stage, vig­or­ous mo­bi­li­ za­tion of the host re­sponse lim­its the spread of in­fec­tion and con­trib­utes to pus ac­cu­mu­la­tion in an ab­scess space within the in­fected tis­sue. Since bac­te­ria at these sites are rel­a­tively in­ac­ces­si­ble to sys­temic an­ti­bi­ot­ics, an­ ti­bi­ot­ics alone are usu­ally in­ca­pa­ble of re­solv­ing ab­scesses. Resolution of an ab­scess can be­gin af­ter its drain­age, which may oc­cur as a re­sult of sur­ gi­cal in­ci­sion or by spon­ta­ne­ous rup­ture of the ab­scess and for­ma­tion of a drain­ing si­nus tract.

PENICILLINS
For the large ma­jor­ity of pa­tients who are not al­ler­gic to them, pen­i­cil­lin and amox­i­cil­lin ex­hibit low tox­ic­ity and are of­ten ef­fec­tive for treat­ing mild to moderate odontogenic in­fec­tions. Penicillin V is a bac­te­ri­cidal agent that is ac­tive against strep­to­cocci and many oral an­aer­o­bic spe­cies. Its nar­row spec­ trum is well suited for kill­ing oral bac­te­ria, but it spares a large pro­por­tion of the gut microbiome that would be in­hib­ited by a broad-spectrum agent.
Penicillin V is elim­i­nated by the kid­neys and has a short half-life. CLINDAMYCIN
Clindamycin is a bac­te­rio­static agent that has a large vol­ume of dis­tri­bu­ tion and reaches ef­fec­tive lev­els in most tis­sues, in­clud­ing bone. It is well ab­sorbed from the gut and ex­hib­its good ac­tiv­ity against strep­to­cocci and many Gram-positive an­aer­obes and mod­er­ate to good ac­tiv­ity against staph­y­lo­cocci and many Gram-negative an­aer­obes. These fea­tures make it a good choice for treat­ing mod­er­ate to severe acute odontogenic in­fec­ tions and mild chronic in­fec­tions that do not re­solve af­ter treat­ment with one of the pen­i­cil­lins. Clindamycin is me­ tab­o­lized by the liver and ex­hib­its a higher in­ci­dence of tox­ic­ity than pen­ i­cil­lins. Its most com­mon toxic side ef­fect is di­ar­rhea, but clindamycin may also pre­dis­pose to de­vel­op­ment of co­li­tis.

AZITHROMYCIN
Azithromycin is a macrolide an­ti­bi­otic that is ac­tive against a broad spec­ trum of Gram-positive and Gram-negative path­o­gens and is widely used to treat re­spi­ra­tory tract in­fec­tions. It has mod­er­ate ac­tiv­ity against strep­to­ cocci and an­aer­obes but is less in­hib­i­tory to­ward staph­y­lo­cocci. While it is usu­ally bac­te­rio­static, it pro­duces bac­te­ri­cidal ef­fects at higher con­cen­ tra­tions. Azithromycin and other macrolides are taken up and ac­tively con­cen­trated by a va­ri­ety of host cells, in­clud­ing fi­bro­blasts, ep­i­the­lial cells, and phago­cytes. Once loaded with azithromycin, these cells act as res­er­voirs that help main­tain high an­ti­mi­cro­bial lev­els in tar­get tis­sues (of­ ten higher than con­cen­tra­tions in blood). With its large vol­ume of dis­tri­ bu­tion and long half-life, ef­fec­tive and sus­tained con­cen­tra­tions can be achieved by pre­scrib­ing a 3-day course of a sin­gle 500-mg dose per day. As ex­pected with a sim­ple reg­i­men, pa­tient com­pli­ance is usu­ally good. Azithromycin is use­ful for treat­ing mild to moderate odontogenic in­fec­tions in pa­tients who are al­ler­gic to pen­i­cil­lin. Since it lacks bac­te­ri­cidal ac­tiv­ity, it is not ap­pro­pri­ate for se­vere in­fec­tions or for pa­tients with a com­pro­mised im­mune sys­tem. It is also an ex­pen­sive med­i­ca­tion, and its wide use has con­trib­uted to the emer­gence of re­sis­tance.

The flu­o­ro­quin­o­lones are a class of broad-spectrum bac­te­ri­cidal com­pounds that have rel­a­tively low tox­ic­ity and are use­ful for treat­ing odontogenic in­fec­tions in pen­i­cil­lin-allergic pa­tients. Like the macrolides, these agents are taken up by host cells (es­pe­cially phago­cytes) and have a large vol­ume of dis­tri­bu­tion. They are es­pe­cially good at pen­e­trat­ing bone. The spec­trum of first-generation flu­o­ro­quin­o­lones, which kill many Gram-positive and
Gram-negative bac­te­ria but have low ac­tiv­ity against strep­to­cocci and an­ aer­obes, is not suit­able for treat­ing odontogenic in­fec­tions. Moxifloxacin, a newer agent, has mod­er­ately good ac­tiv­ity against an­aer­obes and good ac­tiv­ity against many Gram-positive and Gram-negative or­gan­isms. While it is ex­pen­sive, it is use­ful for treat­ing chronic in­fec­tions in which an­aer­ obes play a role. Moxifloxacin is well ab­sorbed from the gut and is nor­mally ad­min­is­tered as a 400-mg dose once a day.

Use of Systemic Antibiotics in Periodontal Therapy
Periodontitis is a chronic in­flam­ma­tory dis­ease that de­stroys the sup­port­ ing tis­sues of teeth and, if left un­treated, con­trib­utes to tooth loss. In peri­ odon­ti­tis, polymicrobial com­mu­ni­ties tran­si­tion from host-compatible com­men­sal bac­te­ria to path­o­genic en­ti­ties with the pres­ence of key­stone path­o­gens. The ob­jec­tive of peri­odon­tal scal­ing and root plan­ing (SRP), the stan­dard ap­proach for ini­tial (non­sur­gi­cal) peri­odon­tal ther­apy, is to in­duce clin­i­cal at­tach­ment gain and re­duce pocket prob­ing depth and in­ flam­ma­tion by elim­i­nat­ing path­o­gens from the subgingival biofilm and re­plac­ing them with host-compatible spe­cies. While con­cep­tu­ally sim­ple, this treat­ment is not con­sis­tently ef­fec­tive. Its ef­fec­tive­ness is lim­ited by an­a­tom­i­cal fac­tors (e.g., com­pro­mised ac­cess in deep pock­ets and fur­ca­ tions) and the ex­pe­ri­ence of the op­er­a­tor. As pocket prob­ing depths in­ crease, it is more dif­fi­cult to elim­i­nate subgingival biofilm and in­duce mean­ing­ful changes in mi­cro­bial com­po­si­tion with SRP. A con­trib­ut­ing fac­tor is that some path­o­gens (e.g., Porphyromonas gingivalis, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, and Prevotella intermedia) are ca­pa­ble of in­vad­ing the ep­i­the­lium and con­nec­tive tis­sue ad­ja­cent to peri­odon­tal pock­ets, which makes them dif­fi­cult to elim­i­nate by SRP alone. Moreover, some path­o­gens can col­o­nize sites other than deep pock­ets, in­clud­ing oral mu­cosa, the tongue, and shal­low pock­ets. Studies have in­di­cated that ad­ min­is­tra­tion of an­ti­bi­ot­ics, in con­junc­tion with SRP, can in­duce a rapid and sub­stan­tial re­duc­tion in bac­te­rial lev­els, which fa­cil­i­tates the re­col­o­ni­ za­tion of the subgingival en­vi­ron­ment by host-compatible spe­cies. The dif­ fi­cul­ties as­so­ci­ated with erad­i­cat­ing path­o­gens from the en­tire oral cav­ity and in­creas­ing the pro­por­tion of host-compatible bac­te­ria pro­vi­de a ra­tio­ nale for in­cor­po­rat­ing sys­temic an­ti­bi­ot­ics into the treat­ment of peri­odon­ ti­tis. However, an­ti­bi­ot­ics should be used ju­di­ciously, and their po­ten­tial to in­duce re­sis­tance and other ad­verse re­ac­tions should be weighed against the ex­pected ben­e­fits.
Despite the dif­fi­cul­ties as­so­ci­ated with elim­i­nat­ing subgingival biofilm and re­mov­ing bac­te­rial prod­ucts from roots, most pa­tients with mild-tomoderate chronic peri­odon­ti­tis re­spond fairly well to treat­ment with SRP with­out­ an­ti­bi­ot­ics. With peri­odon­tal pock­ets of 4 to 6 mm in depth, the mean clin­i­cal at­tach­ment gain ob­tained with SRP is 0.55 mm, and the mean prob­ing depth re­duc­tion is 1.29 mm. In pock­ets deeper than 6 mm, SRP pro­vi­des a mean clin­i­cal at­tach­ment gain of 1.19 mm and a mean prob­ing depth re­duc­tion of 2.19 mm.

Use of Antibiotics To Prevent Infection
During some types of den­tal treat­ment (in­clud­ing ex­trac­tions, peri­odon­ tal sur­gery, SRP, peri­odon­tal prob­ing, peri­odon­tal main­te­nance, den­tal im­ plant place­ment, and ini­tial place­ment of or­tho­don­tic bands), there is a high prob­a­bil­ity that oral bac­te­ria will be re­leased into the blood­stream.
Usually, these bac­te­ria are neu­tral­ized by the ac­tions of the in­nate and ac­ quired im­mune sys­tems. In a few pa­tients, how­ever, the bac­te­ria can cause an in­fec­tion in an­other part of the body. For these sus­cep­ti­ble pa­tients, there are cir­cum­stances in which it may be ben­e­fi­cial to ad­min­is­ter a pro­ phy­lac­tic an­ti­bi­otic prior to den­tal treat­ment to de­crease the chance of in­fec­tion. The ra­tio­nale for pre­scrib­ing pro­phy­lac­tic an­ti­bi­ot­ics has been some­what con­tro­ver­sial, how­ever, and the in­di­ca­tions for use have be­come more fo­cused in re­cent years. Currently, the pri­mary in­di­ca­tion is for pa­ tients at risk for de­vel­op­ing in­fec­tive en­do­car­di­tis, an in­fec­tion of the lin­ ing of the heart cham­bers or valves.
Antibiotic pro­phy­laxis only makes sense when the po­ten­tial to pre­vent an in­fec­tion out­weighs the risks of tak­ing an an­ti­bi­otic. The ad­verse ef­fects of an­ti­bi­ot­ics in­clude di­ar­rhea, al­ler­gic re­ac­tions, and the po­ten­tial to in­ duce bac­te­rial re­sis­tance to the an­ti­bi­otic. Evidence sug­gests that bac­ter­ emias pro­duced by den­tal pro­ce­dures rarely cause in­fec­tive en­do­car­di­tis in healthy pa­tients. Transient bac­ter­emias as­so­ci­ated with toothbrush­ing and other rou­tine daily ac­tiv­i­ties are more likely to con­trib­ute to en­do­car­di­tis than den­tal pro­ce­dures. For these rea­sons, the Amer­i­can Heart Association rec­om­mends that pro­phy­lac­tic an­ti­bi­ot­ics should be used only for pa­tients with car­diac con­di­tions as­so­ci­ated with the high­est risk of ad­verse out­ comes from in­fec­tive en­do­car­di­tis, which in­clude the fol­low­ing:

•	 Presence of a pros­thetic car­diac valve or pros­thetic ma­te­rial used for car­diac valve re­pair
•	 Previous in­fec­tive en­do­car­di­tis
•	 Several types of con­gen­i­tal heart dis­ease (CHD), as fol­lows:

°	 Unrepaired cy­a­notic CHD, in­clud­ing pal­li­a­tive shunts and con­

duits
°	 Completely re­paired con­gen­i­tal heart de­fect with pros­thetic ma­te­

rial or de­vice, whether placed by sur­gery or by cath­e­ter in­ter­ven­ tion, dur­ing the first 6 months af­ter the pro­ce­dure
°	 Repaired CHD with re­sid­ual de­fects at the site or ad­ja­cent to the site of a pros­thetic patch or pros­thetic de­vice (which in­hibit endothelialization)

•	 Cardiac trans­plan­ta­tion re­cip­i­ents who de­velop car­diac valvulopathy

These con­di­tions al­most al­ways have an in­creased risk of ad­verse out­comes, so pa­tients with these con­di­tions can ben­e­fit from pro­phy­laxis prior to den­tal pro­ce­dures that in­volve ma­nip­u­la­tion of gin­gi­val tis­sue or the periapical re­gion of teeth or per­fo­ra­tion of the oral mu­cosa. Amoxicillin is the agent of choice for most pa­tients (see Fig. 5). When taken orally, amox­i­cil­lin is ab­sorbed rap­idly and pro­vi­des high and sus­tained con­cen­ tra­tions in the blood. Alternative reg­i­mens for pa­tients who are al­ler­gic to amox­i­cil­lin or un­able to take oral med­i­ca­tions are de­scribed in Fig. 5.

Antibiotics: Mechanisms, Resistance, and Use in Dentistry

In the past, an­ti­bi­otic pro­phy­laxis was rec­om­mended prior to den­tal treat­ment to pre­vent pros­thetic joint in­fec­tions (PJIs) in pa­tients who have had one or more joints re­placed. However, there is no con­clu­sive ev­i­dence in the lit­er­a­ture that es­tab­lishes a di­rect link be­tween bac­ter­emias as­so­ci­ ated with den­tal treat­ment and in­fec­tion of pros­thetic joints. In ad­di­tion, there is lit­tle ev­i­dence that an­ti­bi­otic pro­phy­laxis pre­vents PJIs. Thus, the po­ten­tial ben­e­fits of an­ti­bi­otic pro­phy­laxis do not ex­ceed the risks of an­ ti­bi­otic treat­ment for most pa­tients. The Amer­i­can Dental Association does not rec­om­mend the rou­tine use of an­ti­bi­otic pro­phy­laxis prior to den­tal pro­ce­dures in pa­tients with pros­thetic joints. For pa­tients who have had com­pli­ca­tions with joint re­place­ment sur­gery or have a med­i­cal con­ di­tion that com­pro­mises their abil­ity to cope with an in­fec­tion (e.g., im­ mu­no­sup­pres­sion, in­su­lin-dependent di­a­be­tes, rheu­ma­toid ar­thri­tis, or sys­temic lu­pus er­y­the­ma­to­sus), pro­phy­lac­tic an­ti­bi­ot­ics should only be pre­ scribed af­ter con­sul­ta­tion with the pa­tient’s or­tho­pe­dic sur­geon. Ideally, the or­tho­pe­dic sur­geon should spec­ify the an­ti­bi­otic reg­i­men to be used in cases in which an­ti­bi­ot­ics are deemed nec­es­sary.

K E Y  P O I N T S

Antibiotics can be clas­si­fied ac­cord­ing to their chem­i­cal struc­ture, type of ac­tiv­ity (in­hi­bi­tion of growth versus out­right kill­ing), an­ti­mi­cro­bial spec­trum, and mech­a­nism of ac­tion. Antibiotics pro­duce their an­ti­mi­cro­bial ef­fects by tar­get­ing met­a­bolic path­ways that are es­sen­tial for bac­te­rial sur­vival, in­clud­ing cell wall syn­the­sis and mat­u­ra­tion, pro­tein syn­the­sis by ri­bo­somes,
DNA tran­scrip­tion or rep­li­ca­tion, and fo­late bio­syn­the­sis.

History has re­peat­edly shown that newly in­tro­duced an­ti­bi­ot­ics lose much of their clin­i­cal ef­fec­tive­ness af­ter they have been in use for sev­eral years. Since the de­vel­op­ment of new an­ti­bi­ot­ics has slowed, re­sis­tance is a se­ri­ous threat to pub­lic health and the econ­omy.
Bacteria can ac­quire re­sis­tance through spon­ta­ne­ous mu­ta­tion or by trans­fer of mo­bile ge­netic el­e­ments con­tain­ing re­sis­tance genes.
Bacteria can be­come re­sis­tant by ex­press­ing en­zymes that in­ac­ti­vate or mod­ify the an­ti­bi­otic, by ex­press­ing trans­port pro­teins that pump the an­ti­bi­otic out­ of bac­te­ria, by mod­i­fy­ing the in­tra­cel­lu­lar tar­get of the an­ti­bi­otic, or by downregulating an­ti­bi­otic dif­fu­sion into the cell through outer mem­brane chan­nels (porins).

Bacterial bio­films pres­ent mul­ti­ple mech­a­nisms for tol­er­ance (also termed re­cal­ci­trance) to an­ti­bi­ot­ics and an­ti­mi­cro­bial agents

in­volv­ing the pres­ence of the EPS ma­trix and al­tered mi­cro­en­vi­ron­ ment, ge­netic changes (gene trans­fer, SCVs, ge­netic di­ver­sity), dor­mancy, and per­sister cells. Conventional an­ti­mi­cro­bi­als are se­lected based on their abil­ity to kill rap­idly grow­ing plank­tonic cells with­out­ con­sid­er­ation of ef­fec­tive­ness in the unique con­di­tions of the biofilm mi­cro­en­vi­ron­ment. Targeting bac­te­rial cells alone will likely not be enough; new agents and ther­a­peu­tics that tar­get the biofilm in a ho­lis­tic sense as a dis­tinct en­tity will need to be in­tro­duced.

Since the risk of de­vel­op­ing an­ti­bi­otic re­sis­tance in­creases with the fre­quency and du­ra­tion of bac­te­rial ex­po­sure to an­ti­bi­ot­ics, the key to min­i­miz­ing re­sis­tance is to use an­ti­bi­ot­ics se­lec­tively and spar­ingly, bal­anc­ing the risks of in­duc­ing re­sis­tance against the ben­e­fits that can be ob­tained by kill­ing or in­hib­it­ing bac­te­ria.

Antibiotics are used to treat odontogenic in­fec­tions, to en­hance the out­comes of non­sur­gi­cal treat­ment of ag­gres­sive and se­vere chronic peri­odon­ti­tis, and, in se­lected pa­tients, to pre­vent in­fec­tions that could po­ten­tially re­sult from bac­ter­emias in­duced by den­tal pro­ce­dures.
