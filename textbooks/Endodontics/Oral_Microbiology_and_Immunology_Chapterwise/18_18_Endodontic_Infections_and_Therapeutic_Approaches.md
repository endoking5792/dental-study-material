INTRODUCTION
Endodontics re­lates to the study and treat­ment of dis­eases of the den­tal pulp tis­sue, lo­cated within the cor­o­nal pulp cham­ber and ex­tend­ing into the root ca­nal. Dental pulp largely con­sists of a loose con­nec­tive tis­sue containing as the main cells odontoblasts, fibroblasts, undifferentiated mes­ enchymal cells, and im­mune cells. Pulp is highly vascularized, nerve rich, and ster­ile. Blood ves­sels and nerves en­ter through one or more api­cal fo­ ram­ina at the apex of the tooth. Under nor­mal healthy con­di­tions, in­tact enamel and den­tin, along with a healthy periodontium, pro­tect the pulp, act­ing as a phys­i­cal bar­rier to in­jury and mi­cro­bial in­tru­sion. Infections of the pulp are al­most al­ways sec­ond­ary to breaches in this bar­rier re­ sult­ing from car­ies, iat­ro­genic causes, or trauma and are the re­sult of op­por­tu­nis­tic in­fec­tions with en­dog­e­nous oral bac­te­ria; how­ever, yeasts, ar­chaea, and vi­ruses may also be oc­ca­sion­ally found in in­fected ca­nals.
Bacteria are the ma­jor mi­cro­or­gan­isms im­pli­cated in the eti­ol­ogy of api­ cal peri­odon­ti­tis (also known as periapical peri­odon­ti­tis or periradicular peri­odon­ti­tis). The dis­ease is char­ac­ter­ized by an acute or chronic in­ flam­ma­tory le­sion around the apex (tip) of a tooth root as an ex­ten­sion of bac­te­rial in­va­sion of the tooth pulp. Bacteria or­ga­nize them­selves within the root ca­nal as biofilm com­mu­ni­ties, and, in long-stand­ing in­ fec­tions, bac­te­rial cells can prop­a­gate to other ar­eas of the ca­nal sys­tem, in­clud­ing den­tinal tu­bules, re­cesses, isthmi, and ram­i­fi­ca­tions. In these re­gions, bac­te­ria are more dif­fi­cult to reach and eliminate dur­ing non­sur­ gi­cal root ca­nal treat­ment.

Once es­tab­lished in the root ca­nal, bac­te­ria even­tu­ally con­tact the peri­ odon­tal tis­sues via api­cal and lat­eral fo­ram­ina to in­duce chronic or acute in­flam­ma­tory re­sponses. The chronic re­sponse is usu­ally asymp­tom­atic and al­most in­vari­ably re­sults in bone re­sorp­tion around the root apex.
The acute re­sponse gen­er­ally gives rise to symp­toms of pain and may prog­ress to an ab­scess, a col­lec­tion of pus at the top of the root, with pos­ si­ble sys­temic com­pli­ca­tions. Periapical cysts, or ra­dic­u­lar cysts, are round or pear-shaped struc­tures that may reach large diameters if left untreated. A cyst de­vel­ops from a slowly grow­ing flu­id-filled ep­i­the­lial sac at the tooth apex, orig­i­nat­ing from pulpal ne­cro­sis.

The ul­ti­mate goal of root ca­nal treat­ment is to pre­vent, or pro­mote heal­ing of, api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis. Because api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis has an in­fec­tious eti­ol­ogy, the ra­tio­nale for treat­ment is to pre­vent or erad­i­cate root ca­nal in­fec­tions.

OVERALL DEF­I­NI­TIONS AND CONCEPTS
Based on the an­a­tomic lo­ca­tion, end­odon­tic in­fec­tions can be clas­si­fied as intraradicular or extraradicular. Intraradicular in­fec­tion, as the name im­ plies, oc­curs in­side the root and may spread to the en­tire root ca­nal sys­ tem, es­pe­cially in long-stand­ing pro­cesses. Extraradicular in­fec­tion is a se­quela to intraradicular in­fec­tion and is caused by bac­te­ria that leave the api­cal or lat­eral fo­ram­ina and es­tab­lish them­selves out­side the root ca­nal, ei­ther in the body of the api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis le­sion (e.g., ab­scesses and api­cal ac­ti­no­my­co­sis) or ad­hered to the outer root sur­face (e.g., extraradicular biofilm).
Intraradicular in­fec­tions can be cat­e­go­rized as pri­mary, sec­ond­ary, or per­sis­tent, de­pend­ing on the time at which par­tici­pat­ing bac­te­ria gain en­try into the root ca­nal. Primary in­fec­tion is caused by bac­te­ria that ini­tially in­vaded and col­o­nized the ne­crotic pulp tis­sue and is the cause of pri­mary api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis (Fig. 1). Persistent in­fec­tions are caused by bac­te­ria from a pri­mary in­fec­tion that have re­sisted intracanal an­ti­mi­cro­bial mea­sures and have man­aged to en­dure pe­ri­ods of nu­tri­ent dep­ri­va­tion in a treated ca­nal.
Secondary in­fec­tions are caused by bac­te­ria that are not pres­ent in the pri­ mary in­fec­tion and that are in­tro­duced into the root ca­nal at some point dur­ing or af­ter pro­fes­sional in­ter­ven­tion. The en­try can be due to mi­cro­bial con­tam­i­na­tion oc­cur­ring dur­ing treat­ment, be­tween ap­point­ments, or even af­ter root ca­nal ob­tu­ra­tion. Bacterial spe­cies in­volved in sec­ond­ary in­fec­ tions may be oral or nonoral, de­pend­ing on the cause of in­fec­tion. Persistent and sec­ond­ary in­fec­tions are re­spon­si­ble for sev­eral clin­i­cal prob­lems, in­clud­ ing post­treat­ment api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis (Fig. 2).

PATHWAYS OF ROOT CANAL INFECTION
Under nondisease con­di­tions, den­tin and pulp are ster­ile tis­sues iso­lated from the oral en­vi­ron­ment (and microbiota) by an in­tact hard tis­sue en­ case­ment and a healthy periodontium. If the pro­tec­tive lay­ers of enamel or ce­men­tum are lost as a re­sult of car­ies, crown frac­ture or crack, scal­ing and root plan­ning pro­ce­dures, or at­tri­tion/abrasion, the pulpo-dentin com­plex is ex­posed to the oral en­vi­ron­ment and be­comes at risk of in­fec­tion.
Caries is the most com­mon cause of pulp ex­po­sure and in­fec­tion. In ad­di­tion, bac­te­ria com­pris­ing the biofilm in peri­odon­tal pock­ets may reach the pulp via den­tinal tu­bules at the cer­vi­cal re­gion of the tooth, or lat­eral and api­cal fo­ram­ina, de­pend­ing on pocket depth. The most dra­ matic ef­fects of peri­odon­tal dis­ease on the pulp are ob­served when the peri­odon­tal pocket reaches the root apex. In these cases, bac­te­rial bio­films can cause rup­ture of the main neurovascular bun­dle that pen­e­trates through the api­cal fo­ra­men, lead­ing to pulp ne­cro­sis and fur­ther in­fec­tion by a ret­ro­grade route. In sum­mary, api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis is an in­flam­ma­tory dis­ease with dif­ fer­ent clin­i­cal man­i­fes­ta­tions and is caused by mi­cro­bial in­fec­tion of the den­tal root ca­nal sys­tem. MICROBIAL AGENTS OF ENDODONTIC INFECTIONS
The com­po­si­tion of the end­odon­tic microbiota var­ies ac­cord­ing to the type of in­fec­tion and the var­i­ous forms of api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis. In gen­eral, over 500 dif­fer­ent bac­te­rial spe­cies or phylotypes have al­ready been de­ tected in in­fected root ca­nals by cul­ture or by mo­lec­u­lar meth­ods such as
16S rRNA gene se­quenc­ing or high-throughput se­quenc­ing. At high phy­ lo­ge­netic lev­els, these bac­te­ria be­long to ap­prox­i­ma­tely 20 phyla, with the most com­mon rep­re­sen­ta­tive spe­cies be­long­ing to the phyla Firmicutes,
Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Fusobacteria, Proteobacteria, Spirochaetes, and Synergistetes. About 40 to 60% of the bac­te­rial phylotypes found in in­ fected ca­nals have yet to be cul­ti­vated and fully char­ac­ter­ized (the so-called as-yet-uncultivated phylotypes). In spite of the large list of end­odon­tic bac­te­rial spe­cies or phylotypes, about 20 to 30 spe­cies have been in­vari­ ably de­tected in the large ma­jor­ity of stud­ies and may be re­garded as the main can­di­date end­odon­tic path­o­gens (Table 1). However, it is im­por­tant to note that the abun­dance of par­tic­u­lar spe­cies de­pends on the tim­ing of sam­ple col­lec­tion, es­pe­cially con­sid­er­ing the dy­namic na­ture of biofilm life cy­cles whereby mi­cro­bial com­mu­ni­ties change rap­idly or in suc­ces­ sion over the course of biofilm de­vel­op­men­tal pro­cesses. Furthermore, the rel­a­tive pro­por­tion of a par­tic­u­lar spe­cies may not nec­es­sar­ily re­flect its vir­u­lence, as path­o­gens can or­ches­trate eco­log­i­cal changes or dysbiosis, even when in low num­bers within the mi­cro­bial com­mu­nity, trig­ger­ing the tran­si­tion from a nonvirulent to a path­o­genic biofilm. Enterococcus faecalis, for ex­am­ple, is no­to­ri­ously dif­fi­cult to erad­i­cate from the in­fected root ca­nal be­cause of its re­sis­tance to some an­ti­mi­cro­bi­als used in end­odon­ tic ther­apy, abil­ity to cope with var­i­ous stresses such as high pH, and ef­fi­ ciency in in­vad­ing den­tinal tu­bules, thereby per­sist­ing in the en­vi­ron­ment for pro­longed pe­ri­ods. The typ­i­cal fea­tures of the dif­fer­ent types of end­o­ don­tic in­fec­tions are de­tailed be­low.

A long-held de­sire in end­odon­tic mi­cro­bi­ol­ogy has been to find a sin­gle or at least a group of bac­te­rial spe­cies that is re­spon­si­ble for acute symp­ toms. However, while sev­eral bac­te­rial spe­cies seem to be more prev­a­lent when as­so­ci­ated with pain, the very same spe­cies have also been en­coun­tered in asymp­tom­atic cases. The pos­si­bil­ity ex­ists that some of these spe­cies re­ally play a role in mak­ing the bac­te­rial mixed com­mu­nity more vir­u­lent. Several other fac­tors can be re­garded as in­flu­en­tial to the de­vel­op­ment of symp­toms, in­clud­ing dif­fer­ences in vir­u­lence among clonal types of the same spe­cies, bac­te­rial in­ter­ac­tions in the mul­ti­spe­cies com­mu­nity re­sult­ing in col­lec­tive path­o­ge­nic­ity, to­tal and spe­cific bac­te­rial counts, and host-related fac­tors.

Microbes Associated with Posttreatment
Apical Periodontitis
Posttreatment api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis is the form of the dis­ease as­so­ci­ated with end­odon­ti­cally treated teeth. It is usu­ally a per­sis­tent dis­ease, caused by bac­te­ria that en­dured treat­ment pro­ce­dures. However, in some in­ stances, api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis was not pres­ent at the time of treat­ment and ap­peared some time later. These cases rep­re­sent an emer­gent dis­ease, which is usu­ally caused by sec­ond­ary intraradicular in­fec­tion.
There is an in­creased risk of root ca­nal treat­ment fail­ure when bac­te­ ria re­sist the ef­fects of treat­ment. The main cause of bac­te­rial per­sis­tence is in­ad­e­quate treat­ment, but even dil­i­gent ther­apy may oc­ca­sion­ally fail to com­pletely erad­i­cate bac­te­ria (or re­duce the num­bers to lev­els that do not cause fur­ther prob­lems) from the root ca­nal sys­tem. Persistent bac­te­ria found in sam­ples taken im­me­di­ately af­ter root ca­nal prep­a­ra­tion pro­ce­dures are usu­ally Gram-positive spe­cies, in­clud­ing Streptococcus spp., E. faecalis,
Actinomyces spp., Olsenella spp., Parvimonas micra, Pseudoramibacter alactolyticus, and Propionibacterium spp.
Management of teeth with post­treat­ment api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis usu­ally in­volves retreatment. The microbiota found in teeth un­der­go­ing retreatment is com­posed of a more re­stricted group of spe­cies than that in pri­mary in­fec­ tions. Canals that were ap­par­ently treated ac­cord­ing to ac­cept­able stan­dards, even with per­sis­tent dis­ease, have been shown to har­bor fewer than 5 spe­cies.
On the other hand, teeth with sub­stan­dard pre­vi­ous root ca­nal treat­ment may har­bor 10 to 30 spe­cies, a fig­ure sim­i­lar to that found in pri­mary in­fec­tions.
Bacterial counts in retreatment cases vary from 103 to 107 cells per ca­nal. The poorer the treat­ment qual­ity or the larger the post­treat­ment api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis, the higher the num­ber of spe­cies per ca­nal. The most com­monly de­tected spe­cies in post­treat­ment teeth is E. faecalis, a fac­ul­ta­ tive an­aer­o­bic Gram-positive coc­cus, that is rarely found in pri­mary in­ fec­tions. Streptococcus spe­cies are also very fre­quent and dom­i­nant in sam­ples taken from teeth with post­treat­ment dis­ease. Other bac­te­rial spe­ cies, such as P. alactolyticus, Propionibacterium spp., P. micra, and Filifactor alocis, are also com­monly in­volved in per­sis­tent intraradicular in­fec­tions.
Yeasts, es­pe­cially Candida spe­cies, which are only spo­rad­i­cally found in pri­mary in­fec­tions, are more fre­quent in root ca­nal-treated teeth.

Apart from acute and chronic api­cal ab­scesses, it re­mains con­tro­ver­sial whether or not asymp­tom­atic chronic api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis le­sions may be in­fected for a long pe­riod be­yond ini­tial tis­sue in­va­sion.
In some teeth with post­treat­ment api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis, es­pe­cially those with symp­toms, extraradicular bac­te­rial in­fec­tion has been ob­served as a biofilm that is ei­ther ad­her­ing to the outer api­cal root sur­face (some­times with cal­ci­fied ar­eas re­sem­bling peri­odon­tal cal­cu­lus) or form­ing co­he­sive ac­ti­no­my­cotic col­o­nies within the body of the in­flam­ma­tory le­sion. Species of the gen­era Actinomyces, Propionibacterium, Fusobacterium, Prevotella,
Treponema, Porphyromonas, Tannerella, Streptococcus, and Campylobacter have been iden­ti­fied in these le­sions. Extraradicular in­fec­tions may not be man­age­able by non­sur­gi­cal retreatment and may re­quire perira­ dicular sur­gery to re­store periapical health and save the tooth.

BIOFILMS AND THE ENDODONTIC MICROENVIRONMENT
A biofilm is a ses­sile mul­ti­cel­lu­lar com­mu­nity com­posed of mi­cro­bial cells firmly at­tached to a sur­face and en­meshed in a self-pro­duced ma­trix of ex­tra­cel­lu­lar po­ly­meric sub­stances. In gen­eral, the ex­tra­cel­lu­lar ma­trix is the ma­jor com­po­nent of the biofilm mass (85 to 90%), while cells com­ prise a mi­nor com­po­nent (10 to 15%). Biofilms rep­re­sent the main form in which bac­te­ria or­ga­nize them­selves in end­odon­tic in­fec­tions.
In un­treated teeth with ne­crotic pulps, plank­tonic bac­te­ria of­ten oc­ cur in the lu­men of the main root ca­nal, sus­pended in a fluid phase and/or en­meshed in the ne­crotic pulp tis­sue. From a ther­a­peu­tic stand­point, these bac­te­ria can be eas­ily ac­cessed and elim­i­nated by in­stru­ments and irrig­ ants used dur­ing end­odon­tic treat­ment. However, in teeth with pri­mary or post­treat­ment api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis, bac­te­ria form­ing ses­sile mul­ti­spe­cies bio­films are usu­ally ob­served ad­her­ing to the den­tinal walls of the root ca­nal sys­tem and may rep­re­sent a chal­lenge for proper dis­in­fec­tion (Fig. 3).
Bacterial bio­films are pres­ent in the api­cal part of the root ca­nal sys­tem in 70 to 80% of the teeth with pri­mary or post­treat­ment api­cal peri­odon­ ti­tis. Root ca­nal bio­films are usu­ally thick, mul­ti­lay­ered, and com­posed of dif­fer­ent bac­te­rial morphotypes. There seems to be a high in­ter­in­di­vid­ ual var­i­abil­ity in the mor­phol­ogy of end­odon­tic bio­films. In many cases, bio­films cov­er­ing the root ca­nal walls are faced and sur­rounded by ag­glom­ er­a­tions of po­ly­mor­pho­nu­clear neu­tro­phils from the ex­u­date that seeps into the ca­nal.

Bacterial ad­ hesins lead­ing to col­la­gen bind­ing and co­op­er­a­tive interbacterial coadhe­ sion may in­flu­ence the abil­ity of cer­tain spe­cies to in­vade den­tinal tu­bules.
Invasion by fac­ul­ta­tive an­aer­obes gen­er­ates en­vi­ron­men­tal con­di­tions (e.g., low re­dox, tis­sue dam­age), which pro­motes the growth of an­aer­o­bic or­gan­isms.
Endodontic bio­films may even ex­tend to other ar­eas of the root ca­nal sys­tem, in­clud­ing lat­eral ca­nals, api­cal ram­i­fi­ca­tions, isthmi, and re­cesses.
In these ar­eas, bac­te­ria are more dif­fi­cult to reach and elim­i­nate dur­ing treat­ment. Biofilms are more fre­quent in the api­cal root ca­nals of teeth with large api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis le­sions, es­pe­cially those his­to­path­o­log­i­ cally di­ag­nosed as cysts. These le­sions com­prise long-stand­ing path­o­logic pro­cesses and may have a poorer prog­no­sis for heal­ing fol­low­ing non­sur­ gi­cal root ca­nal treat­ment. Biofilms ad­her­ing to the outer root sur­face around the api­cal or lat­eral fo­ram­ina are a form of extra­radicular in­fec­ tion. However, oc­cur­rence of extraradicular bio­films is rare and is re­ported to oc­cur in <6% of cases with api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis. Extraradicular bio­ films are al­most al­ways as­so­ci­ated with intraradicular bio­films and clin­i­cal symp­toms. In many cases, the extraradicular biofilm may ex­hibit ar­eas of cal­ci­fi­ca­tion that re­sem­ble peri­odon­tal cal­cu­lus, even in teeth with no peri­odon­tal pock­ets.

There is a grow­ing be­lief that the mul­ti­spe­cies biofilm com­ mu­nity is the unit of path­o­ge­nic­ity, with spe­cific path­o­gens or­ches­trat­ing changes in the com­mu­nity to cause dis­ease. Likewise, it is also pos­si­ble that some of the spe­cies fre­ quently found in the path­o­genic mi­lieu may be trig­ger­ing spa­tio­tem­po­ral mi­cro­bial and bio­chem­i­cal changes that war­rant fur­ther elu­ci­da­tion given the dy­namic na­ture of the biofilm life style. Apical peri­odon­ti­tis is a re­sult of com­plex and dy­namic host-pathogen in­ter­ac­tions. The host de­fense fac­tors are char­ac­ter­ized by co­ex­is­tence of in­nate and adap­tive im­mune re­sponses, which may have both pro­tec­tive and de­struc­tive ef­fects (e.g., pus for­ma­tion and bone re­sorp­tion). The symp­ tom­atic forms of the dis­ease in­clude acute api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis and acute api­cal ab­scess. The lat­ter com­prises a se­vere acute in­flam­ma­tory re­ac­tion of the periradicular tis­sues, char­ac­ter­ized by rapid on­set, pus for­ma­tion, spon­ ta­ne­ous and throb­bing pain, ten­der­ness to chew­ing, and swell­ing of the ad­ ja­cent soft tis­sues. The asymp­tom­atic forms are his­to­path­o­log­i­cally clas­si­fied as gran­u­loma (chron­i­cally in­flamed tis­sue sep­a­rated from the bone by a fi­brous cap­sule) or cyst (sim­i­lar to the gran­u­loma, ex­cept for the pres­ence of a path­o­log­i­cal cav­ity lined by ep­i­the­lium). The chronic api­cal ab­scess is as­so­ ci­ated with a si­nus tract, from which there is usu­ally an in­ter­mit­tent dis­ charge of pus.

ROOT CANAL TREATMENT: CHALLENGES AND CURRENT
THERAPEUTIC APPROACHES
The aim of end­odon­tic treat­ment is to al­le­vi­ate symp­toms of pulpal and periradicular or­i­gin and pre­vent or treat api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis. Endodontic pro­ce­dures should be per­formed un­der asep­tic con­di­tions to avoid the in­ tro­duc­tion of mi­cro­bial con­tam­i­nants into the root ca­nal sys­tem that could cause a sec­ond­ary in­fec­tion. Treatment it­self es­sen­tially con­sists of de­bride­ ment aided by ir­ri­ga­tion, with the pur­pose of re­mov­ing bac­te­ria lo­cated on the root ca­nal walls and within den­tinal tu­bules at a small dis­tance from the pulp sur­face, de­bris, and po­ten­tial sub­strates for bac­te­rial re­growth.
An intracanal med­i­ca­tion (ICM) that re­mains ac­tive be­tween ap­point­ments may be nec­es­sary to en­sure max­i­mal re­duc­tions in bac­te­rial cell num­bers and en­do­toxin (li­po­po­ly­sac­cha­ride [LPS], from Gram-negative bac­te­ria) in in­fected root ca­nals. All pro­ce­dures are in­tended to es­tab­lish con­di­tions for in­fec­tion to be con­trolled and not re­cur. In this re­gard, it is im­por­tant that the pre­pared root ca­nal be prop­erly filled, ei­ther by a tis­sue-compatible root ca­nal fill­ing ma­te­rial (ob­tu­ra­tion) or, in some spe­cific cases, by new healthy tis­sue (re­gen­er­a­tive end­odon­tic pro­ce­dures). Furthermore, place­ ment of a de­fin­i­tive well-adapted cor­o­nal res­to­ra­tion fol­low­ing root ca­nal treat­ment helps pre­vent fur­ther cor­o­nal microleakage, root ca­nal re­in­fec­ tion, and root frac­ture, thus con­trib­ut­ing to the long-term suc­cess of the treat­ment.

Variations in Bacterial Susceptibility to Disinfection Procedures
As men­tioned pre­vi­ously, end­odon­tic bac­te­ria are found sus­pended in the lu­men of the main root ca­nal (plank­tonic form) or ad­her­ing to the ca­nal walls, form­ing biofilm struc­tures (ses­sile form). They can pen­e­trate into den­tinal tu­bules and reach re­cesses, isthmi, and ram­i­fi­ca­tions of the root ca­ nal sys­tem. Furthermore, bac­te­ria can be found at the gut­ta-percha/sealer in­ter­face and can form bio­films on the ex­ter­nal api­cal root sur­face. Colo­ nization of these sites is di­rectly re­lated to the time of in­fec­tion and the com­po­si­tion of the microbiota.
Bacteria in the plank­tonic form and those ad­her­ing to the root ca­nal walls are mostly re­moved by the chemomechanical ac­tion of in­stru­ments, ir­ri­ga­tion, and chem­i­cal aux­il­iary sub­stances dur­ing root ca­nal prep­a­ra­ tion. However, the ef­fi­cacy of these pro­ce­dures may also de­pend upon the sus­cep­ti­bil­ity of the in­volved spe­cies, which may not be uni­form. More­ over, oc­cur­rence of bac­te­ria in ar­eas that are dif­fi­cult to ac­cess or are even in­ac­ces­si­ble to treat­ment, such as den­tinal tu­bules, ram­i­fi­ca­tions, and isthmi, rep­re­sents a chal­lenge for dis­in­fec­tion. Residual bac­te­ria sur­viv­ing in these ar­eas may put the treat­ment out­come at risk, es­pe­cially if they find a sus­tain­able source of nu­tri­ent sup­ply from the periradicular tis­sues.
Some per­sis­tent bac­te­ria may not cause re­in­fec­tion if com­mu­ni­ca­tion with the periradicular tis­sues is de­nied by proper ob­tu­ra­tion. Posttreatment re­sid­ual in­fec­tion with clin­i­cal im­pli­ca­tion will de­pend, among other fac­tors, on the bal­ance be­tween bac­te­rial and host de­fense fac­tors. In most cases, the re­duc­tion of bac­te­rial and en­do­toxin lev­els ob­tained by end­odon­tic treat­ ment may be enough to pro­mote periradicular tis­sue heal­ing. Studies eval­ u­at­ing the suc­cess of root ca­nal treat­ment in teeth with pri­mary apical periodontitis have shown suc­cess rates rang­ing from 80 to 94%. However, in teeth with posttreatment apical periodontitis, the suc­cess rates range from
66.7 to 84.5% in the ab­sence or the pres­ence of cor­o­nal seal­ing, re­spec­tive­ly.

Chemomechanical Preparation
The aim of chemomechanical prep­a­ra­tion (CMP) is to clean, shape, dis­in­ fect, and en­large the root ca­nal us­ing end­odon­tic in­stru­ments as­so­ci­ated with ir­ri­ga­tion and the use of aux­il­iary chem­i­cal sub­stances. Since bac­te­ ria are also found in­fect­ing den­tinal tu­bules, in­stru­men­ta­tion should re­ move some por­tion of den­tin as well. However, care must be taken to not ex­ces­sively re­move den­tinal struc­ture, which might weaken the root and pre­dis­pose it to frac­ture.
The ef­fec­tive­ness of us­ing ei­ther hand or ro­tary in­stru­men­ta­tion in re­ mov­ing bac­te­ria from the root ca­nal sys­tem has been shown in many stud­ ies. The de­vel­op­ment of nick­el-titanium (NiTi) ro­tary file sys­tems has re­sulted in sig­nif­i­cant prog­ress in me­chan­i­cal prep­a­ra­tion of the root ca­nal space, pro­mot­ing greater en­large­ment of the root ca­nal with less trans­por­ ta­tion than hand in­stru­men­ta­tion with stain­less-steel fi­les.
Sterilization of the root ca­nal sys­tem is not pos­si­ble with cur­rent tech­ niques, in­stru­ments, and sub­stances, and thus dis­in­fec­tion is the re­al­is­tic goal of treat­ment. To achieve op­ti­mal dis­in­fec­tion of the root ca­nal sys­tem,
CMP should elim­i­nate or at least sub­stan­tially re­duce plank­tonic and ses­ sile bac­te­ria, sub­strate, and con­cen­tra­tions of bac­te­rial vir­u­lence fac­tors, in­clud­ing LPS (a ma­jor cell wall com­po­nent of Gram-negative bac­te­ria)

Larger api­cal prep­a­ra­tion sizes pro­vide greater bac­te­rial and LPS re­ duc­tion dur­ing end­odon­tic treat­ment. Apical en­large­ment al­lows for bet­ter re­moval of in­fected den­tin, en­hances the flush­ing ac­tion of irrigants in the api­cal third, and sig­nif­i­cantly re­duces the bac­te­rial load and LPS lev­els in the ca­nal. The pos­si­ble draw­backs of larger api­cal prep­a­ra­tion sizes in­clude the risks of ac­ci­dents and the weak­en­ing of the root, which can pre­dis­ pose to fu­ture frac­ture. To cir­cum­vent the lat­ter, the use of larger in­stru­ments with small or var­i­able ta­pers has been rec­om­mended, so that while the api­cal prep­a­ra­tion is larger, there is no ex­ces­sive re­moval of den­tin in the cor­o­nal por­tion of the root. Clinical stud­ies have in­di­cated that api­cal en­ large­ment re­sults in in­creased heal­ing out­come for pa­tients with ne­crotic pulp and api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis.
Because mi­cro­or­gan­isms can oc­cur in the very api­cal por­tion of the ca­nal of teeth with api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis, the api­cal limit of prep­a­ra­tion should be as close as pos­si­ble to the ca­nal ter­mi­nus. Studies dem­on­strate that the best treat­ment out­come is ob­served when the ter­mi­nus of prep­a­ ra­tion and ob­tu­ra­tion is be­tween 0 and 2 mm short of the root apex.
The in­tro­duc­tion of re­cip­ro­cat­ing NiTi sin­gle-file-based sys­tems has raised new per­spec­tives on the me­chan­i­cal root ca­nal prep­a­ra­tion. Recipro­ cating in­stru­men­ta­tion has been pro­posed to sim­plify and shorten the root ca­nal shap­ing pro­ce­dure as well as to re­duce in­stru­ment fa­tigue. Most stud­ ies show that re­cip­ro­cat­ing sin­gle-file sys­tems are as ef­fec­tive as multifile ro­tary sys­tems in re­mov­ing bac­te­ria and en­do­tox­ins from root ca­nals.
It has been shown that in­stru­ments do not uni­formly touch all­ the root ca­nal walls. Micro-computed to­mo­graphic anal­y­sis of CMP pro­ce­ dures re­ported that the amount of un­touched ca­nal area ranged from 28 to 61% af­ter us­ing sin­gle-file re­cip­ro­cat­ing and con­ven­tional multifile ro­ tary sys­tems. Untouched sur­face ar­eas of the root ca­nal walls may har­bor bio­films that re­main un­af­fected by prep­a­ra­tion and may com­pro­mise the long-term treat­ment out­come.
Current tech­nol­ogy for me­chan­i­cal prep­a­ra­tion is par­tic­u­larly in­ef­fec­ tive in de­brid­ing oval-shaped ca­nals, leav­ing un­touched fins or re­cesses on the buc­cal and/or lin­gual ex­ten­sions. These re­cesses may har­bor un­af­ fected re­sid­ual bac­te­rial bio­films and serve as a po­ten­tial source of per­sis­ tent in­fec­tion. This em­pha­sizes the key role of us­ing an­ti­bac­te­rial chem­i­cal agents as irrigants and plac­ing an ICM in an at­tempt to sup­ple­ment the ef­fects of me­chan­i­cal de­bride­ment.

Root Canal Irrigation
During CMP, the com­bi­na­tion of ir­ri­gat­ing so­lu­tions and chem­i­cal aux­il­ iary sub­stances im­proves ca­nal clean­ing and dis­in­fec­tion achieved by in­ stru­ments. Irrigation pro­motes the pro­gres­sive elim­i­na­tion of vi­tal or ne­crotic tis­sue rem­nants and den­tinal chips re­leased by the ac­tion of root ca­nal in­stru­ments. The use of an­ti­mi­cro­bial sub­stances dur­ing CMP im­proves bac­te­rial re­duc­tion, es­pe­cially in the main ca­nal lu­men, act­ing on plank­tonic bac­te­ ria and dis­rupt­ing bio­films. However, the an­ti­mi­cro­bial sub­stance may have lim­ited ac­tion on bio­films in noninstrumented ar­eas. Complex mul­ ti­spe­cies bio­films are more re­sis­tant to the an­ti­mi­cro­bial ef­fects of ir­ri­gat­ing and chem­i­cal aux­il­iary sub­stances than pre­vi­ously re­ported for mono­cul­ture bio­films or plank­tonic cells.
Chemical aux­il­iary sub­stances should have a broad spec­trum of an­ ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tion, dis­solve or­ganic soft tis­sue, in­ac­ti­vate bac­te­rial vir­ u­lence fac­tors, have low cy­to­tox­ic­ity, serve as lu­bri­cants for in­stru­ments, and re­move the smear layer with­out­ caus­ing ir­ri­ta­tion to the periradicu­ lar tis­sues. Different sub­stances have been pro­posed and used as chem­i­ cal aux­il­iary sub­stances dur­ing CMP, in­clud­ing so­dium hy­po­chlo­rite (NaOCl), chlor­hex­i­dine (CHX), and eth­yl­ene­di­amine­tet­ra­ace­tic acid (EDTA).
NaOCl is widely used be­cause of its pow­er­ful an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tiv­ity, tis­sue- and biofilm-dissolving abil­ity, low cost, and avail­abil­ity from many com­mer­cial sources. Clinical stud­ies have shown that ir­ri­ga­tion with NaOCl pro­motes sig­nif­i­cantly bet­ter dis­in­fec­tion of ca­nals than sa­line. However,
NaOCl is highly cy­to­toxic and may cause se­ri­ous con­se­quences when api­ cally ex­trud­ed.
CHX has also been used as an ef­fec­tive chem­i­cal aux­il­iary sub­stance, par­tic­u­larly due to its an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tion against Gram-negative and
Gram-positive oral bac­te­ria, its ad­sorp­tion to den­tal hard tis­sues with grad­ual and pro­longed re­lease at ther­a­peu­tic lev­els, and its lower cy­to­tox­ ic­ity. It is ­able to dif­fuse into the root ca­nal sys­tem and the den­tin tu­bu­lar struc­ture to ex­ert an­ti­mi­cro­bial ef­fects. An ap­proach for ir­ri­ga­tion uti­lizes
CHX gel and in­ter­me­di­ate flushes with in­ert so­lu­tions, such as dis­tilled wa­ter. Several clin­i­cal stud­ies have found no sig­nif­i­cant dif­fer­ence be­tween the an­ti­mi­cro­bial ef­fects of NaOCl and CHX.
EDTA is widely used in end­odon­tics for smear layer re­moval be­cause of its che­lat­ing ac­tion. It also has some an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tiv­ity, has an effect on LPS, and can pro­mote re­lease of growth fac­tors from den­tin. In or­der to op­ti­mize its ef­fects, EDTA can be ul­tra­son­i­cally ac­ti­vat­ed.
Although sev­eral stud­ies have dem­on­strated high lev­els of bac­te­rial load re­duc­tion af­ter CMP us­ing ei­ther NaOCl or CHX, com­plete elim­i­ na­tion of bac­te­ria and LPS has rarely been ob­served in teeth with pri­mary or per­sis­tent or sec­ond­ary in­fec­tions. Intracanal Medication
Although CMP pro­ce­dures can re­move a large pro­por­tion of intracanal bac­te­rial pop­u­la­tions, bac­te­ria may per­sist on un­pre­pared den­tinal walls and in isthmi, ram­i­fi­ca­tions, re­cesses, and den­tinal tu­bules. For this rea­ son, ap­pli­ca­tion of an ICM af­ter CMP for a given pe­riod of time be­tween ap­point­ments may be nec­es­sary to im­prove dis­in­fec­tion. The ef­fi­cacy of
ICM is de­pen­dent on the an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tiv­ity of the agent used, the time it re­mains in the ca­nal, its ap­pli­ca­tion to the en­tire ex­tent of the pre­pared ca­nal, and the sus­cep­ti­bil­ity of the spe­cies in­volved in the root ca­nal in­fec­ tion. Other in­di­ca­tions for ICM not re­lated to in­fec­tion are the pres­ence of teeth with open api­ces, in­suf­fi­cient time to con­clude treat­ment, and fa­tigue of the pa­tient or op­er­a­tor.
Whether an interappointment med­i­ca­tion is needed to im­prove dis­in­fec­ tion and thus en­hance treat­ment out­come is one of the most con­tro­ver­sial is­sues in end­odon­tics. Histobacteriologic and mo­lec­u­lar micro­biology stud­ ies showed that a two-visit pro­to­col us­ing ICM with cal­cium hy­drox­ide paste re­sulted in im­proved mi­cro­bi­o­log­i­cal sta­tus of the root ca­nal sys­tem com­pared with a sin­gle-visit pro­to­col. On the other hand, many out­come stud­ies showed no sig­nif­i­cant dif­fer­ences in the suc­cess rate of teeth treated in one or two vis­its. The prob­lem with these pro­spec­tive stud­ies is that they all­ had a small sam­ple size and were sta­tis­ti­cally un­der­pow­ered. There is a need for lon­gi­tu­di­nal out­come stud­ies with large sam­ple sizes to eval­u­ate the dif­fer­ences, if any, be­tween treat­ments con­cluded in one or two ap­ point­ments.

Several an­ti­mi­cro­bial sub­stances have been used as ICMs, among them cal­cium hy­drox­ide as­so­ci­ated with in­ert (e.g., sa­line, dis­tilled wa­ter) or bi­o­log­i­cally ac­tive (CHX, cam­phor­ated parachlorophenol) vehicles or CHX gel alone.
Calcium hy­drox­ide [Ca(OH)2] is widely used as an ICM be­cause of its an­ti­bac­te­rial ac­tion and abil­ity to in­duce min­er­al­ized tis­sue for­ma­tion and in­ac­ti­vate LPS. In ad­di­tion, cal­cium hy­drox­ide paste tem­po­rar­ily fills the ca­nal and acts as a phys­i­cal bar­rier, help­ing pre­vent both re­in­fec­tion and nu­tri­ent sup­ply to re­sid­ual bac­te­ria. The an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tiv­ity of
Ca(OH)2 de­rives from its high pH (around 12), through di­rect ac­tion of the hy­droxyl ions on the bac­te­ria. However, the slow dif­fu­sion of these ions through den­tin and soft tis­sues, or even the buff­er­ing ca­pac­ity of den­tin, may in­ter­fere with the an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tion of Ca(OH)2 mixed with in­ert car­ri­ers. In ad­di­tion, while most end­odon­tic bac­te­ria are killed in high-pH con­di­tions, E. faecalis can sur­vive and pos­si­bly cause per­sis­ tent in­fec­tions. Therefore, the as­so­ci­a­tion of Ca(OH)2 with CHX or other bi­o­log­i­cally ac­tive car­ri­ers has been rec­om­mended to im­prove the an­ti­mi­ cro­bial ef­fects.
CHX can also be used as an ICM, es­pe­cially in the gel form. It is ac­ tive against a wide range of oral bac­te­ria and yeasts. It also ex­hib­its sub­ stantivity, i.e., CHX binds to hy­droxy­ap­a­tite of enamel or den­tin and to acidic an­ionic groups of gly­co­pro­teins, be­ing slowly re­leased as its con­ cen­tra­tion in the me­dium de­creases, thus al­low­ing pro­longed an­ti­mi­cro­ bial ef­fects. CHX alone does not act as a phys­i­cal bar­rier and does not ex­hibit radiopacity.

Root Canal Obturation and Coronal Restoration
Root ca­nal fill­ing is the last phase of root ca­nal ther­apy and should be per­ formed un­der ad­e­quate clin­i­cal con­di­tions. The ob­tu­ra­tion must com­ pletely fill the pre­pared ca­nal, with­out­ pro­mot­ing phys­i­cal or chem­i­cal in­jury to the periradicular tis­sues, in or­der to per­mit un­event­ful periradic­ ular tis­sue heal­ing. Traditionally, root ca­nal ob­tu­ra­tion com­prises com­ plete fill­ing of the root ca­nal space by ma­te­ri­als such as gut­ta-percha and seal­ers, which should ex­hibit ap­pro­pri­ate phys­i­cal and bi­o­log­i­cal prop­er­ ties. Root ca­nal fill­ing is in­tended to per­pet­u­ate the dis­in­fec­tion state ob­ tained by CMP and ICM, elim­i­nat­ing voids for re­growth of re­sid­ual bac­te­ria and iso­lat­ing them from the periradicular tis­sues. A good fill­ing should also pre­vent tis­sue flu­ids from seep­ing into the ca­nal, which might provide re­sid­ual bac­te­ria with nu­tri­ents and allow re­col­o­ni­za­tion by oral bac­te­ria. Coronal res­to­ra­tion of an end­odon­ti­cally treated tooth aims to iso­late the root ca­nal fill­ing from the oral en­vi­ron­ment, pre­vent­ing leak­ age of sa­liva into the ca­nal, which might lead to re­in­fec­tion and com­pro­ mise treat­ment out­come. In ad­di­tion, the cor­o­nal res­to­ra­tion pro­tects the re­main­ing tooth struc­ture and helps pre­vent frac­ture.
Success of the end­odon­tic treat­ment de­pends on max­i­mal re­duc­tion of bac­te­ria and their prod­ucts to lev­els com­pat­i­ble with periradicular tis­sue heal­ing. In ad­di­tion, mea­sures should be taken to avoid con­tam­ i­na­tion of the ca­nals be­fore, dur­ing, and af­ter end­odon­tic ther­apy, al­ low­ing the re­pair of the periradicular tis­sues and re­es­tab­lish­ing tooth func­tion.

CONCLUDING REMARKS AND PERSPECTIVES
The de­vel­op­ment of mo­lec­u­lar tech­niques for mi­cro­bial iden­ti­fi­ca­tion fa­ vored the di­ag­no­sis and ex­panded the knowl­edge of the com­po­si­tion of the end­odon­tic microbiota. A myr­iad of spe­cies has been iden­ti­fied in as­ so­ci­a­tion with the dif­fer­ent types of end­odon­tic in­fec­tions, and the chal­ lenge lies in un­rav­el­ing the im­por­tance and the clin­i­cal im­pli­ca­tions of mi­cro­bi­o­log­i­cal find­ings at dif­fer­ent sites. In any case, com­mu­ni­ty-based in­ter­ac­tions seem to be the key el­e­ment for the growth and sur­vival of mi­cro­or­gan­isms in their hab­i­tat and the con­se­quent abil­ity to pro­duce dis­ ease. Alternatively, it is also pos­si­ble that more re­sil­ient path­o­gens adapted to per­sist in the end­odon­tic en­vi­ron­ment could mod­u­late the biofilm com­ po­si­tion and vir­u­lence. This may be the case for E. faecalis in teeth with post­treat­ment dis­ease. Moreover, the com­plex­ity of the root ca­nal en­vi­ ron­men­tal con­di­tions fa­vors the es­tab­lish­ment of a highly di­ver­si­fied mi­ cro­bial com­mu­nity or sur­vival of spe­cific path­o­gens that var­ies ac­cord­ing to their an­a­tom­i­cal lo­ca­tion and to the time that they gain en­try into the root ca­nal. Bacteria lo­cated in ar­eas dis­tant from the main ca­nal rep­re­sent a chal­lenge for proper dis­in­fec­tion dur­ing treat­ment. Bacteria that sur­vived the ef­fects of treat­ment may po­ten­tially re­pop­u­late the pulp space, de­lay­ing or im­ped­ing the periradicular tis­sue re­par­a­tive pro­cess. In re­gen­er­a­tive end­ odon­tics, re­sid­ual bac­te­ria can de­stroy the re­gen­er­at­ing tis­sues.
Resistance to an­ti­bi­ot­ics has been dem­on­strated for sev­eral bac­te­rial iso­ lates of end­odon­tic in­fec­tions. Therefore, mo­lec­u­lar iden­ti­fi­ca­tion meth­ods should in­clude screen­ing for an­ti­bi­otic re­sis­tance genes, which will al­low for a faster and more ap­pro­pri­ate se­lec­tion of an­ti­bi­ot­ics to treat po­ten­tially se­ri­ ous forms of end­odon­tic in­fec­tions, es­pe­cially the acute api­cal ab­scess.

K E Y  P O I N T S

Endodontic in­fec­tions are usu­ally a se­quela to car­ies and are clas­si­fied as intraradicular or extraradicular in­fec­tion. The lat­ter is usu­ally as­so­ci­ated with symp­toms. The for­mer can be sub­di­vided into pri­mary (the main cause of pri­mary api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis), sec­ond­ary (re­sult­ing from con­tam­i­na­tion of the ca­nal af­ter pro­fes­sional in­ter­ven­tion), or per­sis­tent (the main cause of post­treat­ment api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis). Apical peri­odon­ti­tis is an in­fec­tious dis­ease caused by bac­te­rial in­fec­tion of the root ca­nal sys­tem. The com­po­si­tion of the end­odon­tic microbiota var­ies sig­nif­i­cantly from in­di­vid­ual to in­di­vid­ual, sug­gest­ing that api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis has a het­ero­ge­neous eti­ol­o­gy.

Biofilms are found in the api­cal root ca­nal sys­tem in the large ma­jor­ity of teeth with pri­mary or post­treat­ment api­cal peri­odon­ti­ tis. Biofilms can prop­a­gate to ar­eas of the root ca­nal sys­tem dis­tant from the main ca­nal, in­clud­ing re­cesses, isthmi, and ram­i­fi­ca­tions.
In these ar­eas, bac­te­ria are dif­ fi­cult to reach and eliminate dur­ing end­odon­tic treat­ment. Extraradicular bio­films are a rare oc­cur­ rence. When pres­ent, they are usu­ally as­so­ci­ated with symp­toms.

Endodontic treat­ment es­sen­tially en­com­passes me­chan­i­cal and chem­i­cal pro­ce­dures to de­bride the root ca­nal and re­move bac­te­ria and their prod­ucts, de­bris, and po­ten­tial sub­strates for bac­te­rial re­growth. An ICM may be nec­es­sary to ob­tain max­i­mal bac­te­rial and en­do­toxin re­duc­tion of in­fected root ca­nals. Efficacy of

treat­ment pro­ce­dures de­pends on the abil­ity to reach bac­te­ria lo­cated in other ar­eas of the ca­nal sys­tem and the sus­cep­ti­bil­ity of the in­volved spe­cies, which may not be uni­form.

The cur­rent avail­able irrigants and intracanal med­i­ca­tions are not ef­fec­tive against all­ mi­cro­or­gan­isms found in the root ca­nal. The tra­di­tional non­spe­cific an­ti­mi­cro­bial agents ap­pear to be lim­ited in con­trol­ling in­fec­tion. In par­al­lel, a well-adapted de­fin­i­tive cor­o­nal res­to­ra­tion should be placed fol­low­ing root ca­nal treat­ment to pre­vent cor­o­nal microleakage, root ca­nal re­in­fec­tion, and root frac­ture.

Technological ad­vances of in­stru­men­ta­tion have yielded sig­nif­i­cant im­prove­ments in the abil­ity to shape root ca­nals, with fewer pro­ce­dural com­pli­ca­tions. The use of an­ti­mi­cro­bial agents and dif­fer­ent ir­ri­ga­tion sys­tems can pro­vide sev­eral ad­van­tages in the end­odon­tic out­come. Integration of new tech­nol­o­gies, to­gether with en­hanced tech­niques and ma­te­ri­als, may help daily clin­i­cal prac­tice. Furthermore, some tech­ni­cal pro­ce­dures, such as the in­crease in api­cal prep­a­ra­tion, a more ef­fec­tive sys­tem of ir­ri­ga­tion de­liv­ery, and ac­ti­va­tion of the irrigant, can pro­mote and make more pre­dict­able the re­duc­tion of intracanal bac­te­ria, es­pe­cially in com­plex an­a­tom­i­cal and noninstrumented ar­eas of the root ca­nal sys­tem.

Considering the role of bac­te­ria in the in­duc­tion and per­pet­u­a­tion of pulpal and periradicular le­sions, it is nec­es­sary to es­tab­lish guide­lines for op­ti­mum end­odon­tic treat­ment, aim­ing not only to ob­tain max­i­mum bac­ te­rial and vir­u­lence fac­tor re­duc­tion from the in­fected root ca­nals, but also to avoid con­tam­i­na­tion of the ca­nals be­fore, dur­ing, and af­ter end­ odon­tic ther­apy (see Fig. 5). Undoubtedly the cur­rent tech­nol­ogy for me­ chan­i­cal prep­a­ra­tion is not ef­fec­tive in de­brid­ing all­ ar­eas of the root ca­nal sys­tem. Likewise, chem­i­cal dis­in­fec­tion alone is in­ca­pa­ble of complete bac­ te­rial and biofilm eradication. Consequently, as­so­ci­a­tions of novel tech­ niques that al­low for bet­ter ir­ri­ga­tion of the pulp space with ef­fec­tive aux­il­iary chem­i­cal sub­stances are nec­es­sary in an at­tempt to sup­ple­ment me­chan­i­cal de­bride­ment. Currently avail­able intracanal med­i­ca­tions are not ef­fec­tive against all­ mi­cro­or­gan­isms found in the root ca­nal.
Despite the lim­i­ta­tions of treat­ment pro­ce­dures in com­pletely erad­i­ cat­ing the end­odon­tic in­fec­tion, the suc­cess rate of cur­rent an­ti­mi­cro­bial treat­ment is very high and pre­dict­able. This is be­cause an ad­e­quate treat­ ment gen­er­ally suc­ceeds in re­duc­ing and con­trol­ling in­fec­tion to the point where it does not com­pro­mise the periradicular tis­sue heal­ing. Therefore, end­odon­tic treat­ment is a very im­por­tant ap­proach to main­tain teeth and the health of the oral cav­ity. Even in cases in which api­cal peri­odon­ti­tis does not heal or emerges fol­low­ing the ini­tial end­odon­tic treat­ment, root ca­nals can still be re­treated or the tooth sub­jected to periradicular sur­ gery, with a rea­son­ably high chance of re­stor­ing healthy periradicular tis­ sue and re­ten­tion of the tooth.
