INTRODUCTION
Dental car­ies is unique among bacterially mediated in­fec­tious dis­eases be­ cause di­e­tary sug­ars, par­tic­u­larly su­crose, are key de­ter­mi­nants for the man­i­fes­ta­tion of this dis­ease. The in­ter­ac­tions of mi­cro­or­gan­isms with sug­ars on the tooth sur­face result in the for­ma­tion of a biofilm known as den­tal plaque, the first step of the path­o­log­i­cal pro­cess. This biofilm fa­cil­ i­tates the de­vel­op­ment of an acid mi­lieu into which the min­er­al­ized tis­sue of the tooth dis­solves, lead­ing to the on­set of car­i­ous le­sions. Thus, den­tal car­ies is a biofilm- and di­et-dependent dis­ease.

Dental car­ies con­tin­ues to plague many in so­ci­ety. The eco­nom­i­cally de­prived are most of­ten af­flicted, through a high prev­a­lence of the dis­ease in fam­i­lies, mal­nu­tri­tion, and poor di­e­tary hab­its, com­bined with lack of oral health aware­ness and lim­ited ac­cess to pre­ven­tive mea­sures such as fluo­ride. A par­tic­u­larly vir­u­lent type of den­tal car­ies, early child­hood car­ ies (ECC), arises from an al­most con­tin­u­ous ex­po­sure to sug­ars in a nurs­ ing bot­tle or sim­i­lar con­tainer, re­sult­ing in an over­whelm­ing col­o­ni­za­tion of the tooth sur­faces by mutans group strep­to­cocci (par­tic­u­larly Strepto­ coccus mutans) and other acidogenic bac­te­ria. This dis­ease is an ex­cel­lent ex­am­ple of a ma­lev­o­lent in­ter­ac­tion of sug­ars from diet and car­i­o­genic bac­te­ria.

Saliva plays a crit­i­cal role in mod­u­lat­ing the car­ies pro­cess. It har­bors myr­iad an­ti­bac­te­rial sub­stances, fa­cil­i­tates clear­ance of res­i­dues from the mouth, and of course has sig­nif­i­cant buff­er­ing and remineralizing ca­pac­ity.
The lat­ter can be boosted by fluo­ride to re­pair early car­i­ous le­sions. Indi­ viduals who lack sa­liva through ra­di­a­tion for can­cer treat­ment, Sjögren’s syn­drome, or use of hyposalivatory pre­scrip­tion drugs are all­ at el­e­vated risk to de­velop dev­as­tat­ing lev­els of car­i­ous le­sions.

Overall, den­tal car­ies is con­trol­la­ble. Available ev­i­dence shows very clearly that the dis­ease runs in fam­i­lies, al­though the ev­i­dence that there is a ge­netic com­po­nent is sparse. Children of par­ents (par­tic­u­larly the mother) who dis­play a high num­ber of un­treated car­i­ous le­sions pres­ent a microbiota col­o­nized by el­e­vated pop­u­la­tions of car­i­o­genic or­gan­isms caused by a sug­ar-rich diet. Thus, den­tal car­ies should be treated as a fam­ ily dis­ease, in­volv­ing pre­ven­tive measures for care­giv­ers as well as sib­lings.

TOOTH STRUCTURE AND DEVELOPMENT
The tooth con­sists of a supragingival struc­ture or crown above the gumline (gin­gi­val mar­gin) and a root struc­ture be­low the gum (Fig. 1). The white, vis­i­ble part of the crown is enamel, which cov­ers the den­tin and the pulp.
Enamel is one of the hard­est bi­o­logic tis­sues known. The cells re­spon­si­ble for enamel for­ma­tion, the am­e­lo­blasts, os­cil­late in func­tion dur­ing amelo­ genesis; they can as­sume a se­cre­tive, min­er­al­iz­ing, or re­sorp­tive mor­phol­ogy.
Enamel is a min­er­al­ized tis­sue formed be­fore tooth erup­tion, by two pro­ cess­es: an ini­tial se­cre­tion of a pro­tein ma­trix, which is con­com­i­tantly fol­ lowed by min­er­al­i­za­tion. The main pro­teins in the se­cre­tion phase are amelogenin and enamelin. Enamelin is el­e­vated in as­par­tic and glu­tamic acid res­i­dues and is phos­phor­y­lated. Amelogenin, on the other hand, con­tains high lev­els of pro­line and his­ti­dine as well as glu­ta­mate and leu­cine. As enamel de­vel­ops, prisms of min­er­als are formed us­ing the pro­tein ma­trix as guid­ance; at the end of the pro­cess, most of the amelogenin con­tent is lost, whereas enamelin re­mains bound in the crys­tal struc­ture. The con­cen­ tra­tion of pro­teins in ma­ture enamel is only of about 1%, with 95% of it be­ing com­posed of the min­eral hy­droxy­ap­a­tite.
The min­eral of enamel, i.e., hy­droxy­ap­a­tite, is the first el­e­ment fac­ing the ac­tiv­ity of the car­i­o­genic biofilm, as de­scribed in detail in this chap­ter.
Although the com­po­si­tion of enamel is mainly hy­droxy­ap­a­tite, the min­ eral in enamel can be re­garded as a bi­o­log­i­cal ap­a­tite, in which trace el­e­ ments can be in­cor­po­rated dur­ing the min­er­al­i­za­tion pro­cess. Lead (Pb), for ex­am­ple, found in de­cid­u­ous and per­ma­nent enamel of chil­dren, is a per­ma­nent marker of en­vi­ron­men­tal pol­lu­tion oc­cur­ring dur­ing preg­ nancy or child­hood. Also, fluo­ride con­cen­tra­tion is el­e­vated in enamel formed un­der ex­po­sure to wa­ter fluo­ri­da­tion com­mu­nity pro­grams or nat­ u­rally fluo­ri­dated wa­ter. However, the im­por­tance of this in­creased con­ cen­tra­tion in form­ing stron­ger, ac­id-resistant crys­tals has been chal­lenged, and to­day it is be­lieved that the main an­ti­car­ies ef­fect of fluo­ride is not sys­temic (preeruptive), as de­tailed later. The min­er­al­i­za­tion of enamel still con­tin­ues af­ter tooth erup­tion in the oral cav­ity be­cause sa­li­va’s con­cen­ tra­tion of cal­cium and phos­phate makes it su­per­sat­u­rated with re­spect to hy­droxy­ap­a­tite. Saliva nor­mally pro­motes the deposition of ad­di­tional min­er­als in enamel, in­clud­ing those con­tain­ing fluo­ride.
Dentin, the ma­te­rial di­rectly un­der the enamel (Fig. 1) and sup­port­ing it, also has a high min­eral con­tent. Dentin is much more closely re­lated to bone and also orig­i­na­tes from me­so­der­mal tis­sue. SITES OF CARIOUS LESIONS

Coronal Caries
Dental car­ies oc­cur­ring on the re­gion of the tooth that is above the gin­gi­ val (gum) mar­gin is termed supragingival car­ies, or cor­o­nal car­ies. The smooth sur­faces of the tooth are ac­ces­si­ble to sal­i­vary flow, are eas­ier to clean, and are sub­jected to me­chan­i­cal abra­sive forces from the tongue and lips. Therefore, bac­te­ria need spe­cial­ized col­o­ni­za­tion mech­a­nisms to at­tach and ac­cu­mu­late on these sur­faces. In con­trast, bac­te­ria can be­come em­bed­ded in the pits and fis­sures of the tooth crown with­out­ spe­cial­ized ad­her­ence mech­a­nisms. Caries le­sions may oc­cur rap­idly in an erupt­ing tooth, pro­tected from the clean­ing abra­sive forces of mas­ti­ca­tion. If de­ min­er­al­iza­tion oc­curs at this site, it fol­lows the di­rec­tion of the enamel rods (the ba­sic unit of enamel struc­ture) and runs per­pen­dic­u­larly from the tooth sur­face to the den­tin. This is termed pit and fis­sure car­ies, and the ad­vanc­ing front is a zone of ac­id-demineralized den­tin that does not con­tain bac­te­ria. Caries that is left un­treated can prog­ress right through the den­tin and into the pulp (Fig. 1). Root Surface Caries
Root sur­face car­ies oc­curs on sur­faces of the teeth that are nor­mally cov­ ered by the gums, when they be­come ex­posed to the oral cav­ity. The dis­ ease is a sig­nif­i­cant oral health con­cern in the el­derly pop­u­la­tion and is likely to be­come more prob­lem­atic in the fu­ture, be­cause peo­ple are liv­ing lon­ger and re­tain­ing more of their nat­u­ral teeth, but with re­ced­ing gingi­ vae. The ex­posed ce­men­tum is very thin and softer and more ac­id-soluble than enamel. As ce­men­tum is eas­ily de­min­er­al­ized or worn, root den­tin is ex­posed, in which car­ies lesions progress faster than in enam­el; this even­ tu­ally leads to ex­po­sure of the den­tal pulp. The pro­gres­sion of root sur­ face car­ies is par­al­leled by dis­col­or­ation and changes in tex­ture. There is tran­si­tion from a hard to a softer or more leath­ery sur­face and then to a soft ma­te­rial as the pulp is ex­posed, weak­en­ing the tooth struc­ture and lead­ing to the pos­si­bil­ity of frac­tures.

DIET AND DENTAL CARIES

Cariogenic Effect of the Diet
The com­po­si­tion of diet is one of the cru­cial el­e­ments in the eti­ol­ogy and path­o­gen­e­sis of den­tal car­ies. Among di­e­tary sug­ars, su­crose is the most car­i­o­genic be­cause it serves as sub­strate for the pro­duc­tion of both ex­tra­ cel­lu­lar po­ly­sac­cha­rides and ac­ids, which play key roles in the ini­ti­a­tion and de­vel­op­ment of car­i­o­genic bio­films on the tooth sur­face (Fig. 2). The ex­tra­cel­lu­lar po­ly­sac­cha­rides, rich in glu­cans, pro­vide bac­te­rial bind­ing sites that fa­cil­i­tate the col­o­ni­za­tion of car­i­o­genic mi­cro­or­gan­isms while form­ing a ma­trix scaffold that pro­vi­des me­chan­i­cal sta­bil­ity, co­he­sive­ness, and pro­tec­tion against harm­ful com­pounds, such as an­ti­mi­cro­bial agents.
The po­ly­sac­cha­ride ma­trix also con­trols the dif­fu­sion of fer­ment­able sub­ strates through the biofilm, al­low­ing their uti­li­za­tion by bac­te­ria in the in­ner biofilm lay­ers and lim­it­ing the ac­tion of sa­liva to clear and buffer the ac­ids pro­duced within. In ad­di­tion, sim­i­lar to other sim­ple sug­ars, su­ crose is also me­tab­o­lized into ac­ids by acidogenic mi­cro­or­gan­isms re­sid­ing within the biofilm.
The re­la­tion­ship be­tween the prev­a­lence and in­ci­dence of den­tal car­ ies and sugar ex­po­sure has been well rec­og­nized in ep­i­de­mi­o­log­i­cal, clin­ i­cal, and well-controlled an­i­mal stud­ies. Furthermore, both the pop­u­la­tions of car­i­o­genic mi­cro­or­gan­isms and the on­set and se­ver­ity of den­tal car­ies are influ­enced by the fre­quency of in­ges­tion of sug­ars. The like­li­hood of car­i­o­genic at­tack on the enamel in­creases each time sugar is in­gested. In ad­di­tion, sugar can en­hance the vir­u­lence of the car­i­o­genic biofilm by pro­ mot­ing the for­ma­tion of the dif­fu­sion-modulating ma­trix and by se­lect­ing for a highly acidogenic-aciduric microbiota (see Chapter 12). Although fre­ quency of sugar con­sump­tion is cer­tainly im­por­tant, its ef­fects may be mod­i­fied by the in­ter­val be­tween in­take events, as the fun­da­men­tal prob­ lem is the du­ra­tion for which sug­ars are pres­ent in the mouth. Clearly the phys­i­cal prop­er­ties of the food, as well as the clear­ing ac­tion of sa­liva, have a ma­jor in­flu­ence on re­ten­tion of sug­ars in the mouth.

The ef­fects of “per­pet­u­al” sugar in the mouth is starkly il­lus­trated in early child­hood car­ies, where the child’s den­ti­tion is dev­as­tated as a re­sult of an in­fant or tod­dler be­ing al­lowed to have a nurs­ing bot­tle con­tain­ing sug­ars ad li­bi­tum. It is note­wor­thy that the teeth dis­play an over­whelm­ing col­o­ni­za­tion by car­i­o­genic bac­te­ria such as S. mutans and the es­tab­lish­ ment of a less di­verse yet highly acidogenic microbiota, which may in­clude
Bifidobacterium spe­cies and Scardovia wiggsiae. Among the sub­stances fre­quently placed in the nurs­ing bot­tle are soft drinks, which have el­e­ vated amounts of sug­ars. Honey too is a pop­u­lar choice be­cause it is “nat­ u­ral”; how­ever, it is highly car­i­o­genic, as shown in ex­per­i­men­tal in vi­vo stud­ies.
The role of starch in the eti­ol­ogy of den­tal car­ies is something of an enigma. There are nu­mer­ous types of starch with dis­tinct struc­tures and phys­i­cal prop­er­ties. During World War II, when sugar was ra­tioned or un­ avail­able and starches were a prime source of cal­o­ries, the prev­a­lence and in­ci­dence of enamel den­tal car­ies de­creased, only to in­crease shortly af­ter su­crose and other fer­ment­able car­bo­hy­drates be­came more avail­able.
Overall, it ap­pears that starches on their own con­trib­ute lit­tle to the car­ i­o­genic pro­cess, even though they can greatly in­flu­ence the phys­i­cal re­ten­ tion of food. However, starches can en­hance the cariogenicity of su­crose through boost­ing the vir­u­lence of car­i­o­genic bac­te­ria. Partially di­gested starch (by am­y­lase in sa­liva) boosts pro­duc­tion of in­sol­u­ble po­ly­sac­cha­ rides and ac­ids by S. mutans that are the ma­jor vir­u­lence prop­er­ties of the car­i­o­genic biofilm. In vi­tro, in si­tu, and an­i­mal-based in­ves­ti­ga­tions sug­ gest strongly that com­bi­na­tions of starch and sugar are more car­i­o­genic than su­crose alone.

Anticariogenic Properties of Food
The car­i­o­genic po­ten­tial of any par­tic­u­lar food can­not be di­rectly re­lated to the sugar con­cen­tra­tion. The amount of pro­tein and its com­po­si­tion, lipid con­cen­tra­tion, amounts of phos­phate and cal­cium, and the pres­ence of food pre­ser­va­tives such as so­dium ben­zo­ate can all­ have ef­fects on the cariogenicity of food. For ex­am­ple, lec­tins are pro­teins found in foods such as soy­bean, wheat germ, to­ma­toes, and car­rots that re­act with spe­ cific sug­ars linked to a pro­tein core. By bind­ing to con­stit­u­ents in the ac­ quired enamel pel­li­cle, lec­tins can af­fect bac­te­rial ad­he­sion to teeth and fur­ther col­o­ni­za­tion by mi­cro­or­gan­isms. Clearly, more at­ten­tion has been fo­cused on di­ets with foods that pro­mote for­ma­tion of car­i­ous le­sions than on car­ies-protective prop­er­ties. Nevertheless, sev­eral foods have been ex­am­ined for anticariogenic effect; cheese is fore­most among these. Nu­ merous in si­tu stud­ies, small-scale hu­man tri­als, and an­i­mal-based re­search un­der the most in­tense car­i­o­genic chal­lenges have all­ re­vealed the cario­ static po­ten­tial of in­gest­ing cheese as in­fre­quently as once or twice per day.
There are sev­eral pos­si­ble mech­a­nisms of ac­tion, in­clud­ing an­ti­bac­te­rial ef­fects of fatty ac­ids, ca­sein pep­tides, or nisin as well as in­creased cal­cium and phos­phate lev­els and in­tense stim­u­la­tion of sal­i­vary flow. Also, sev­ eral vi­ta­mins and trace el­e­ments have been ex­plored for their cariostatic or car­ies-enhancing ef­fects sys­tem­i­cally and top­i­cally. Evidence to sup­port top­i­cal ap­pli­ca­tion of vi­ta­mins to pre­vent le­sion for­ma­tion or im­pact the mi­cro­bial pop­u­la­tions of the mouth is lack­ing. On the other hand, el­e­ vated lev­els of lead and se­le­nium in drink­ing wa­ter are as­so­ci­ated with in­creased sus­cep­ti­bil­ity to den­tal car­ies, likely due to the for­ma­tion of more eas­ily sol­u­bi­lized enamel. In con­trast, both cop­per and iron in wa­ter or ap­plied top­i­cally may re­duce car­ies de­vel­op­ment, again likely through ef­fects on enamel sol­u­bil­i­ty.
The use of al­ter­na­tive sweet­en­ers may also change the cariogenicity of foods. In gen­eral, sweet­en­ers can be clas­si­fied as non­nu­tri­tive (e.g., sac­cha­rin, as­par­tame, and sucralose) and nu­tri­tive (such as the sugar al­ co­hols sor­bi­tol and xy­li­tol). Most of the sugar sub­sti­tutes are noncariogenic be­cause they are not acidogenic or are poorly me­tab­o­lized by bac­te­ria, re­ sult­ing in re­duced amounts of ac­ids in den­tal plaque. Furthermore, these agents do not en­hance car­i­o­genic bac­te­rial col­o­ni­za­tion on den­tal sur­faces and are not sub­strates for ex­tra­cel­lu­lar po­ly­sac­cha­ride pro­duc­tion. CARIOGENIC DENTAL BIOFILM FORMATION
The com­po­si­tion of the diet di­rectly in­flu­ences the ca­pac­ity of path­o­gens to col­o­nize the tooth sur­face and de­velop car­i­o­genic bio­films. Teeth are cov­ered by an ac­quired enamel pel­li­cle within sec­onds fol­low­ing a thor­ ough clean­ing. The pel­li­cle con­tains a va­ri­ety of host mol­e­cules de­rived from sa­liva, such as pro­line-rich pro­teins, statherin, his­ti­dine-rich pro­teins, mu­cins, am­y­lase (in ac­tive form), ly­so­zyme, lactoperoxidase, and se­cre­tory
IgA (S-IgA). The pres­ence of mammal-derived con­stit­u­ents pro­vi­des se­lec­ tive bind­ing sites for oral mi­cro­or­gan­isms. Many bacterially de­rived con­ stit­u­ents are also pres­ent in the ac­quired pel­li­cle, in­clud­ing the exo­en­zymes glucosyltransferase (Gtf) and fructosyltransferase (Ftf), along with lipotei­ choic ac­id.
In the ab­sence of di­e­tary sug­ars, mi­cro­or­gan­isms col­o­nize the tooth sur­face, mostly through a com­bi­na­tion of phys­i­cal and adhesin-pellicle re­ cep­tor mech­a­nisms (see Chapter 5). However, su­crose ex­po­sure has a pro­ found in­flu­ence on bac­te­rial col­o­ni­za­tion by boost­ing the ca­pac­ity of mi­cro­or­gan­ism to bind the pel­li­cle. Following in­ges­tion of su­crose, Gtf exo­en­zymes in the pel­li­cle can pro­duce glu­can po­ly­mer (Fig. 3) on the sur­ face of the tooth, thereby pro­vid­ing a novel range of ad­di­tional bind­ing sites for bac­te­ria, which is the first step in the for­ma­tion of car­i­o­genic plaque biofilm. If sug­ars con­tinue to be in­gested, the pop­u­la­tion of mi­cro­ or­gan­isms in­creases dra­mat­i­cally on the tooth sur­face and a biofilm is formed with a glu­can-rich ma­trix (see Chapter 12).
The ma­trix is a hall­mark of bio­films and is con­sid­ered an es­sen­tial vir­u­ lence prop­erty in the path­o­gen­e­sis of den­tal car­ies. The pres­ence of glu­can fa­cil­i­tates the ad­he­sion and co­he­sion of di­verse spe­cies of mi­cro­or­gan­ isms while at the same time en­mesh­ing them in a po­ly­meric ma­trix that contributes to the bulk and dif­fu­sion-limiting prop­er­ties of plaque. When they are ex­posed to sug­ars, the met­a­bolic ac­tiv­ity of acidogenic or­gan­isms em­bed­ded in the ma­trix fa­cil­i­tates the cre­a­tion of acidic mi­cro­en­vi­ron­ments within the biofilm (Fig. 4). The low-pH niches pro­mote the growth and dom­i­nance of aciduric and acidogenic microbiota, whereas other spe­cies

PHYSICOCHEMICAL PHENOMENA IN DENTAL BIOFILM
Carious le­sions de­velop slowly as a re­sult of the ac­tion of the car­i­o­genic biofilm. The del­e­te­ri­ous ef­fect of the biofilm on teeth is due to the sol­u­bil­ity of tooth min­er­als in ac­ids. The end prod­ucts of bac­te­rial me­tab­o­lism, most of which are or­ganic ac­ids (such as lac­tic acid), are ca­pa­ble of cre­at­ing a de­min­er­al­iz­ing en­vi­ron­ment in which enamel or den­tin loses min­er­als.
The main min­eral com­po­nent of enamel and den­tine is hy­droxy­ap­a­tite, a min­eral com­posed of cal­cium, phos­phate, and hy­droxyl with a gen­eral for­mula of Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2, and substituted with ad­di­tional com­po­nents ac­quired dur­ing the min­er­al­i­za­tion pro­cess (e.g., car­bon­ate, fluo­ride) or even af­ter teeth are ex­posed to the oral cav­ity (e.g., fluo­ride). Hydroxyapa­ tite is highly in­sol­u­ble, and its sol­u­bil­ity prod­uct is rep­re­sented by a con­ stant (Ksp = sol­u­bil­ity prod­uct con­stant) of about 10–117 M18. This means that when a sat­u­rated so­lu­tion con­tain­ing dis­solved hy­droxy­ap­a­tite is an­a­ lyzed in terms of its ionic con­cen­tra­tion, the prod­uct of the ac­tiv­ity of the ions Ca2+, PO4
3–, and OH– is about 10–117 M18. Therefore, a very small amount of cal­cium and phos­phate in so­lu­tion is ­able to main­tain sat­u­ra­tion with re­spect to the solid min­eral, hy­droxy­ap­a­tite. However, given that phos­phate and hy­droxyl, two of the com­po­nent ions of hy­droxy­ap­a­tite, ac­ cept hy­dro­gen ions (H+) pres­ent in the so­lu­tion, the prod­uct of the ac­tiv­ity of these ions in so­lu­tion can change dras­ti­cally when the pH val­ues drop.
Saliva is su­per­sat­u­rated with re­spect to tooth min­er­als. Saliva con­tains cal­cium and phos­phate and is se­creted at a neu­tral pH into the oral cav­ity.
Therefore, teeth never dis­solve in sa­liva, but rather tend to gain min­er­als

Therefore, dur­ing a car­i­o­genic chal­lenge, the biofilm pH re­mains for lon­ger pe­ri­ods of time be­low the crit­i­cal val­ues for den­tin dis­so­lu­tion than for enamel de­min­er­al­iza­tion. As a re­sult, den­tal car­ies pro­ gresses faster in den­tin than in enam­el.

CARIES PREVENTION USING FLUORIDE
Among the many strat­e­gies that can be used to con­trol car­ies, fluo­ride has been by far the most suc­cess­ful. Significant car­ies re­duc­tions oc­curred within a short time frame in pop­u­la­tions ex­posed to fluo­ride from com­ mu­nity sources (mainly fluo­ri­dated wa­ter) or from den­ti­frices con­tain­ing fluo­ride. Hence, the ep­i­de­mi­ol­ogy of car­ies changed dra­mat­i­cally af­ter the in­tro­duc­tion of fluo­ride as an an­ti­car­ies agent in many parts of the world.
Fluoride even at very low con­cen­tra­tions (i.e., be­low 0.02 ppm) is ­able to in­ter­fere phys­i­co­chem­i­cally with the de­min­er­al­iza­tion and remineraliza­ tion pro­cesses. This phys­i­co­chem­i­cal ef­fect of fluo­ride de­rives from the ca­pac­ity of this ion to form fluorapatite [Ca10(PO4)6F2], a min­eral with 104 lower sol­u­bil­ity than hy­droxy­ap­a­tite (the Ksp of fluorapatite is 10–121 M18).
When the biofilm is acid­i­fied and un­der­sat­u­rated with re­spect to hy­droxy­ ap­a­tite and fluo­ride is pres­ent (even at low con­cen­tra­tions), fluorapatite forms, re­duc­ing the over­all loss of cal­cium and phos­phate from the tooth.
Therefore, when fluo­ride is avail­able in the biofilm fluid, net de­min­er­al­ iza­tion only oc­curs af­ter a greater pH drop, be­cause at the same time that hy­droxy­ap­a­tite is dis­solv­ing (be­low pH 5.5), fluorapatite is be­ing formed con­com­i­tantly. It is es­ti­mated that fluorapatite forms un­til the pH is re­ duced be­low 4.5. Thus, the crit­i­cal pH for net min­eral loss from enamel is one unit lower in the pres­ence of fluo­ride. However, the con­com­i­tant in­ cor­po­ra­tion of fluo­ride as fluorapatite in the tooth min­eral as a re­sult of this ef­fect does not sig­nif­i­cantly af­fect the sol­u­bil­ity of the min­eral, be­cause only 5 to 10% of hy­droxy­ap­a­tite crys­tals are substituted by fluorapatite.
In this con­text, the cariostatic ef­fect of fluo­ride re­lies pri­mar­ily on its phys­ i­co­chem­i­cal prop­erty of re­duc­ing the net rate of min­eral dis­so­lu­tion and en­hanc­ing the rate of min­eral for­ma­tion.

The an­ti­car­ies ef­fec­tive­ness of all­ of the above-mentioned meth­ods of fluo­ride de­liv­ery has been clearly dem­on­strated in clin­i­cal tri­als as well as in sys­tem­atic re­views of the lit­er­a­ture, and these meth­ods are cur­rently the main­stay of car­ies pre­ven­tion. Despite great strides since the dis­cov­ery of the an­ti­car­ies ef­fect of fluo­ride, den­tal car­ies con­tin­ues to be a pub­lic health prob­lem. Although ef­fec­tive in re­duc­ing tooth enamel de­min­er­al­ iza­tion and en­hanc­ing remineralization, fluo­ride has lim­ited ef­fects against sugar me­tab­o­lism and biofilm for­ma­tion at low con­cen­tra­tions typ­i­cally found in the mouth through­out­ the day. Thus, new an­ti­car­ies ap­proaches should pro­mote erad­i­ca­tion of bio­films lo­cally while pre­vent­ing acid dis­ so­lu­tion of tooth enamel. Alternative ther­a­peu­tic strat­e­gies with antibio­ film prop­er­ties have been de­vel­oped in re­cent years, in­clud­ing spe­cies-specific an­ti­mi­cro­bial pep­tides tar­get­ing car­i­o­genic path­o­gens, mod­u­la­tion of the biofilm pH via ar­gi­nine me­tab­o­lism, phys­i­cal dis­rup­tion by high-velocity wa­ter microspray, and syn­er­gis­tic com­bi­na­tions of bac­te­rial kill­ing and deg­ra­da­tion of the exopolysaccharide ma­trix. Furthermore, novel tech­ nol­o­gies have emerged us­ing “stimulus-triggered re­lease or ac­ti­va­tion” of agents in re­sponse to acidic pH val­ues for en­hanced se­lec­tiv­ity and con­trolled drug de­liv­ery or po­ten­ti­a­tion in si­tu. Other ex­cit­ing antibio­ film-specific ap­proaches, in­clud­ing nanocoatings, sur­face mod­i­fi­ca­tions, and small mol­e­cules from in vitro and in sil­i­co screen­ing, are emerg­ing at a re­mark­able pace (see Chapter 21). Nevertheless, fur­ther investigation of the mech­a­nisms of ac­tion and tox­ic­ity of these pro­spec­tive agents, us­ing in vi­vo mod­els and val­i­da­tion of their ef­fi­cacy in pre­clin­i­cal studies, is re­quired prior to as­sess­ing clin­i­cal trans­lat­abil­i­ty.

Many in vi­tro biofilm mod­els are avail­able now to test dif­fer­ent as­ pects of the car­ies pro­cess. These in­clude the pro­duc­tion of po­ly­sac­cha­rides and ac­ids from su­crose, the ex­pres­sion of vir­u­lence genes and me­tab­o­lite pro­fil­ing, as well as changes in pH at dif­fer­ent depths and sites of the biofilm struc­ture, of­ten em­ploy­ing im­ag­ing tech­niques such as con­fo­cal mi­cros­copy com­bined with mo­lec­u­lar, spec­tro­scopic, and bio­ chem­i­cal an­a­ly­ses.
Several meth­ods of grow­ing bio­films on hy­droxy­ap­a­tite disks have been es­tab­lished in either batch cul­ture or con­tin­u­ous flow sys­tems, al­low­ ing de­tailed anal­y­sis of the mi­cro­bial com­po­si­tion and its transcriptome/ proteome/metabolome, three-dimensional ar­chi­tec­ture, and the pres­ence of acidic mi­cro­en­vi­ron­ments within the biofilm struc­ture. Furthermore, a va­ri­ety of mul­ti­spe­cies biofilm sys­tems have been de­vel­oped us­ing dif­fer­ent lab­o­ra­tory or clin­i­cal strains, or even us­ing fresh sa­liva as an in­oc­u­lum. Eco­ logical mixed-species biofilm mod­els have been also es­tab­lished to mimic the mi­cro­bi­o­log­i­cal and bio­chem­i­cal changes in­duced by su­crose. Additional mod­i­fi­ca­tions in­clude the use of enamel or den­tin blocks on which the bio­ films are formed, al­low­ing a more pre­cise quan­ti­ta­tive as­sess­ment of min­eral loss. Also, meth­ods have been de­vel­oped em­ploy­ing in­ter­mit­tent ex­po­sure to di­e­tary sug­ars sim­u­lat­ing the “feast or fam­ine” ep­i­sodes to which biofilm bac­te­ria are usu­ally ex­posed. Irrespective of the type of biofilm used or the min­eral sub­strate on which it is grown, biofilm mod­els should be val­ i­dated in terms of dose-response ef­fects us­ing clin­i­cally proven an­ti­mi­cro­ bi­als or an­ti­car­ies sub­stances, such as chlor­hex­i­dine and fluo­ride, un­der re­al­is­tic treat­ment reg­i­mens (e.g., brief-exposure top­i­cal ap­pli­ca­tions of the test agents).

Animal Models
A large va­ri­ety of an­i­mals have been used to ex­plore the eti­ol­ogy, path­o­gen­ e­sis, and means of pre­ven­tion of den­tal car­ies. The re­sults of in­ves­ti­ga­tions car­ried out­ in an­i­mals with val­i­dated pro­to­cols and con­trols may be trans­ lated to hu­mans with re­mark­able pre­ci­sion. Over the years, dogs, mon­keys, ger­bils, fer­rets, ham­sters, mice, and rats of var­i­ous strains have been used as mod­els. Rats are by far the most com­monly used an­i­mals; their in­clu­sion un­der pre­cisely de­fined con­di­tions per­mits the myr­iad as­pects of the path­o­ phys­i­ol­ogy of den­tal car­ies to be ex­plored with con­fi­dence. The in­flu­ence of spe­cific mi­cro­or­gan­isms, in­clud­ing their trans­mis­sion from one an­i­mal to an­other, the pre­cise role of par­tic­u­lar con­stit­u­ents of the diet, ef­fects of sa­ liva, and com­po­si­tion of plaque bio­films, may all­ be ex­plored by us­ing the rat model. The an­i­mals must be free of ex­tra­ne­ous in­fec­tions, such as sialoacre­ denitis vi­rus and Streptococcus rattus, which may af­fect car­ies sus­cep­ti­ bil­ity and ren­der in­ter­pre­ta­tion of data dif­fi­cult.
Many car­ies-promoting di­ets are avail­able; NIH Diet 2000, which con­ tains high amounts of su­crose, is the most com­monly used. Diets con­tain­ing smaller amounts of su­crose are oc­ca­sion­ally used; how­ever, they con­tain el­ e­vated con­cen­tra­tions of ad­her­ent starch, which af­fects car­ies out­come and data in­ter­pre­ta­tion. A com­mon er­ror that oc­curs when an­i­mals are in­cluded in in­ves­ti­ga­tions of car­ies is fail­ure to de­ter­mine whether the an­i­mals’ eat­ing pat­tern is af­fected by the test agent. This prob­lem arises most fre­quently when the test agent is added to the diet. Reduction in eat­ing fre­quency can lead to a dra­matic de­cline in the in­ci­dence of car­i­ous le­sions that that may be at­trib­uted er­ro­ne­ously to the agent be­ing in­ves­ti­gated. This prob­lem may be averted by means of a pro­grammed feeder that con­trols the num­ber, amount, and in­ter­vals of meals that the an­i­mals in­gest. This ap­proach also fa­cil­i­tates the study of de­min­er­al­iza­tion and remineralization un­der in vi­vo con­di­tions with tight con­trol of all­ the rel­e­vant pa­ram­e­ters.
Carious le­sions and their se­ver­ity at the var­i­ous lo­ca­tions of the tooth sur­face (sulcal, smooth, and in­ter­prox­i­mal) may be eval­u­ated read­ily and with pre­ci­sion com­pa­ra­ble to that achieved in stud­ies con­ducted in hu­ mans. It is es­sen­tial that ap­pro­pri­ate sta­tis­ti­cal eval­u­a­tion be con­ducted sep­a­rately on the num­ber of le­sions or their se­ver­ity. Adding all­ the scores gives rise to data that are dif­fi­cult to eval­u­ate. Test sub­stances may be ap­ plied top­i­cally in the mouth as fre­quently as de­sired, or given sys­tem­i­cally by, for ex­am­ple, ga­vage. Effects on the microbiota, sal­i­vary flow, or com­po­ si­tion or any other pa­ram­e­ter can be read­ily and re­pro­duc­ibly de­ter­mined.

In Situ (Intraoral) Models
In si­tu mod­els have been used ex­ten­sively to study car­ies be­cause they in­ tro­duce car­i­o­genic con­di­tions in the mouth of an in­di­vid­ual, with­out­ ex­ pos­ing their own teeth to car­ies de­vel­op­ment. The model, in its sim­plest terms, in­volves plac­ing spec­i­mens pre­pared from hu­man (or bo­vine) tooth enamel (“enamel slab or block”) or dentin in intraoral or den­tal ap­pli­ances (e.g., acrylic or par­tial den­tures), which are worn by hu­man sub­jects. Bio­ films are formed on the enamel slabs, al­low­ing eval­u­a­tion of the ef­fect of test agents on biofilm ac­cu­mu­la­tion and on de­min­er­al­iza­tion. Therefore, car­ies is al­lowed to de­velop in ex­og­e­nous spec­i­mens of enamel or den­tin that are in­cor­po­rated into in si­tu (intraoral) de­vices that are placed in the oral cav­ity. Cariogenic con­di­tions are usu­ally in­duced extraorally by re­ mov­ing the in si­tu de­vice and top­i­cally ap­ply­ing fer­ment­able car­bo­hy­ drates (e.g., drip­ping su­crose so­lu­tion) onto the enamel spec­i­mens to pro­mote biofilm growth. Thus, in si­tu mod­els can be used to as­sess (i) the

K E Y  P O I N T S

Dental car­ies is a diet- and biofilm-dependent dis­ease. The mi­cro­or­gan­isms in the oral cav­ity are re­quired, but not suf­ fi­cient, to cause den­tal car­ies. The for­ma­tion of car­i­o­genic bio­films is de­pen­dent upon di­e­tary sug­ars.

Sucrose is the most car­i­o­genic car­bo­hy­drate in the diet be­cause, in ad­di­tion to be­ing me­tab­o­lized to ac­ids, it serves as a sub­strate for the pro­duc­tion of an ex­tra­cel­lu­lar polysaccharide ma­trix, which pro­foundly changes the struc­ture, the mi­cro­en­vi­ron­ment, and the mi­cro­bial com­po­si­tion of the biofilm.

The as­sem­bly of a dif­fu­sion-modulating ma­trix and bac­te­rial sugar me­tab­o­lism di­rectly con­trib­ute to the cre­a­tion of lo­cal­ized acidic mi­cro­en­vi­ron­ments, which fa­vor the es­tab­lish­ment of an acidogenic/ aciduric microbiota. An acidogenic biofilm in close prox­im­ity to the tooth sur­face al­lows per­sis­tent acid pro­duc­tion (with fre­quent ex­po­sure to di­e­tary sug­ars), which even­tu­ally leads to

ac­id dissolution of the enamel re­sult­ing in car­i­ous le­sions. Thus, the diet can mod­u­late the car­i­o­genic po­ten­tial of the biofilm.

Fluoride is cur­rently the main­stay of car­ies pre­ven­tion, but it does not of­fer com­plete pro­tec­tion. Fluoride ex­erts ma­jor phys­i­co­chem­ i­cal ef­fects by re­duc­ing min­eral loss and re­pair­ing ex­ist­ing car­i­ous le­sions, but it has lim­ited ef­fects against bio­films. New ap­proaches tar­get­ing car­i­o­genic bio­films while en­hanc­ing the pro­tec­tive ef­fects of fluo­ride are be­ing de­vel­oped.

The many as­pects of the in­ter­play be­tween the biofilm, diet, and an­ti­car­ies agents can be in­ves­ti­gated us­ing in vi­tro, an­i­mal or in si­tu biofilm and car­ies mod­els. These mod­els can eval­u­ate (i) the ef­fect of di­e­tary sug­ars on the struc­ture and prop­er­ties of the biofilm, (ii) the antibiofilm prop­er­ties of pro­spec­tive ther­a­peu­tic agents, and (iii) the ef­fect of an­ti­car­ies agents on de­min­er­al­iza­tion of min­eral sub­strates on which bio­films are grown.
