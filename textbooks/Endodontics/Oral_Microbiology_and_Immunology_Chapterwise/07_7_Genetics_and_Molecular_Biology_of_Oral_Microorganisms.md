INTRODUCTION

Fundamental Terms in Bacterial Genetics
Genetic anal­y­sis of oral bac­te­ria be­gan in the 1970s with the first re­ports of plas­mid DNA in a strain of Streptococcus mutans. In the 1980s, mech­a­ nisms of ge­netic ex­change be­tween oral strep­to­coc­cal spe­cies were de­scribed.
The iso­la­tion of nat­u­rally oc­cur­ring strep­to­coc­cal plas­mids led to the de­vel­ op­ment of new mo­lec­u­lar tools for ge­netic stud­ies. By the late 1980s and
1990s, it was pos­si­ble to ge­net­i­cally ma­nip­u­late other oral mi­cro­or­gan­isms, such as Actinomyces spp., Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, and
Porphyromonas gingivalis. Genetic stud­ies were then ex­tended to strains of
Treponema denticola, soon fol­lowed by ge­netic and mo­lec­u­lar an­a­ly­ses of
Fusobacterium nucleatum and Prevotella intermedia.

Progress to­ward a more com­pre­hen­sive un­der­stand­ing of the ge­net­ics of mi­cro­or­gan­isms, as well as cor­re­la­tions be­tween ge­no­types and phe­no­ types un­der a va­ri­ety of en­vi­ron­men­tal con­di­tions, has been ac­cel­er­ated by the de­vel­op­ment of re­com­bi­nant DNA tech­nol­ogy and the pub­li­ca­tion of the nu­cle­o­tide base se­quences of an ev­er-increasing num­ber of bac­te­rial ge­nomes. Indeed, the ge­no­mic se­quences of over 300 oral bac­te­rial spe­cies have now been de­ter­mined. With the advent of high-sensitivity, high-through­ put DNA se­quenc­ing tech­nol­o­gies, the ge­netic com­po­si­tion of biofilm com­ mu­ni­ties taken di­rectly from the oral cav­ity can be ob­tained, by­pass­ing the need for cul­ti­va­tion and ma­nip­u­la­tion of fas­tid­i­ous oral bac­te­ria. The ap­pli­ca­tion of ge­netics and mo­lec­u­lar bi­ol­ogy to stud­ies of the oral micro­ biota is the topic of this chap­ter and Chap­ter 8. Some im­por­tant terms, as they ap­ply to mi­cro­or­gan­isms, and more spe­cif­i­cally to bac­te­ria, are de­fined in Table 1; how­ever, it is as­sumed that the reader has at least a ba­sic un­der­ stand­ing of the fun­da­men­tals of ge­net­ics.

Horizontal Gene Transfer
HGT, or lat­eral gene trans­fer, re­fers to the mech­a­nism by which mi­crobes can ac­quire novel seg­ments of DNA. This trans­fer from do­nor to re­cip­i­ ent can oc­cur be­tween strains of the same spe­cies or among dif­fer­ent spe­ cies (Fig. 1B) and is con­sid­ered the main driv­ing force be­hind the ge­netic evo­lu­tion and ad­ap­ta­tion of the oral mi­cro­bial com­mu­nity. The ex­tent of
HGT among the oral microbiota be­came ev­i­dent with the advent of mo­ lec­u­lar tools that en­able phy­lo­ge­netic com­par­i­sons of spe­cific genes, re­ gions of DNA, or amino acid se­quences. These an­a­ly­ses re­veal lev­els of iden­tity or sim­i­lar­ity of genes with ho­mo­logues in other strains or orthologues in other spe­cies and so pro­vide ev­i­dence of HGT. Such an­a­ly­ses also led to the iden­ti­fi­ca­tion of mo­saic genes. These genes com­prise al­ter­nate blocks of nu­cle­o­tides that have been de­rived from a do­nor and its re­cip­i­ ent as a re­sult of HGT be­tween re­lated spe­cies. More re­cently, se­quencebased para­met­ric an­a­ly­ses have en­abled com­par­i­sons of re­gions of DNA in the con­text of the en­tire ge­nome. Bacterial ge­nomes have a char­ac­ter­is­ tic av­er­age G+C con­tent that can range from about 25 to 75%, and within a given spe­cies, genes are typ­i­cally rel­a­tively uni­form in the dis­tri­ bu­tion of this DNA base com­po­si­tion. For ex­am­ple, P. gingivalis ge­nomes have an av­er­age G+C con­tent of 48%, whereas those of F. nucleatum or
S. mutans have a G+C con­tent of 27% or 36%, re­spec­tively. Thus, iden­ti­ fi­ca­tion of a gene or re­gion of DNA with a base com­po­si­tion mark­edly dif­fer­ent from that of the ge­nome as a whole sug­gests that the atyp­i­cal re­gion of DNA may have been ac­quired by HGT from a spe­cies with a dif­fer­ent G+C con­tent. Similar an­a­ly­ses can be con­ducted us­ing co­don bias and di­nu­cle­o­tide fre­quen­cies. The iden­ti­fi­ca­tion of seg­ments of DNA spe­cif­i­cally as­so­ci­ated with mo­bile ge­netic el­e­ments (MGEs) such as plas­ mids or trans­po­sons (see be­low), or of flank­ing genes with dis­pa­rate base com­po­si­tion, pro­vi­des ad­di­tional ev­i­dence of a for­eign or­i­gin of the DNA.
There are three main mech­a­nisms by which HGT can oc­cur: (i) con­ju­ ga­tion, di­rect trans­fer of DNA be­tween host and re­cip­i­ent, of­ten via a sex pi­lus; (ii) trans­for­ma­tion, up­take of DNA from the ex­og­e­nous en­vi­ron­ ment; or (iii) trans­duc­tion, trans­fer of DNA by bac­te­rio­phage (bac­te­rial vi­rus). Additionally, mem­brane ves­i­cles are now rec­og­nized as con­trib­ut­ ing to HGT. Following up­take by the re­cip­i­ent cell, the DNA is then main­ tained, ei­ther through in­te­gra­tion onto the re­cip­i­ent chro­mo­some or as an ex­tra­chro­mo­somal el­e­ment (e.g., plas­mid).

CONJUGATION
Conjugation in­volves the trans­fer of DNA be­tween two met­a­bol­i­cally ac­ tive cells in a pro­cess that re­quires DNA rep­li­ca­tion, DNA trans­port, and cell-to-cell con­tact (Fig. 3). This con­tact is of­ten me­di­ated by for­ma­tion of a tube (sex pi­lus) or pore, through which the DNA is ac­tively trans­ferred.
DNA el­e­ments trans­ferred by con­ju­ga­tion com­prise plas­mids or trans­po­ sons (so-called “jump­ing genes”). A DNA el­e­ment that pos­sesses all­ of the genes nec­es­sary for trans­fer­ring it­self is re­ferred to as “conjugative.” By con­trast, DNA that is “mobilizable” pos­sesses one or more prop­er­ties nec­ es­sary for conjugative trans­fer, but most of the genes re­quired for con­ju­ ga­tion are en­coded by an­other co­res­i­dent conjugative plas­mid or on the do­nor chro­mo­some. Evidence of HGT by con­ju­ga­tion in oral mi­crobes in­cludes the pres­ence of MGEs ca­pa­ble of conjugative trans­fer in oral bac­te­rial iso­lates and ex­am­ ples of con­ju­ga­tion in vi­tro in which do­nor, re­cip­i­ent, or both have been oral spe­cies. Among the most com­mon types of MGE trans­fer­able by con­ju­ga­ tion are plas­mids. Many Gram-negative bac­te­ria har­bor conjugative plas­ mids known as F (sex fac­tor) plas­mids. These en­code the pro­duc­tion of sex pili that ini­ti­ate con­tact be­tween re­cip­i­ent and do­nor cells, lead­ing to the for­ma­tion of ag­gre­gates pos­sess­ing an in­ter­cel­lu­lar mem­brane pore that en­ ables DNA trans­port (Fig. 3A). The ini­tial phase at the DNA level in­volves a sin­gle-strand cleav­age at the or­i­gin of trans­fer me­di­ated by a relaxase en­ zyme com­plex. This is fol­lowed by trans­port of the nicked strand of DNA from the do­nor to the re­cip­i­ent cell and syn­the­sis of com­ple­men­tary DNA in both cells to yield a dou­ble-stranded prod­uct. A large num­ber of Grampositive, and some Gram-negative, bac­te­rial spe­cies har­bor conjugative plas­ mids that me­di­ate their own DNA trans­fer to, and rep­li­ca­tion in, a re­cip­i­ent host but do not en­code func­tions re­quired for the for­ma­tion of do­norrecipient pairs or ag­gre­gates. For these plas­mids to be trans­ferred from a do­nor to a re­cip­i­ent cell, it is nec­es­sary that po­ten­tial do­nors and re­cip­i­ents come into con­tact in­ci­den­tally on a solid sur­face. In na­ture, such in­ci­den­tal con­tact might oc­cur in a biofilm such as den­tal plaque.
Conjugative plas­mids are large due to the re­quire­ment for genes suf­ fi­cient to con­fer all­ of the prop­er­ties in­volved in conjugative trans­fer. For ex­am­ple, the conjugative plas­mid pVT745 from A. actinomycetemcomitans is com­posed of 25.4 kb, and the genes nec­es­sary for DNA pro­cess­ing and mat­ing are en­coded in two gene clus­ters. The E. faecalis plas­mid pAMβ1 is a broad-host-range conjugative plas­mid of 26.5 kb that has been in­tro­duced into a num­ber of dis­tinct oral strep­to­cocci, in­clud­ing S.
mutans, Streptococcus san­gui­nis, and Streptococcus salivarius. Mobilizable plas­mids are much smaller and in­clude pYHBil (5.1 kb) from P. intermedia and pVA380-1 (4.2 kb) from Streptococcus ferus. Transfer of conjugative plas­mids in E. faecalis is con­trolled by two pep­tide pher­o­ mones, an in­ducer and an in­hib­i­tor (Fig. 3B). The gene en­cod­ing the in­ducer pep­tide is located on the E. faecalis chro­mo­some and trig­gers ac­ti­va­tion of the sig­nal­ing path­way re­quired for con­ju­ga­tion. The gene en­cod­ing the in­hib­i­tor pep­tide is car­ried on the conjugative plas­mid and func­tions to en­sure that con­ju­ga­tion will not oc­cur be­tween two cells car­ry­ing the same conjugative plas­mid. These pep­tide pher­o­mones are highly spe­cific and will only in­duce con­ju­ga­tion of the cog­nate plas­mid.

TRANSFORMATION
Transformation in­volves the up­take of cell-free DNA from the en­vi­ron­ ment by re­cip­i­ent bac­te­ria. The abil­ity of a bac­te­rium to ac­tively take up
DNA is re­ferred to as “com­pe­tence,” and spe­cific en­vi­ron­men­tal con­di­ tions can in­flu­ence com­pe­tence de­vel­op­ment. Natural trans­for­ma­tion ap­ pears to be wide­spread in bac­te­ria and com­mon to many oral spe­cies, in­clud­ing P. gingivalis and sev­eral oral Streptococcus spe­cies (Table 2). It is for the lat­ter that the pro­cess has been best de­scribed.
In strep­to­cocci, com­pe­tence de­vel­ops dur­ing the early to late log­a­rith­ mic phase of growth, and there are con­sid­er­able dif­fer­ences in the op­ti­ mal con­di­tions for any spe­cific strain or spe­cies. Nonetheless, all­ nat­u­rally trans­form­able strep­to­cocci share the fol­low­ing com­pe­tence-related char­ ac­ter­is­tics: (i) a set of core genes re­quired for up­take of ex­og­e­nous DNA, ex­pres­sion of which is reg­u­lated by al­ter­na­tive sigma fac­tor σx (also known as ComX or SigX); (ii) a pep­tide pher­o­mone-sensing sys­tem that in­duces ex­pres­sion of σx; and (iii) ac­ces­sory reg­u­la­tory mech­a­nisms that mod­u­late
σx ac­tiv­ity in re­sponse to a va­ri­ety of in­ter­nal or ex­ter­nal cues. To date, two sig­nal­ing sys­tems that di­rectly reg­u­late comX ex­pres­sion have been iden­ti­ fied, ComCDE and ComRS (Fig. 4).

Following in­duc­tion of the late com­pe­tence genes, the trans­for­ma­tion pro­cess is ini­ti­ated with bind­ing of dou­ble-stranded DNA to the bac­te­rial cell sur­face. This bind­ing is not de­pen­dent upon the DNA se­quence and is sen­si­ tive to DN­ase treat­ment. For S. pneumoniae, re­cent ev­i­dence sug­gests that the ini­tial DNA bind­ing is me­di­ated by a trans­for­ma­tion pi­lus, ho­mol­o­gous to type IV pili of Gram-negative bac­te­ria, that de­liv­ers DNA to the cell mem­ brane. Following nick­ing of the DNA by a spe­cific nu­cle­ase (e.g., EndA in S.
pneu­mo­niae), a sin­gle DNA strand is trans­ported into the bac­te­rial cell in a
3′-to-5′ di­rec­tion, while the other strand is de­graded. Within the bac­te­rial cell, the DNA strand is pro­tected from in­tra­cel­lu­lar deg­ra­da­tion by sin­glestrand DNA-binding pro­teins (e.g., DprA, RecA). DNA may then be incorporated into the re­cip­i­ent cell chro­mo­some in a RecA-dependent pro­cess if it con­tains a suf­fi­cient re­gion of ho­mol­o­gous DNA. Plasmid DNA may cir­cu­lar­ ize and be­come es­tab­lished as an ex­tra­chro­mo­somal rep­li­cat­ing el­e­ment, but this pro­cess is lim­ited in ef­fi­ciency due to the recircularization re­quire­ment.
Alongside prod­ucts re­quired for DNA up­take, late com­pe­tence genes of­ten en­code mu­rein hy­dro­lases, oth­er­wise known as fratricins. These cell wall-degrading en­zymes serve to link trans­for­ma­tion with a cell ly­sis phe­nom­e­non known as frat­ri­cide. Best char­ac­ter­ized for S. pneumoniae, frat­ri­cide de­scribes a com­pe­tence-induced cell ly­sis mech­a­nism, in which a subfraction of cells is killed, re­leas­ing their DNA into the ex­tra­cel­lu­lar en­vi­ron­ment. This DNA can then be taken up by the sur­viv­ing com­pe­tent pop­u­la­tion, pro­mot­ing ge­netic ex­change. Likewise, late com­pe­tence genes of S. mutans en­code bac­te­rio­cins such as mutacin IV or CipB (mutacin V).
This has been shown ex­per­i­men­tally to al­low S. mutans to take up DNA from closely re­lated, com­pet­ing spe­cies, such as S. gordonii, or from a subfraction of its own pop­u­la­tion, re­spec­tively. Thus, nat­u­ral com­pe­tence de­ vel­op­ment in strep­to­cocci ap­pears to in­volve a com­plex co­or­di­na­tion of mech­a­nisms pro­vid­ing a pool of do­nor DNA as well as the up­take of en­ vi­ron­men­tal DNA.

Haemophilus spe­cies and A. actinomycetemcomitans, dif­fers from that found in the strep­to­cocci. The USS of Haemophilus influenzae and A. actinomycetemcomitans, char­ac­ter­ized by a 9-bp core se­quence (5′-AAGTGCGTT), is found at much greater fre­quency in the ge­nome than would be ex­pected if its oc­cur­rence were ran­dom.

These re­sults dem­on­strate the rel­e­vance of trans­for­ma­tion in nor­mal oral ecol­ogy and path­o­gen­e­sis and the im­ por­tance of com­men­sal mi­cro­or­gan­isms as a “re­pos­i­to­ry” for ge­netic de­ter­mi­nants.

TRANSDUCTION
Transduction re­fers to the in­tro­duc­tion of DNA into a bac­te­rial cell by a bac­te­rio­phage. Bacteriophages have been found in a va­ri­ety of bac­te­rial spe­cies, and are the most abun­dant vi­ral en­ti­ties on Earth. However, trans­ duc­tion re­quires spe­cific re­cep­tors on the re­cip­i­ent cell that are rec­og­nized by the bac­te­rio­phage, and this re­quire­ment lim­its the bac­te­rial host range of bac­te­rio­phage. This qui­es­cent “pro­phage” DNA may be in­duced to lytic growth with sub­se­ quent pro­duc­tion of phage par­ti­cles and bac­te­rial cell ly­sis.

MEMBRANE VESICLE-MEDIATED GENE TRANSFER
Another mech­a­nism that is now rec­og­nized as a po­ten­tial con­trib­ut­ing fac­tor to HGT among mi­cro­bial com­mu­ni­ties is mem­brane ves­i­cle (MV) bio­gen­e­sis. MVs are dis­crete struc­tures, en­closed by a lipid bi­la­yer, that range from 50 to 300 nm in di­am­e­ter. They may be re­leased into the en­vi­ ron­ment or re­tained on the bac­te­rial cell sur­face. MVs are known to be pro­duced by a wide range of Gram-negative bac­te­ria, aris­ing from bud­ding of the outer mem­brane ves­i­cles (OMVs), but more re­cently MVs have also been as­so­ci­ated with Gram-positive bac­te­ria such as Bacillus subtilis and Staphylococcus au­re­us. Among the oral bac­te­ria, MV pro­duc­tion has been best de­scribed for P. gingivalis but has also been re­ported for A.
actinomycetemcomitans, Tannerella for­syth­ia, S. san­gui­nis, and S. mutans.
Where in­ves­ti­gated, MV bio­gen­e­sis has been shown to be an ac­tive pro­ cess. Comparative an­a­ly­ses of MVs with the bac­te­rial cells from which they orig­i­nate indicate en­rich­ing of spe­cific lip­ids and pro­teins to the mem­brane bi­la­yer, al­though the mech­a­nis­tic ba­sis for this re­mains to be de­fined. Furthermore, while highly spe­cies spe­cific, MVs have been shown to carry a vast ar­ray of vir­u­lence fac­tors, in­clud­ing tox­ins or deg­ra­da­tive en­zymes, and im­ mune mod­u­la­tors such as li­po­po­ly­sac­cha­ride (LPS), pep­ti­do­gly­can, or small
RNAs. MVs can thus be con­sid­ered im­por­tant com­po­nents of the ar­se­nal of a num­ber of bac­te­rial path­o­gens. For ex­am­ple, MVs of P. gingivalis have been shown to pro­mote bac­te­rial coaggregation with a va­ri­ety of oral mi­cro­ or­gan­isms (e.g., T. denticola, strep­to­cocci, Actinomyces, Candida albicans), along with host tis­sue de­struc­tion, im­mune eva­sion and nu­tri­ent cap­ture.
Of rel­e­vance to HGT is the dis­cov­ery that MVs may also carry DNA.
This pro­vi­des the op­por­tu­nity for MVs to (i) serve as DNA de­liv­ery ve­hi­cles fol­low­ing fu­sion with the sur­face of re­cip­i­ent cells or (ii) fa­cil­i­tate re­lease of DNA into the en­vi­ron­ment that can be taken up by nat­u­rally com­pe­tent bac­te­ria via trans­for­ma­tion. MV-mediated DNA trans­fer has been dem­on­ strated us­ing Escherichia coli strain O157:H7. This strain pro­duces MVs car­ry­ing genes en­cod­ing cy­to­tox­ins, an­ti­bi­otic re­sis­tance, or ex­per­i­men­ tally added green fluo­res­cent pro­tein (GFP), which can be trans­ferred into and ex­pressed by E. coli strain JM109 and Salmonella enterica serovar
Enteridis. These genes orig­i­nated from chro­mo­somal, phage, or plas­mid
DNA, dem­on­strat­ing the ca­pac­ity for MVs to carry DNA from mul­ti­ple sources. No di­rect ev­i­dence has yet been pro­vided for MV-mediated DNA trans­fer among oral bac­te­ria, but the de­tec­tion of DNA in MVs of S. mutans im­plies that this may rep­re­sent a fourth mech­a­nism that con­trib­utes to HGT among the oral microbiota.
Based on the myr­iad of ex­am­ples listed here, it is clear that HGT mech­a­nisms are wide­spread among bac­te­ria that in­habit the oral cav­ity.
Conjugative trans­fer of plas­mids or trans­po­sons plays an im­por­tant role, as ex­em­pli­fied by the spread of an­ti­bi­otic re­sis­tance genes. Natural trans­ for­ma­tion and trans­duc­tion in spe­cific groups of oral spe­cies are im­por­tant for trans­fer of vir­u­lence fac­tors and reassortment of al­leles. The ex­tent of
HGT likely re­flects the fact that the ac­qui­si­tion of new DNA may con­ trib­ute to en­vi­ron­men­tal and evo­lu­tion­ary di­ver­si­fi­ca­tion, al­low­ing the bac­te­rium to sur­vive un­der a wider range of en­vi­ron­men­tal con­di­tions, and to evolve over time. Defense from Foreign DNA

RESTRICTION AND MODIFICATION SYSTEMS
Although HGT mech­a­nisms are wide­spread among oral bac­te­ria, ex­ces­ sive trans­fer of DNA is ev­i­dently det­ri­men­tal, as most bac­te­ria have mech­ a­nisms in place to reg­u­late the tim­ing and source of DNA that is ac­cept­able for trans­fer. A com­mon mi­cro­bial de­fense against for­eign DNA is the pro­ duc­tion of re­stric­tion en­do­nu­cle­ases, which are en­zymes ca­pa­ble of rec­og­ niz­ing spe­cific 4- to 8-bp DNA se­quences, designated re­stric­tion sites, and then “cut­ting” DNA con­tain­ing this re­stric­tion site into smaller frag­ments.
Restriction en­zymes are typ­i­cally un­able to cleave DNA that has been meth­yl­ated at the re­stric­tion site, and thus host bac­te­ria will also en­code a mod­i­fi­ca­tion en­zyme re­spon­si­ble for meth­yl­a­tion of the spe­cific nu­cle­o­ tides in the host cell. Thus, a bac­te­rial host en­codes a re­stric­tion-modification sys­tem, which meth­yl­ates the host DNA and cleaves any in­com­ing DNA that lacks the cor­rect meth­yl­a­tion. The abil­ity of a bac­te­rial sys­tem to tar­ get “non­self” DNA for deg­ra­da­tion is pos­tu­lated to func­tion in pro­tec­tion against in­vad­ing DNA but may also pro­vide a nu­tri­ent pool for syn­the­sis of host cell nu­cleic ac­ids. The pro­por­tion of bac­te­rial strains that en­code re­stric­tion mod­i­fi­ca­tion sys­tems may be as high as 80%, and re­stric­tion en­do­nu­cle­ases have been iden­ti­fied in all­ the bac­te­rial ge­nomes of oral spe­ cies pub­lished to date. Multiple re­stric­tion-mod­i­fi­ca­tion sys­tems may oc­cur in a sin­gle strain. For ex­am­ple, up to seven pos­si­ble re­stric­tion-mod­i­fi­ca­tion sys­tems were iden­ti­fied in the ge­nome se­quence of one strain of F. nucleatum. The pres­ence of these de­fenses is a nat­u­ral part of bac­te­rial ge­nomes, but the ex­is­tence of re­stric­tion en­do­nu­cle­ases can be a sig­nif­i­cant bar­rier to the de­vel­op­ment of ef­fi­cient lab­o­ra­tory pro­to­cols for ge­netic trans­fer in cer­tain bac­te­rial strains.

CRISPR-Cas SYSTEMS
More re­cently, a sec­ond form of de­fense from for­eign DNA has been dis­ cov­ered. A fam­ily of re­pet­i­tive DNA se­quences des­ig­nated as “clus­tered reg­u­larly in­ter­spaced short pal­in­dromic re­peats” (CRISPR) has been de­ tected in dif­fer­ent pro­kary­otes (Bacteria and Archaea), but not in eu­kary­ otes or vi­ruses. CRISPR-Cas sys­tems func­tion as adap­tive im­mune sys­tems in pro­kary­otes, due to the unique abil­ity to rec­og­nize for­eign se­quences and mount ro­bust re­sponses against in­fec­tions. The ef­fec­tor com­plexes gen­er­ated from CRISPRs and their flank­ing CRISPR-associated (cas) genes func­tion as RNA-guided nu­cle­ases that se­quence-specifically de­ grade in­vad­ing for­eign mo­bile ge­netic ma­te­rial, such as bac­te­rio­phages or conjugative plas­mids. Before the dis­cov­ery of CRISPRs, adap­tive im­mune sys­tems were con­sid­ered pres­ent only in eu­kary­otes. Therefore, CRISPRCas has fun­da­men­tally re­de­fined the de­fense sys­tems in mi­crobes. In ad­ di­tion, func­tional CRISPR-Cas sys­tems can be con­structed in vi­tro and thus have ap­pli­ca­tion in ge­nome ed­it­ing. Indeed, ge­nome ed­it­ing in mam­ ma­lian cells has been ac­com­plished via en­gi­neered CRISPR-Cas sys­tems, which have be­come rev­o­lu­tion­ary tools for ba­sic re­search and bio­tech­ nol­ogy. CRISPR-Cas sys­tems can be used to ef­fi­ciently edit the ge­nomes of var­i­ous spe­cies, in­clud­ing an­i­mals, plants, and mi­crobes. The ap­pli­ca­ tions of en­gi­neered CRISPR are dis­cussed in Chapter 8. The cur­rent chap­ ter de­scribes nat­u­rally oc­cur­ring CRISPR-Cas sys­tems in pro­kary­otes.

CRISPR struc­ture
CRISPR loci are pres­ent in roughly 40% of bac­te­rial ge­nomes and
90% of ar­chaeal ge­nomes. The se­quences of the CRISPR have proven to be re­mark­ably di­verse, with a high level of po­ly­mor­phism in dif­fer­ent spe­cies and strains. At the ge­no­mic level, CRISPR loci in na­ture gen­er­ally con­sist of di­rect re­peats of 21 to 50 bp, sep­a­rated by spacer se­quences of sim­i­lar length, pre­ceded by a leader se­quence of 300 to 500 bp, and flanked by the cas genes (Fig. 7A).
A CRISPR ar­ray con­sists of num­bers of al­ter­nat­ing re­pet­i­tive se­ quences (di­rect re­peats) and nonrepetitive se­quences (spac­ers). Each re­ peat is al­ways fol­lowed by a unique spacer. The num­ber of re­peats/spacers per lo­cus var­ies, from two to 120 in dif­fer­ent spe­cies, and these rep­re­sent the most widely dis­trib­uted fam­ily of re­peats among pro­kary­otic ge­nomes.
Repeats range in size from 21 to 50 bp and are par­tially pal­in­dromic, in­ di­cat­ing the for­ma­tion of sec­ond­ary struc­tures such as hair­pins. Conserved re­peat se­quences are sep­a­rated by unique spacer se­quences of sim­i­lar length. Spacers are typ­i­cally of ex­og­e­nous or­i­gin and ac­quired di­rectly from for­eign trans­mis­si­ble ge­netic el­e­ments such as bac­te­rio­phages and plas­mids. The ac­qui­si­tion of the spacer se­quences al­lows the CRISPR sys­tem to pos­sess her­i­ta­ble mem­ory of pre­vi­ous in­fec­tion. Some spac­ers match the se­quences in host ge­nomes, in­di­cat­ing the pos­si­bil­ity of a role in gene reg­u­la­tion or au­to­im­mu­nity in pro­kary­otes.

CRISPR clas­si­fi­ca­tion
Despite their rel­a­tively short his­tory, there are ro­bust in­ves­ti­ga­tions in CRISPR-Cas sys­tems and, thereby, mul­ti­ple no­men­cla­ture and clas­si­fi­ ca­tion sys­tems. The abil­ity to re­pro­duc­ibly cleave DNA at a de­fined base pair se­quence pro­vided the nec­es­sary foun­da­tion for the de­vel­op­ment of DNA map­ping and se­quenc­ ing tech­niques. In ad­di­tion, each en­zyme leaves char­ac­ter­is­tic ends on the cleaved DNA mol­e­cule, which may be blunt or over­hang­ing with ex­ten­ sions of ei­ther 3′ or 5′ ends. The over­hang­ing ends, re­ferred to as “sticky” or
“co­he­sive” ends, pro­vide a pow­er­ful tem­plate for reannealing of the DNA based on the spec­i­fic­ity of the base pair se­quence. The in­ser­tion of het­er­ol­o­gous DNA into a plas­mid and its prop­a­ga­tion in E. coli constitute the pro­cess known as clon­ing. The clon­ing and char­ac­ter­iza­tion of genes and gene prod­ucts of oral bac­te­ria were first re­ported in the early 1980s.
Early re­com­bi­nant DNA tech­nol­ogy re­lied to a large ex­tent on nat­u­ rally oc­cur­ring re­stric­tion en­do­nu­cle­ase sites. This proved cum­ber­some when con­ve­nient sites were not pres­ent, but this lim­i­ta­tion has largely been over­come with a ma­jor tech­no­log­i­cal ad­vance in the 1980s, namely the de­vel­op­ment of the PCR. PCR (Fig. 8) is a pro­cess by which mil­li­ons of cop­ies of a spe­cific re­gion of DNA can be syn­the­sized us­ing a min­ute quan­ tity of DNA tem­plate, a pool of the four in­di­vid­ual de­oxy­ri­bo­nu­cle­o­tides, and short ol­i­go­nu­cle­o­tide prim­ers com­ple­men­tary to DNA se­quences flank­ing the re­gion of in­ter­est. The de­sired DNA frag­ment is am­pli­fied in a cy­cli­cal ther­mal pro­cess of de­na­tur­ation, an­neal­ing of ol­i­go­nu­cle­o­tide prim­ ers, and syn­the­sis of com­ple­men­tary DNA with in­cor­po­ra­tion of nu­cle­o­tides us­ing a ther­mo­sta­ble DNA po­ly­mer­ase. A sin­gle DNA frag­ment can be am­pli­fied to hun­dreds of mil­li­ons of cop­ies in 30 to 40 cy­cles, and the re­ sult­ing DNA mol­e­cule is re­ferred to as an “amplimer.” A pow­er­ful use of
PCR tech­nol­ogy in­volves the ad­di­tion of novel base pairs into the ol­i­go­ nu­cle­o­tide prim­ers and thus the amplimer. This can en­able new re­stric­tion en­do­nu­cle­ase sites or re­gions of ho­mol­ogy to be in­tro­duced to fa­cil­i­tate sub­se­quent mo­lec­u­lar ma­nip­u­la­tions. Today the ba­sic pro­cesses of spe­ cific DNA cleav­age and religation in com­bi­na­tion with PCR al­low the de­sign and en­gi­neer­ing of al­most un­lim­ited com­bi­na­tions of DNA frag­ ments of di­verse bi­o­log­i­cal or syn­thetic or­i­gin.

BA­SIC PLASMID FEATURES
The clon­ing and char­ac­ter­iza­tion of DNA from oral bac­te­rial spe­cies in ex­pres­sion hosts such as E. coli or other het­er­ol­o­gous hosts can pro­vide con­sid­er­able in­for­ma­tion re­gard­ing in­di­vid­ual genes and, in some cases, their gene prod­ucts. Ultimately, how­ever, it is im­por­tant to be ­able to con­ duct mo­lec­u­lar ma­nip­u­la­tions in the bac­te­rial spe­cies of in­ter­est so that gene func­tion can be eval­u­ated in the na­tive host back­ground. One re­ quire­ment for the ma­jor­ity of such stud­ies is the avail­abil­ity of vec­tors into which DNA can be added and which can be in­tro­duced into the host of choice. The most ba­sic prop­erty of such a vec­tor is the abil­ity to func­ tion as a rep­li­con in the host of in­ter­est. Other prop­er­ties com­mon to vec­tors in­clude (i) the pres­ence of at least one, and pref­er­a­bly sev­eral, unique re­stric­tion en­do­nu­cle­ase rec­og­ni­tion se­quences to serve as clon­ing sites for the in­tro­duc­tion of ho­mol­o­gous or het­er­ol­o­gous DNA and (ii) a se­lect­able trait, such as re­sis­tance to an an­ti­bi­otic, to de­tect the pres­ence of the vec­tor in a host into which it has been in­tro­duced. The Rep pro­tein binds to a DNA se­quence within the cor­re­spond­ing ori that is char­ac­ter­is­tic of that rep­li­con. Host pro­teins re­quired for rep­li­ca­tion are pro­vided in trans from genes on the host bac­te­rial chro­mo­some. Plasmids can be cat­e­go­rized ac­ cord­ing to the par­tic­u­lar mech­a­nism by which they rep­li­cate. Two gen­eral modes of rep­li­ca­tion have been iden­ti­fied for cir­cu­lar plas­mids: (i) theta (also the mech­a­nism used by most bac­te­ria for rep­li­ca­tion of their chro­mo­somes) and (ii) roll­ing-circle (RC) rep­li­ca­tion. Both theta- and RC-replicating plas­ mids have been iso­lated from and used in mo­lec­u­lar an­a­ly­ses of oral mi­cro­ or­gan­isms (Table 5).

The sec­ond most im­por­tant fea­ture of a vec­tor is the pres­ence of a marker that en­ables se­lec­tion for cells that pos­sess it. Quite of­ten, the pres­ ence on a plas­mid of a se­lect­able marker is what has led to its dis­cov­ery.

DIRECTED MUTAGENESIS
Mutagenesis of a tar­get chro­mo­somal gene to con­struct an iso­genic mu­ tant is of­ten a highly suc­cess­ful ap­proach to ex­am­ine the func­tion of that gene and its gene prod­uct. Two ap­proaches that can be used for the gen­ er­a­tion of iso­genic mu­tants are in­ser­tion-duplication mu­ta­gen­e­sis and al­ le­lic re­place­ment.
Insertion-duplication mu­ta­gen­e­sis is achieved us­ing a sui­cide vec­tor. An in­ter­nal frag­ment of the tar­get gene open read­ing frame is cloned into the sui­cide vec­tor. When it is trans­formed into the bac­te­rial host, ho­mol­ogy be­ tween the tar­get gene se­quences on the chro­mo­some and vec­tor per­mits a sin­gle cross­over re­com­bi­na­tion event. This re­sults in in­te­gra­tion of the vec­ tor into the chro­mo­somal lo­cus of the tar­get gene, which splits the read­ing frame into two parts, in­ter­rupted by the in­serted vec­tor se­quence (Fig. 11A).
Transformants are iden­ti­fied and main­tained by the se­lect­able trait (e.g., an­ ti­bi­otic re­sis­tance) en­coded by the sui­cide vec­tor. Particular care has to be taken with this ap­proach when the tar­get gene is part of an op­eron. The in­ te­gra­tion event not only disrupts the tar­get gene se­quence but also sep­a­rates the pro­moter from down­stream genes. Thus, ex­cept in cases where the fi­nal gene within an op­eron is the tar­get, this ap­proach will dis­rupt ex­pres­sion of other genes within the op­eron, in­creas­ing the risk of po­lar ef­fects. To elim­i­ nate this pos­si­bil­ity, some sui­cide vec­tors now in­clude an in­duc­ible pro­moter up­stream of the mul­ti­ple clon­ing site, which can be used to con­trol ex­pres­ sion of down­stream genes within an op­eron fol­low­ing in­te­gra­tion.
Genes of bac­te­rial spe­cies that are nat­u­rally trans­form­able, such as many of the oral strep­to­coc­cal spe­cies and P. gingivalis, can be ma­nip­u­ lated with­out­ a re­quire­ment for vec­tors of any kind by marked al­le­lic re­ place­ment. One ap­proach by which markerless mu­ta­tions can be gen­er­ated is to uti­lize a con­di­tion­ally rep­li­cat­ing, tem­per­a­ture-sensitive vec­tor. 35°C. This is cloned into pJRS322 in E. coli and the re­sult­ing con­struct sub­se­quently in­tro­duced into its Gram-positive host. This is fol­lowed by a two-step in­te­gra­tion and ex­ci­sion pro­cess. Erythromycin-resistant transformants are ini­tially iden­ ti­fied fol­low­ing growth at the per­mis­sive tem­per­a­ture of 30°C. SITE-DIRECTED MUTAGENESIS
The di­rected mu­ta­gen­e­sis strat­e­gies de­scribed above are par­tic­u­larly use­ ful when seek­ing to de­lete an en­tire gene(s). However, if more sub­tle or spe­cific mu­ta­tions are re­quired, then site-directed (or site-specific) mu­ta­ gen­e­sis ap­proaches are re­quired (Fig. 12). These per­mit tar­geted sub­sti­tu­ tions, in­ser­tions, or de­le­tions to be made, which can be ex­ploited, for ex­am­ple, to ex­plore the func­tions of spe­cific do­mains within a gene, or to al­low the re­moval or in­ser­tion of a re­stric­tion en­do­nu­cle­ase site or tag.
When the de­sired end prod­uct is a lin­ear dou­ble-stranded DNA mol­ e­cule, a strat­egy known as primer ex­ten­sion can be used (Fig. 12A). This uses nested prim­ers to mu­tate the tar­get re­gion. Two ol­i­go­nu­cle­o­tide primer pairs are re­quired, A/B and C/D, in which prim­ers B and C an­neal to op­po­site strands of the tar­get site within the DNA tem­plate and carry the mis­matched ba­ses. PCR am­pli­fi­ca­tion gen­er­ates two amplicons that over­lap at the 3′ (prim­ers A/B) or 5′ (prim­ers C/D) end via the mu­tated tar­get re­gion. A sec­ond stitch PCR us­ing both amplicons as tem­plate and prim­ers A and D will then gen­er­ate the fi­nal com­bined amplicon car­ry­ing the de­sired mu­ta­tion. Subsequently, this can be cloned or used di­rectly to ma­nip­u­late a nat­u­rally trans­form­able spe­cies.
If the gene to be mu­tated is al­ready cloned within a vec­tor, an in­ verse PCR ap­proach can be taken (Fig. 12B). This uses two back-toback prim­ers, each mod­i­fied with a 5′ phos­phate group, to am­plify the whole con­struct, which is then li­gated to the cir­cu­lar form. The primer bind­ing sites can be mu­tated by al­ter­ing the primer se­quences to con­tain base mis­matches. Insertions can be made by add­ing flank­ing se­quences to the prim­ers, or de­le­tions can be made by sim­ply leav­ing a space be­ tween the two prim­ers. To re­move the orig­i­nal tem­plate (which lacks the mu­ta­tion) fol­low­ing PCR, prod­ucts can be di­gested with re­stric­tion en­do­nu­cle­ase DpnI. DpnI cleaves only when its rec­og­ni­tion site is meth­ yl­ated and thus is ac­tive against tem­plate DNA (which has been ex­ tracted from its host mi­cro­or­gan­ism) but not the newly syn­the­sized amplicon car­ry­ing the mu­ta­tion. The 5′ phos­phate groups at the ter­mini of the mu­tated amplicon then per­mit its cir­cu­lar­i­za­tion in a T4 li­gasemediated li­ga­tion re­ac­tion. The re­sult­ing mu­tated con­struct can then be in­tro­duced into the host mi­cro­or­gan­ism.

TRANSPOSONS
Integration vec­tors are use­ful for cre­at­ing knock­out­ mu­ta­tions in sit­u­a­tions where the DNA se­quence of a gene is known. In other cir­cum­stances, it may be de­sir­able to iden­tify genes as­so­ci­ated with a par­tic­u­lar phe­no­type, with­ out­ prior knowl­edge of the DNA se­quence. The iden­ti­fi­ca­tion and anal­y­sis of genes that en­code a va­ri­ety of phe­no­typic traits have been aided by ap­pli­ ca­tion of trans­po­sons to the ran­dom in­ser­tional mu­ta­gen­e­sis of bac­te­rial ge­nomes. When a trans­po­son in­serts into a bac­te­rial ge­nome, if the site of in­ser­tion is within a struc­tural gene, a de­fec­tive gene prod­uct (pro­tein) will be made, if any prod­uct at all­ is syn­the­sized. The phe­no­typic trait(s) im­ parted by that gene prod­uct (or an op­eron, if the in­ter­rupted gene is fol­ lowed by other genes within the op­eron and the in­ser­tion re­sulted in po­lar ef­fects) can be iden­ti­fied by com­par­ing the prop­er­ties of the iso­genic mu­tant to its wild-type par­ent strain. A va­ri­ety of trans­po­sons have been em­ployed in the ran­dom mu­ta­gen­e­sis of oral bac­te­rial ge­nomes, fol­lowed by stud­ies of the phe­no­types af­fected. One of the most use­ful trans­po­sons for in­ser­tional mu­ta­gen­e­sis has been the conjugative trans­po­son Tn916. Selection for the trans­fer of Tn916 via con­ju­ga­tion in­volves the use of me­dia se­lec­tive for re­ cip­i­ent strains that have be­come re­sis­tant to tet­ra­cy­cline, fol­lowed by screen­ ing of the re­sis­tant iso­lates for al­ter­ations in de­tect­able phe­no­types. The site of trans­po­son in­te­gra­tion can be de­tected by the clon­ing, from the mu­tant strain, of Tn916-associated DNA car­ry­ing the tetM gene and flank­ing chro­ mo­somal DNA. Among the oral strep­to­cocci, Tn916 has been used in the iden­ti­fi­ca­tion and study of genes en­cod­ing in­ter­ge­neric coaggregation in a strain of S. gordonii, a fi­bro­nec­tin-binding pro­tein in S. san­gui­nis, and the abil­ity to pro­duce rods, ex­hibit aciduricity, and ac­cu­mu­late in­tra­cel­lu­lar po­ ly­sac­cha­rides by strains of S. mutans.

Screening
Integration or sui­cide vec­tors en­able tar­geted mu­ta­gen­e­sis of genes of in­ ter­est. In con­trast, trans­po­sons en­able mu­ta­gen­e­sis at un­de­fined sites, so mu­tants that dis­play the loss of a phe­no­type of in­ter­est can be char­ac­ter­ ized to de­ter­mine which ge­netic loci con­trib­ute to that phe­no­type. The use of trans­po­sons could then be con­sid­ered to be phe­no­typ­i­cally tar­geted, as op­posed to the use of in­te­gra­tion vec­tors, which are ge­net­i­cally tar­geted.
With the wealth of ge­nome se­quence data avail­able, ge­net­i­cally tar­geted mu­ta­gen­e­sis is fa­cil­i­tated by knowl­edge of the se­quence and or­ga­ni­za­tion of genes pre­dicted by their DNA se­quence to be of in­ter­est. However, it is sig­nif­i­cant that of­ten more than a third of the genes in an an­no­tated ge­ nome are un­as­signed in func­tion. This high­lights the need for phe­no­typ­i­ cally tar­geted mu­ta­gen­e­sis, which pro­vi­des a pow­er­ful ap­proach to de­lin­eate novel genes con­trib­ut­ing to a phe­no­type.

CONCLUSIONS
Analyses of the ge­no­mic DNA se­quences of oral mi­cro­or­gan­isms pro­vide im­por­tant in­for­ma­tion for in­ves­ti­ga­tion of mi­cro­bial tax­on­omy, me­tab­o­ lism, and vir­u­lence mech­a­nisms. Comparative se­quence an­a­ly­ses of mi­cro­ bial ge­nomes show a tre­men­dous di­ver­sity in ge­netic com­po­si­tion among spe­cies and the im­por­tance of hor­i­zon­tal gene trans­fer in the evo­lu­tion of mi­cro­or­gan­isms. The pace of such stud­ies is, and will be, has­tened by the avail­abil­ity of the to­tal ge­no­mic se­quence of an ev­er-increasing num­ber of oral bac­te­rial spe­cies. Such se­quences pro­vide im­por­tant in­for­ma­tion as to which genes of a spe­cies are pres­ent and likely to con­trib­ute to col­o­ni­ za­tion, sur­vival, and growth in the oral en­vi­ron­ment and to the in­ter­face with host de­fenses. Investigations into nat­u­ral gene trans­fer mech­a­nisms in oral bac­te­ria re­veal the im­por­tance of hor­i­zon­tal DNA trans­fer to sur­ vival in the oral cav­ity and pro­vide ge­netic tools for the ad­vanced anal­y­sis of oral bac­te­ria. The ap­pli­ca­tion of ge­netic and mo­lec­u­lar bi­o­log­i­cal tools to the study of oral mi­cro­bi­ol­ogy now in­cludes im­por­tant oral bac­te­rial gen­era, such as Aggregatibacter, Porphyromonas, Actinomyces, Prevotella,
Fusobacterium, and Treponema. With the avail­abil­ity of the ge­netic and mo­lec­u­lar tools de­scribed in this chap­ter, it is pos­si­ble to con­firm a func­tion

K E Y  P O I N T S

DNA can be ac­quired by bac­te­rial cells through ei­ther ver­ti­cal or hor­i­zon­tal (lat­eral) trans­mis­sion. In ver­ti­cal trans­mis­sion, there is trans­fer of a copy of the pa­ren­tal DNA to daugh­ter cells upon cell di­vi­sion. In hor­i­zon­tal trans­mis­sion, there is ac­qui­si­tion of for­eign
DNA. The known mech­a­nisms re­spon­si­ble for hor­i­zon­tal gene trans­fer (HGT) are: trans­for­ma­tion, the up­take of na­ked DNA by com­pe­tent re­cip­i­ent cells; con­ju­ga­tion, the trans­fer of DNA through cell-to-cell con­tact; and trans­duc­tion, the in­tro­duc­tion of DNA into a bac­te­rial cell by bac­te­rio­phages con­tain­ing ei­ther ran­dom (gen­er­al­ ized trans­duc­tion) or spe­cific (spe­cial­ized trans­duc­tion) pieces of
DNA from the pre­vi­ous host of the phage. Membrane ves­i­cles may also have a role to play in DNA trans­fer.

Bacteria also have mech­a­nisms to block trans­fer of cer­tain types of for­eign DNA. Restriction-modification and CRISPR-Cas (for clus­tered reg­u­larly in­ter­spaced short pal­in­dromic re­peats and CRISPR-associated [cas] genes) sys­tems are two com­mon meth­ods used to de­stroy for­eign DNA by cleav­age or deg­ra­da­tion. These same bac­te­rial sys­tems have been adapted for use in mo­lec­u­lar bi­ol­ogy, for mod­i­fi­ca­tion and ma­nip­u­la­tion of DNA.

The de­vel­op­ment of com­pe­tence (the abil­ity to take up DNA by trans­for­ma­tion) in strep­to­cocci in­volves a pep­tide pher­o­ mone-sensing sys­tem that in­duces ex­pres­sion of al­ter­na­tive sigma fac­tor σx (also known as ComX or SigX). Two sig­nal­ing sys­tems have been iden­ti­fied, ComCDE and ComRS. In some
Gram-negative bac­te­ria, there is spec­i­fic­ity of DNA up­take with the pres­ence of up­take sig­nal se­quences (USS) be­ing re­quired.
Regulation of com­pe­tence in Gram-negative bac­te­ria can oc­cur by ca­tab­o­lite re­pres­sion.

Plasmids are ex­tra­chro­mo­somal rep­li­cat­ing ge­netic el­e­ments.
Most plas­mids are pres­ent in their host cells as dou­ble-stranded co­va­lently closed cir­cu­lar DNA (cccDNA) mol­e­cules. Plasmids must en­code the el­e­ments re­quired for au­ton­o­mous rep­li­ca­tion, in­clud­ing an or­i­gin of rep­li­ca­tion (ori) and con­tig­u­ous gene(s) en­cod­ing rep­li­ca­tion pro­teins and other con­trol­ling el­e­ments. Some na­tive plas­mids en­code pro­teins that con­fer re­sis­tance to an­ti­bi­ot­ ics. Other phe­no­types that may be en­coded by plas­mids in­clude vir­u­lence traits, met­a­bolic ca­pa­bil­i­ties, or the pro­duc­tion of bac­te­rio­cins. Plasmids may be ei­ther conjugative or mobilizable.
Conjugative plas­mids pos­sess all­ of the genes nec­es­sary for conjugative trans­fer. Mobilizable plas­mids re­quire genes en­cod­ing conjugative func­tions to be pro­vided by an­other plas­mid or the chro­mo­some. Circular plas­mids rep­li­cate by theta and roll­ing cir­cle (RC) mech­a­nisms.

Transposons are dis­crete nonreplicative seg­ments of DNA ca­pa­ble of in­sert­ing into, and be­ing rep­li­cated as part of, a bac­te­rial rep­li­con such as a chro­mo­some, plas­mid, or bac­te­rio­ phage ge­nome. They may also move from one site in a DNA mol­e­cule to an­other. Insertion of a trans­po­son into DNA is me­di­ated by in­ser­tion se­quence (IS) el­e­ments, which are lo­cated at the ends of trans­po­sons and en­code the en­zyme transposase, which cat­a­lyzes the in­ser­tion events.

Transposon in­ser­tion is usu­ally ran­dom; it may oc­cur at vir­tu­ally any site on a DNA mol­e­cule, in­de­pen­dent of ex­ten­sive ho­mol­ogy.
Thus, trans­po­sons can be used for ran­dom mu­ta­gen­e­sis where screen­ing for a phe­no­typic prop­erty is pos­si­ble. Some trans­po­sons can be trans­ferred by con­ju­ga­tion.

A bac­te­rial mu­tant in which only a de­fined gene has been al­tered is termed an iso­genic mutant; such alteration will lead to a change or loss of func­tion spe­cific to that gene. Restoration of the gene and/ or its ex­pres­sion, re­ferred to as com­ple­men­ta­tion of the mu­tant, should re­store func­tion.

A com­mon ap­proach to gen­er­at­ing iso­genic mu­tants is to use a vec­tor that does not rep­li­cate in the bac­te­rium of in­ter­est (a sui­cide vec­tor) but into which has been cloned DNA ho­mol­o­gous to a re­gion of the tar­geted gene, as well as a re­sis­tance de­ter­mi­ nant that pro­vi­des a marker for se­lec­tion. A sin­gle re­com­bi­na­tion (or cross­over) event will re­sult in in­te­gra­tion of the en­tire plas­mid and dis­rup­tion of the gene. Alternatively, a gene may be de­leted via al­le­lic ex­change in which a dou­ble re­com­bi­na­tion event be­tween iden­ti­cal DNA re­gions on the vec­tor and chro­mo­some that flank the tar­get gene re­sults in swap­ping of the tar­get gene with a se­lect­able marker. Markerless al­le­lic re­place­ment strat­e­gies have also been de­vi­sed that use tem­per­a­ture-sensitive or counterselectable vec­tors. Site-directed mu­ta­gen­e­sis ap­proaches can be used to make sub­sti­tu­tions, in­ser­tions, or de­le­tions within a tar­get gene, in­clud­ing primer ex­ten­sion or in­verse PCR strat­e­gies.
Phenotypic an­a­ly­ses are used to eval­u­ate the ef­fect of the gene dis­rup­tion. In all­ cases, it is im­por­tant to as­sess any po­ten­tial ef­fects on genes down­stream of the tar­get gene.

Shuttle vec­tors that rep­li­cate in both E. coli and an­other spe­cies fa­cil­i­tate clon­ing (in E. coli) and trans­fer of ge­netic ma­te­rial into the spe­cies of in­ter­est. These vec­tors can be used to gen­er­ate dou­ble re­com­bi­na­tion mu­tants or to pro­vide an in­tact copy of a gene for com­ple­men­ta­tion. Restoration of gene func­tion by com­ple­men­ta­tion en­sures that the phe­no­typic ef­fect ob­served was due to in­ac­ti­va­tion of the gene and not to a po­lar ef­fect on a down­stream gene or other un­in­tended mu­ta­tion.
