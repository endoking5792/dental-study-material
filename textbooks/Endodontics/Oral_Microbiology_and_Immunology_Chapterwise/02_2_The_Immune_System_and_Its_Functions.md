INTRODUCTION—WHAT IS IMMUNITY?
Immunity (et­y­mo­log­i­cally de­rived from immunis, Latin for “ex­empt”) has been his­tor­i­cally viewed as the body’s abil­ity to re­sist in­fec­tious dis­ease.
The as­sem­blage of tis­sues, cells, and mol­e­cules that de­fend against harm­ ful mi­cro­or­gan­isms (vi­ral, bac­te­rial, fun­gal, or par­a­sitic path­o­gens) make up the im­mune sys­tem, and their col­lec­tive and co­or­di­nated re­ac­tion is called the im­mune re­sponse. The im­mune sys­tem, there­fore, needs to dis­ tin­guish for­eign (“non­self ”) ma­te­rial from “self ” tis­sues and cells. Moreover, the im­mune sys­tem needs to neu­tral­ize or elim­i­nate non­self while spar­ing self from im­mune at­tack. The lat­ter con­cept is known as im­mune tol­er­ance, in that the body needs to “tol­er­ate” its own tis­sues. If im­mune tol­er­ance breaks down, the re­sult­ing ab­er­rant im­mune re­sponse against self-tissues may cause dis­or­ders known as au­to­im­mune dis­eas­es.

The self/nonself par­a­digm pro­vided a use­ful con­cep­tual frame­work for im­mune sys­tem re­sponses to ex­og­e­nous path­o­genic mi­cro­or­gan­isms;
how­ever, it could not ex­plain im­mu­nity to all­ types of po­ten­tial threats.
For in­stance, im­mu­nity to can­cer could not read­ily fit into the self/nonself con­cept be­cause tu­mor cells are not ac­tu­ally non­self but rather rep­re­sent
“al­tered self.” Immune re­sponses are also ac­ti­vated to cope with tis­sue dam­age that is caused by me­chan­i­cal in­jury (ster­ile in­flam­ma­tion). This has led to the prop­o­si­tion that the im­mune sys­tem has pri­mar­ily evolved to dis­crim­i­nate be­tween safe and dan­ger­ous en­ti­ties rather than be­tween self and non­self. According to the so-called dan­ger model, the im­mune sys­ tem is alarmed by sig­nals re­leased from dam­aged cells (due to in­jury or in­fec­tion), such as nu­cleic ac­ids, heat shock pro­teins, and other in­tra­cel­lu­ lar mol­e­cules, col­lec­tively known as alarmins. It has also be­come ev­i­dent that the im­mune sys­tem is con­stantly ac­tive—even when not ex­posed to path­o­gens or other types of threats—es­pe­cially at mu­co­sal sur­faces where lo­cal im­mune cells pro­ac­tively main­tain a sym­bi­otic re­la­tion­ship with com­men­sal mi­cro­or­gan­isms and pre­serve tis­sue in­teg­rity. Moreover, the ac­ti­va­tion of tolerogenic mech­a­nisms is re­quired to pre­vent un­de­sir­able re­sponses such as al­ler­gies, which are con­di­tions caused by over­ex­u­ber­ant re­ac­tions of the im­mune sys­tem to nor­mally in­noc­u­ous sub­stances from the en­vi­ron­ment. Granulocytes
This is a di­verse group of cir­cu­lat­ing leu­ko­cytes, in­clud­ing neu­tro­phils (po­ly­mor­pho­nu­clear leu­ko­cytes, or PMNs), eo­sin­o­ phils, and ba­so­phils, of which neu­tro­phils are the most nu­mer­ous in the pe­riph­eral blood. They are ex­tremely po­tent phago­cytes and are crit­i­cal to im­mu­nity against ex­tra­cel­lu­lar bac­te­rial in­fec­tions. Their gran­ules con­tain a va­ri­ety of an­ti­mi­cro­bial mol­e­cules, in­clud­ing en­zymes, cat­ionic pro­teins, defensins and a sys­tem for gen­er­a­tion of re­ac­tive ox­y­gen rad­i­cals. Neutrophils also dis­play Fc and com­ple­ment re­cep­tors on their cell sur­faces, which al­low them to ef­ fi­ciently phago­cy­tose mi­cro­bial cells coated with an­ti­body and/or frag­ments of com­ple­ment. They can also form and re­lease ex­tra­cel­lu­lar DNA traps (NETs, for neu­tro­phil ex­tra­cel­lu­lar traps), i.e., web-like scaf­folds that con­cen­trate re­leased an­ti­mi­cro­bial mol­e­cules re­sult­ing in the trap­ping and ex­tra­cel­lu­lar kill­ing of bac­te­ria. Eosinophils have a role in im­mu­nity against par­a­sites, while the ex­act func­tion of ba­so­phils re­mains poorly un­der­stood.

Monocytes/macrophages
Monocytes are cir­cu­lat­ing phago­cytic cells that can rec­og­nize mi­cro­bial cells via pat­tern rec­og­ni­tion re­cep­tors (PRRs) and can take part in clear­ance of in­tra­cel­lu­lar mi­cro­bial in­fec­tions via an­ti­body-dependent cell-me­di­ated cy­to­tox­ic­ity (ADCC).
Monocytes mi­grate from the vas­cu­la­ture and dif­fer­en­ti­ate into tis­sue res­i­dent pop­u­la­tions of mac­ro­phages (Fig. 9). In ad­di­tion to their an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tiv­i­ties, mac­ro­phages are po­tent sources of proinflammatory me­di­a­tors and can func­tion as an­ti­gen-presenting cells (APCs) for T cells. Thus, mac­ro­phages serve as a key link be­tween in­nate and adap­tive im­mu­ni­ty.

Dendritic cells
This is a het­ero­ge­neous group of in­nate im­mune cells. So-called clas­si­cal or con­ven­tional den­dritic cells (DCs) are req­ui­site for the in­duc­tion of most adap­tive im­mune re­sponses due to their abil­ity to cap­ture an­ti­gens, mi­grate to sec­ond­ary lym­phoid tis­sues, and stim­u­late the ac­ti­va­tion of helper T (Th) cells. They ex­press PRRs on their cell sur­faces, al­low­ing them to rec­og­nize mi­cro­bial cells and mol­e­cules. Activated DCs syn­the­size cy­to­kines and upregulate cell surface ex­pres­sion of costimulatory mol­e­cules, lead­ing them to func­tion as ex­tremely po­tent APCs.

Mast cells
Mast cells are typ­i­cally se­ques­tered in the con­nec­tive tis­sue un­der­ly­ing ep­i­the­lial and mu­co­sal sur­faces. Similar to cir­cu­lat­ing ba­so­phils, they ex­hibit large num­bers of cy­to­plas­mic gran­ules con­tain­ing a va­ri­ety of bio­ac­tive mol­e­cules, in­clud­ing en­zymes, cy­to­kines, and proinflammatory me­di­a­tors. Mast cells ex­press Fcε receptors and PRRs on their cell sur­faces such that they can be ac­ti­vated to re­lease the con­tents of their gran­ules by cross-link­ ing of IgE and mi­crobe-associated mo­lec­u­lar pat­terns/damageassociated mo­lec­u­lar pat­terns (MAMPs/DAMPs), re­spec­tively. Specialized tis­sue-resident mac­ro­phages. Macrophage pre­cur­sors are re­leased into the cir­cu­la­tion as mono­cytes and mi­grate through­out­ the body. As in­di­cated, ma­ture tis­sue mac­ro­phages are stra­te­gi­cally lo­cated in var­i­ous or­gans and tis­sues, where they per­form im­por­tant ac­tiv­i­ties.
These in­clude im­mune sur­veil­lance (in­flam­ma­tory re­sponses and phago­cy­to­sis) and in­ges­tion and deg­ra­da­tion of dead cells and de­bris. Macrophages also con­trib­ute to the res­o­lu­tion of in­flam­ma­tion (af­ter an in­fec­tion is cleared) and tis­sue re­pair and re­mod­el­ing. Innate lym­phoid cells
Innate lym­phoid cells (ILCs) are the most re­cently iden­ti­fied fam­ily of in­nate im­mune cells. Although they are lym­phoid cells, they lack T-cell an­ti­gen re­cep­tors (TCRs) or B-cell an­ti­gen re­cep­tors (BCRs) and can­not re­spond in an an­ti­gen-specific man­ner. ILCs are pre­dom­i­nantly found at bar­rier sur­faces (e.g., skin, air­ways, and in­tes­tine), where they col­lec­tively reg­u­late im­mu­nity, in­flam­ma­tion, and tis­sue re­pair. ILCs can ad­di­tion­ally main­tain im­mune tol­er­ance to­ward the com­men­sal microbiota in the gut. Different groups of ILCs ex­press dis­tinct sets of cy­to­kines and thus are re­spon­si­ble for spe­cial­ized func­tions (de­tails in the text).

Natural killer cells
Natural killer (NK) cells are lym­phoid lin­e­age cells that are con­sid­ered me­di­a­tors of in­nate im­mu­nity. Their ac­tiv­ity is reg­u­lated by the bal­ance of sig­nals gen­er­ated by ac­ti­vat­ing ver­sus in­hib­i­tory cell sur­face re­cep­tors. Activated NK cells rap­idly in­duce ap­o­pto­sis in tar­get cells and are par­tic­u­larly im­por­tant in me­di­at­ ing im­mu­nity against vi­ral in­fec­tions and tu­mor cells. This com­monly in­volves ADCC.

T lymphocytes
T cells are the ef­fec­tors of adap­tive cell-me­di­ated im­mune re­sponses.
Most T cells ex­hibit ei­ther CD4 or CD8 on their cell sur­faces and func­tion as Th cells and cy­to­toxic T cells (CTLs), re­spec­tively. Each cell ex­presses mul­ti­ple cop­ies of a unique ver­sion of the α/β form of the
TCR. T cells are stim­u­lated by an­ti­gen in the sec­ond­ary lym­phoid tis­sues and sub­se­quently mi­grate to ex­tra­vas­cu­lar sites in re­sponse to sig­nals pro­vided by el­e­ments of the in­nate im­mune sys­tem.
Multiple func­tional sub­sets of Th cells ex­ist that pro­duce dis­tinct cy­to­kines and take part in dis­crete cell-me­di­ated and/or hu­moral im­mune re­sponses (details in the text). CTLs are cen­tral to erad­i­ca­ tion of ob­li­gate in­tra­cel­lu­lar path­o­gens, most no­ta­bly vi­ruses. A mi­nor pop­u­la­tion of T cells typ­i­cally found in the gut and other mu­co­sal bar­rier sites ex­press the γ/δ ver­sion of TCR. They are thought to play an im­por­tant role in the rec­og­ni­tion of lipid an­ti­gens from, e.g., my­co­bac­te­ria.

B cells
B cells rep­re­sent the ef­fec­tor cells of adap­tive hu­moral im­mu­nity due to their unique abil­ity to syn­the­size an­ti­body mol­e­cules. They uti­lize a mem­brane-bound ver­sion of an­ti­body as their cell surface an­ti­gen re­cep­tor. Each cell is mono­spe­cific and ex­presses mul­ti­ple cop­ies of the same an­ti­body mol­e­cule. B cells un­dergo an­ti­genic stim­u­la­tion in sec­ond­ary lym­phoid tis­sues and dif­fer­en­ti­ate into an­ti­body-secreting plasma cells that are the source of an­ti­body found in blood, se­cre­tions, and con­nec­tive tis­sue. B cells are also in­volved in the in­duc­tion of cell-me­di­ated im­mune re­sponses by vir­tue of their ca­pac­ity to func­tion as an­ti­gen-presenting cells for Th cells.

The main com­po­nents of the adap­tive im­mune sys­tem are lym­pho­ cytes (B and T cells) and their prod­ucts, in­clud­ing an­ti­bod­ies (also re­ ferred to as im­mu­no­glob­u­lins) and cy­to­kines. IMMUNE RECOGNITION MECHANISMS
A fun­da­men­tal func­tion of the im­mune sys­tem is to sense the pres­ence of in­fec­tion and other types of in­sults. Although tra­di­tion­ally con­sid­ered as non­spe­cific, in­nate im­mu­nity is en­dowed with ad­e­quate spec­i­fic­ity to dis­ tin­guish be­tween dif­fer­ent clas­ses of mi­cro­or­gan­isms as well as to de­tect host cell dam­age. On the other hand, the adap­tive im­mune sys­tem has ex­ qui­site spec­i­fic­ity and can dis­crim­i­nate pre­cisely be­tween in­di­vid­ual mi­cro­ bes, even of the same spe­cies (i.e., can de­tect strain-dependent an­ti­genic var­i­abil­ity). However, adap­tive im­mu­nity can elicit a re­sponse only af­ter it is in­formed by the in­nate sys­tem that the rec­og­nized tar­get is dan­ger­ous to the host. If lym­pho­cytes are in­structed that there is no dan­ger, then they be­ come un­re­spon­sive (tol­er­ant) to the rec­og­nized tar­get. As long as the ac­ti­va­tion pro­cess is not in­hib­ited by spe­cial­ized reg­u­la­tory pro­teins (phys­i­o­log­i­cally pres­ent on host cells but not nor­mally on mi­cro­bial cell sur­faces or other for­eign sur­faces), this ini­ti­a­tion is fol­lowed by rapid prop­a­ga­tion of the al­ter­na­tive path­way through an am­pli­fi­ca­tion loop. In other words, any­ thing not ac­tively rec­og­nized as self will be con­sid­ered as non­self and will be tagged for opsonophagocytosis or di­rect ly­sis (Fig. 6). This strat­egy, which is as­so­ci­ated with the al­ter­na­tive path­way, re­quires less ef­fort than main­tain­ing an ex­ten­sive rep­er­toire of PRMs and is rem­i­nis­cent of the miss­ing-self rec­og­ni­tion strat­egy, orig­i­nally de­scribed in NK cells. NK cells rec­og­nize cells that lack or have lost host-specific MHC mol­e­cules, al­though it was sub­se­quently shown that NK cell rec­og­ni­tion de­pends on both the pres­ence and ab­sence of spe­cific mo­lec­u­lar mark­ers. It should be added, how­ever, that the al­ter­na­tive path­way of com­ple­ment can also be trig­gered by bac­te­rial LPS and lipooligosacharide via the plasma pro­tein pro­per­din at­tached to bac­te­rial sur­fac­es.

In con­trast to cells that me­di­ate in­nate im­mu­nity, B and T cells ex­hibit high spec­i­fic­ity with re­spect to rec­og­ni­tion of mi­crobes and dam­aged or trans­formed host cells. Individual cells ex­hibit mul­ti­ple cop­ies of a dis­tinct an­ti­gen re­cep­tor on their cell sur­face such that each cell is only ca­pa­ble of rec­og­niz­ing a sin­gle an­ti­gen. As stated above, BCRs are in fact an­ti­body mol­e­cules. For most T cells, the an­ti­gen re­cep­tors are com­posed of an α and a β chain, while a small sub­set of cells ex­press a re­cep­tor com­posed of a γ and a δ chain. INDUCTION AND STAGES OF THE IMMUNE RESPONSE
The dis­tinc­tion be­tween in­nate and adap­tive mech­a­nisms of im­mu­nity is con­ve­nient for the pur­pose of cat­e­go­riz­ing dif­fer­ent types of im­mu­no­log­ i­cal re­sponses. However, this dis­tinc­tion can be some­what mis­lead­ing, as it may im­ply that the two op­er­ate in­de­pen­dently of one an­other. The same can be said about dis­tin­guish­ing be­tween hu­moral and cell-me­di­ated forms of adap­tive im­mu­nity. The re­al­ity is that many of the cells and mol­ e­cules in­volved in in­nate im­mu­nity are es­sen­tial for the in­duc­tion and reg­u­la­tion of adap­tive im­mune re­sponses. This is es­pe­cially the case upon ini­tial ex­po­sure to a given mi­cro­bial path­o­gen, known as the pri­mary im­ mune re­sponse. Similarly, there is sig­nif­i­cant in­ter­ac­tion be­tween the cel­lu­ lar and mo­lec­u­lar el­e­ments of the hu­moral and cell-me­di­ated com­po­nents of the adap­tive im­mune sys­tem. It is, there­fore, im­per­a­tive to ap­pre­ci­ate that there is a sub­stan­tial amount of cross talk be­tween many of the var­i­ ous com­po­nents of in­nate and adap­tive im­mu­nity that can re­cip­ro­cally upand downmodulate one or an­other com­po­nent. This leads to re­fine­ment of the over­all re­sponse to one that best ad­dresses the chal­lenge at hand.
Cells typ­i­cally con­sid­ered me­di­a­tors of in­nate im­mu­nity serve as the sen­ti­nels that rec­og­nize the pres­ence of dan­ger, such as the iden­ti­fi­ca­tion of path­o­genic mi­crobes at their por­tals of en­try into the body. Thus,
Lang­er­hans cells, DCs, mac­ro­phages, and mast cells that are found in con­nec­tive tis­sue un­der­ly­ing all­ ep­i­the­lial and mu­co­sal sur­faces serve as part of the body’s ini­tial bar­rier to in­fec­tion. Via en­gage­ment of PRRs by their cog­nate MAMPs or DAMPs, the cells can sense when some­ thing is amiss whether it be a mi­cro­bial in­fec­tion or trau­ma-induced tis­sue dam­age. As dis­cussed pre­vi­ously, one out­come is the re­lease of proinflammatory me­di­a­tors by ac­ti­vated host cells. This leads to the ac­ ti­va­tion and/or re­cruit­ment of ad­di­tional el­e­ments of in­nate im­mu­nity to the site of dan­ger, in­clud­ing com­ple­ment pro­teins and neu­tro­phils. In the con­text of a bac­te­rial in­fec­tion, ac­ti­va­tion of com­ple­ment serves to en­hance elim­i­na­tion of the mi­crobes via opsonization, ly­sis, and gen­er­ a­tion of ad­di­tional proinflammatory sig­nals (Fig. 6). Relative to trauma, the in­flam­ma­tory re­ac­tion not only pro­vi­des a bar­rier to pos­si­ble in­fec­ tion but also ini­ti­ates the pro­cess of wound heal­ing via the re­cruit­ment of phago­cytic cells to elim­i­nate de­bris re­sult­ing from dam­age to pa­ren­ chy­mal and stro­mal cells as well as the ex­tra­cel­lu­lar ma­trix. In many sit­u­a­tions, the in­nate im­mune re­sponse is suf­fi­cient to over­come or pre­ vent a mi­cro­bial in­fec­tion, thereby re­sult­ing in res­o­lu­tion of an in­fec­tion and nor­mal wound heal­ing. However, there are nu­mer­ous cir­cum­stances when this does not oc­cur, thus ne­ces­si­tat­ing the as­sis­tance of adap­tive im­mu­nity. It is there­fore not sur­pris­ing that in­nate im­mune cells are es­ sen­tial for the in­duc­tion of adap­tive im­mune re­ac­tions, and in­flam­ma­ tion mo­bi­lizes the com­po­nents of hu­moral and cell-me­di­ated re­ac­tions to the site of dan­ger.

HALLMARK PROPERTIES OF IMMUNITY
From the dis­cus­sion above, it be­comes ob­vi­ous that in­nate and adap­tive im­mu­nity dis­play cer­tain sim­i­lar­i­ties. Both arms of the im­mune re­sponse ex­hibit spe­cial­i­za­tion, that is, the abil­ity to gen­er­ate re­sponses that are tai­ lored for op­ti­mal de­fense against dif­fer­ent types of chal­lenges. Another com­mon char­ac­ter­is­tic is phe­no­typic plas­tic­ity, in other words, the ca­pac­ ity of im­mune cells to adapt their func­tion in re­sponse to mi­cro­en­vi­ron­ men­tal fac­tors in a man­ner that al­lows them to bet­ter de­fend or serve the tis­sues where they are re­cruited or re­side. For in­stance, de­pend­ing on the type of in­fec­tion and cy­to­kine mi­lieu, mac­ro­phages can ac­quire M1 or M2 fea­tures that en­able them to func­tion ei­ther as proinflammatory cells that me­di­ate path­o­gen clear­ance or as anti-inflammatory cells that pro­mote tis­ sue re­pair, re­spec­tively. Moreover, as al­luded to above, Tregs can ac­quire the phe­no­typic and func­tional char­ac­ter­is­tics of Th17 cells, as they can ex­tin­guish ex­pres­sion of their mas­ter reg­u­la­tor FoxP3 and start pro­duc­ ing IL-17 in the pres­ence of IL-6 and the ab­sence of TGF-β. This phe­no­ typic plas­tic­ity could be ben­e­fi­cial when there is need for more Th17 cells to fight an in­fec­tion (e.g., a path­o­genic fun­gus), but it could ex­ac­er­bate pa­thol­ ogy in the set­ting of au­to­im­mu­nity. Despite these sim­i­lar­i­ties, in­nate and adap­tive im­mu­nity also dis­play fun­da­men­tal dif­fer­ences that de­fine each of the two arms of the host im­mune re­sponse. These are sum­ma­rized be­low.

Properties of Adaptive Immunity
Classically, hu­moral and cell-me­di­ated im­mune re­sponses were de­scribed mainly in the con­text of prop­er­ties that dis­tin­guished them from in­nate im­mu­ni­ty: (i) in­duc­ibil­ity, (ii) spec­i­fic­ity, (iii) di­ver­sity of the an­ti­gen re­cep­ tor rep­er­toires of B and T cells, (iv) mem­ory, and (v) nonreactivity to self.
B and T cells leave the pri­mary lym­phoid or­gans in a rest­ing state and must be stim­u­lated by an an­ti­gen through their an­ti­gen re­cep­tor in the con­text of ad­di­tional sig­nals in or­der to be ac­ti­vated. This rep­re­sents the fun­da­men­tal ba­sis for the in­duc­ible na­ture of adap­tive im­mune re­sponses.
Individual B and T cells ex­press mul­ti­ple cop­ies of a unique an­ti­gen re­cep­tor

Along these lines, it be­came ap­ par­ent that ran­dom re­com­bi­na­tion of gene seg­ments en­coded within the hu­man ge­nome, along with an ex­tremely high rate of so­matic mu­ta­tion af­fect­ing re­ar­ranged im­mu­no­glob­u­lin genes, pro­vi­des the po­ten­tial for the gen­er­a­tion of an in­cred­i­bly large num­ber of dis­tinct BCRs and TCRs. Once ac­ti­vated, a B or T cell un­der­goes pro­lif­er­a­tion, yield­ing a pop­u­la­tion of cells with the same an­ti­gen spec­i­fic­ity. Most of the cells will dif­fer­en­ti­ate into ef­fec­tor cells, while a small num­ber be­come mem­ory cells that will take part in sub­se­quent re­sponses to the an­ti­gen. The ac­ti­va­tion re­quire­ments of mem­ ory cells are gen­er­ally less strin­gent than those of B or T cells that take part in the pri­mary (ini­tial) re­sponse to the an­ti­gen. Furthermore, the an­ti­gen re­cep­tors ex­pressed by these cells, mem­ory B cells in par­tic­u­lar, man­i­fest sub­tle changes that lead to high­er-affinity in­ter­ac­tions with an­ti­gen. There­ fore, in the con­text of sec­ond­ary ex­po­sure to a mi­cro­bial path­o­gen, the host im­mune re­sponse is me­di­ated pre­dom­i­nantly by ac­ti­va­tion of mem­ ory cells. Relative to pri­mary re­sponses, sec­ond­ary re­sponses (also re­ferred to as mem­ory or re­call re­sponses) typ­i­cally oc­cur much more quickly and with greater ef­fi­ciency re­gard­ing their qual­i­ta­tive char­ac­ter­is­tics. As a re­ sult, sec­ond­ary re­sponses ef­fec­tively in­hibit mi­crobes from es­tab­lish­ing them­selves in a host and thereby pre­vent in­fec­tions.
The phe­nom­e­non of im­mu­no­logic mem­ory un­der­lies the con­cept of vac­ci­na­tion. The vac­cine stim­u­lates a pri­mary re­sponse on the part of the re­cip­i­ent that in­cludes the gen­er­a­tion of a path­o­gen-specific pop­u­la­tion of mem­ory B and/or T cells. Subsequent nat­u­ral ex­po­sures to the path­o­gen elicit mem­ory re­sponses, which, at least in prin­ci­ple, thwart in­fec­tion. The fi­nal hall­mark of adap­tive im­mu­nity is the abil­ity of B and T cells to spe­cif­i­ cally re­act against unique for­eign chal­lenges (i.e., mi­cro­bial path­o­gens, tu­ mor cells, and in­sect/reptile venom, amongst oth­ers) while main­tain­ing a state of nonreactivity against host mol­e­cules. This in­volves a va­ri­ety of mech­a­nisms act­ing on B or T cells, in ad­di­tion to the anergic pro­cess dis­ cussed above, ex­press­ing self-reactive an­ti­gen re­cep­tors that col­lec­tively re­ sult in a state of im­mu­no­logic tol­er­ance to­wards self tissues. For many years, it was be­lieved that the dif­fer­ences be­tween adap­tive and in­nate forms of im­mu­nity were un­am­big­u­ously de­fined by the five char­ac­ter­is­tics at­trib­uted to hu­moral and cell-me­di­ated im­mune re­sponses. As dis­cussed through­out­ this chap­ter, how­ever, the prop­er­ties that dis­tin­guish adap­tive and in­nate im­ mu­nity from one an­other are not as clear as pre­vi­ously thought.

Properties of Innate Immunity
The fea­tures of in­nate im­mu­nity are of­ten de­scribed in con­trast­ing terms with those of adap­tive im­mu­nity. For in­stance, in­nate im­mune re­sponses al­ways form the first line of de­fense, whereas adap­tive im­mune re­sponses de­velop later. Another strik­ing dif­fer­ence in­volves the na­ture of the re­cep­ tors in­volved. Innate im­mune re­cep­tors are en­coded in the germ line and thus have lim­ited di­ver­sity, whereas adap­tive im­mune re­cep­tors are gen­er­ ated ran­domly by gene re­com­bi­na­tion, thus cre­at­ing an un­lim­ited re­cep­tor

Recent ad­vances, how­ever, show that this is not en­tirely true. Although in­nate im­mune cells lack the ex­qui­site an­ti­gen spec­i­fic­ity, clonality, and lon­gev­ity of adap­tive lym­pho­cytes, they can be trained to “re­mem­ber” fol­low­ing ex­po­sure to in­fec­tion or vac­ci­na­tion. Des­ ignated trained im­mu­nity, this ca­pac­ity en­ables in­nate cells (e.g., mono­ cytes, mac­ro­phages, and NK cells) to dis­play en­hanced re­spon­sive­ness af­ter sec­ond­ary stim­u­la­tion with mi­cro­bial path­o­gens, which can thereby be elim­i­nated more read­ily by in­nate im­mune means. The mo­lec­u­lar ba­sis of trained im­mu­nity ap­pears to in­volve sus­tained changes in the tran­scrip­ tional pro­grams of in­nate im­mune cells, known as epi­ge­netic re­pro­gram­ ming (as op­posed to per­ma­nent ge­netic changes ow­ing to mu­ta­tions and re­com­bi­na­tion). Although in­nate im­mune mem­ory is shorter lived and less spe­cific than clas­si­cal adap­tive im­mune mem­ory, it can con­trib­ute to ef­fi­cient con­trol of in­fec­tions.

K E Y  P O I N T S

The im­mune sys­tem has pri­mar­ily evolved to dis­crim­i­nate be­tween safe and dan­ger­ous rather than be­tween self and non­self.
Immunity aims at a bal­anced state, termed ho­meo­sta­sis. In ho­meo­static im­mu­nity, host de­fenses and in­flam­ma­tion are ad­e­quate to fight and re­solve in­fec­tion (or other types of in­sults in­clud­ing ster­ile in­jury) while main­tain­ing tol­er­ance to pre­vent au­to­im­mu­nity and al­lergy or de­struc­tive re­sponses to sym­bi­otic mi­cro­bial com­mu­ni­ties at mu­co­sal bar­rier sites.

Adaptive im­mune cells (B and T lym­pho­cytes) can rec­og­nize vir­tu­ally any pos­si­ble an­ti­gen and are ac­ti­vated if in­nate im­mu­nity fails to con­trol an in­fec­tion or other types of in­sults.
However, in­nate im­mu­nity is not sim­ply an ini­tial ob­sta­cle to in­fec­tion and does ex­hibit a mo­di­cum of spec­i­fic­ity. It can dis­tin­guish be­tween dif­fer­ent clas­ses of mi­cro­or­gan­isms as well as de­tect host cell dam­age and other types of dan­ger. Innate im­mune mech­a­nisms can there­fore in­form adap­tive im­mu­nity about the na­ture of the en­coun­tered an­ti­gens and de­ter­mine whether an adap­tive im­mune re­sponse is war­ranted. If lym­pho­ cytes are in­structed that there is no dan­ger, they be­come tol­er­ant to the rec­og­nized tar­get.

Innate im­mune cells use germ line-encoded re­cep­tors called pat­tern rec­og­ni­tion re­cep­tors (PRRs) to rec­og­nize con­served struc­tural fea­tures of mi­crobes, termed mi­crobe-associated mo­lec­u­lar pat­terns (MAMPs), that are shared by dis­tinct clas­ses of mi­crobes. PRRs also rec­og­nize en­dog­e­nous mol­e­cules that sig­nify cell dam­age or death, termed dam­age-associated mo­lec­u­lar pat­terns (DAMPs). In con­trast, in­di­vid­ual B and T cells each ex­press mul­ti­ple cop­ies of a unique an­ti­gen re­cep­tor that en­able the cells to dis­tin­guish fine struc­tural dif­fer­ences, even be­tween dif­fer­ent parts of the same an­ti­gen. The ex­qui­site spec­i­fic­ity of adap­tive im­mune re­sponses is a re­sult of a com­plex se­ries of mo­lec­u­lar ge­netic events through which seg­ments of
DNA are re­ar­ranged dur­ing de­vel­op­ment to gen­er­ate an ar­ray of re­cep­tors that col­lec­tively have the po­ten­tial to in­ter­act with any an­ti­gen.

An ef­fec­tive im­mune re­sponse to ini­tial chal­lenge with a spe­cific mi­cro­bial path­o­gen ne­ces­si­tates in­ter­ac­tions be­tween the var­i­ous mol­e­cules and cells of in­nate and adap­tive im­mu­nity. Innate im­mune cells are cen­tral to the in­duc­tion of an­ti­gen-specific adap­tive im­mune re­sponses within sec­ond­ary lym­phoid tis­sues.

The im­mune sys­tem has evolved tai­lored ef­fec­tor func­tions to op­ti­mally re­spond to dis­tinct types of threats. Broadly speak­ing, there are three key types of in­nate and adap­tive cell-me­di­ated im­mu­ni­ty.
•	 Type 1 re­sponses are shaped mainly by the pro­duc­tion of in­ter­leu­kin (IL)-12 by DCs (also mac­ro­phages) and lead to the ac­ti­va­tion of NK cells and ILC1s, fol­lowed by the ac­ti­va­tion of Th1 cells and CD8+ CTLs. The main mo­lec­u­lar ef­fec­tors of type 1 re­sponses in­clude in­ter­feron (IFN)-γ, which ac­ti­vates in­tra­cel­lu­ lar kill­ing in mac­ro­phages, and cy­to­toxic mol­e­cules such as perforin. Type 1 re­sponses can be ef­fec­tive against in­tra­cel­lu­lar path­o­gens (e.g., vi­ruses and in­tra­cel­lu­lar bac­te­ria) and tu­mors.

•	 Type 2 re­sponses are shaped by the pro­duc­tion of IL-25, IL-33, and thy­mic stro­mal lymphopoietin (TSLP) by nonhematopoietic cells, lead­ing to the ac­ti­va­tion of ILC2s and eo­sin­o­phils, fol­lowed by the de­vel­op­ment of Th2 cells and IgE-positive B cells. The main mo­lec­u­lar ef­fec­tors are IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, which ac­ti­vate mast cells, ba­so­phils, and eo­sin­o­phils. Type 2 re­sponses can pro­tect against large ex­tra­cel­lu­lar par­a­sites (such as hel­minths) and ven­oms. Amphiregulin (AREG) is also pro­duced as part of a type 2 re­sponse and in­duces tis­sue re­pair af­ter in­fec­tion.
•	 Type 3 re­sponses are shaped by the pro­duc­tion of IL-1β and IL-23 by DCs and mac­ro­phages, which lead to the ac­ti­va­tion of ILC3s and Th17 cells. The main mo­lec­u­lar ef­fec­tors of type 3 re­sponses are IL-17 and IL-22. IL-17 or­ches­trates the re­cruit­ment of neu­tro­phils and IL-22 in­duces pro­duc­tion of an­ti­mi­cro­bial pep­tides and main­tains ep­i­the­lial bar­rier func­tion. Type 3 re­sponses can be ef­fec­tive against ex­tra­cel­lu­lar mi­cro­or­gan­isms (such as ex­tra­cel­lu­lar bac­te­ria and fun­gi).

Given that the func­tional scope of the im­mune sys­tem ex­tends be­yond the erad­i­ca­tion of path­o­gens (it also main­tains tol­er­ance to self and sym­bi­otic mi­cro­bial com­mu­ni­ties at bar­rier sites, per­forms tis­sue re­pair, and clears dam­aged or ap­o­pto­tic cells), it is more ac­cu­rate to view the im­mune sys­tem as a force that pro­motes ho­meo­sta­sis rather than kill­ing.
