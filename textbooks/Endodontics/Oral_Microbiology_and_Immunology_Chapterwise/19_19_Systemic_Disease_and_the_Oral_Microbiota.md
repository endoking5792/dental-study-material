## Systemic Disease

## and the Oral Microbiota

INTRODUCTION
The oral microbiota com­prises spe­cies that span the con­tin­uum from strict com­men­sal (with­out­ harm­ing the host) to path­o­genic. The path­o­gens are best known for their abil­i­ties to cause in­fec­tions that are nor­mally lim­ited to the oral cav­ity, in­clud­ing den­tal car­ies and peri­odon­ti­tis, the oral ve­sic­ u­lar le­sions caused by cer­tain hu­man her­pes­vi­ruses (such as her­pes sim­ plex vi­rus type 1 [HSV-1]), and the ero­sive mu­co­sal le­sions caused by the fun­gus Candida albicans. However, when these mi­cro­or­gan­isms or their com­po­nents en­ter the cir­cu­la­tion or con­nec­tive tis­sues, they may in­crease the risk of sys­temic dis­ease. A strong causal as­so­ci­a­tion has been sug­gested for oral bac­te­ria in sys­temic dis­eases, in­clud­ing in­fec­tive en­do­car­di­tis (IE) and dis­sem­i­nated in­tra­vas­cu­lar co­ag­u­la­tion (DIC). Oral mi­cro­or­gan­isms can cause as­pi­ra­tion pneu­mo­nia, sep­tic shock, and brain ab­scesses. All are life-threatening dis­eases. Oral bac­te­ria have also been as­so­ci­ated with path­o­genic mech­a­nisms in ne­phri­tis, rheu­ma­toid ar­thri­tis, and Behçet’s dis­ease (blood ves­sel in­flam­ma­tion, or vas­cu­li­tis). As shown in some ep­i­ de­mi­o­log­i­cal stud­ies, and sup­ported by an­i­mal mod­els, oral in­fec­tions such as peri­odon­tal dis­eases have been as­so­ci­ated with the oc­cur­rence of such di­verse con­di­tions as ath­ero­scle­ro­sis and pre­term low-birth-weight in­fants. Human pap­il­lo­ma­vi­rus (HPV), trans­mit­ted by sex­ual ac­tiv­i­ties, has be­come rec­og­nized as a ma­jor eti­o­log­i­cal agent in head and neck can­ cer, an ex­am­ple of lo­cal in­fec­tion caus­ing re­gional and sys­temic dis­ease of high mor­bid­ity and mor­tal­ity. Similarly, other oral vi­ruses, such as hu­man her­pes­vi­rus 8 (HHV-8), may be caus­a­tive of HIV-1- and im­mu­no­sup­pres­ sion-associated can­cers (i.e., Kaposi’s sar­coma), while HIV-1 can be trans­ mit­ted through breached oral mu­cosa to cause AIDS. Based upon mo­lec­u­lar, an­i­mal, and hu­man stud­ies, IE serves as an ex­cel­lent proof-of-principle model to il­lus­trate the en­try of oral mi­crobes into sys­temic com­part­ments, sub­se­quent dis­sem­i­na­tion, man­i­fes­ta­tions of vir­u­lence, and host re­sponses.
Understanding other sys­temic in­fec­tions of oral or­i­gin must re­flect the vir­ u­lence char­ac­ter­is­tics of the caus­a­tive microbiota. This chap­ter ex­plores the plausibility of the un­der­ly­ing bi­o­log­i­cal idea that oral mi­crobes may con­ trib­ute more gen­er­ally to sys­temic dis­ease.

ROUTES FROM ORAL TO SYSTEMIC COMPARTMENTS

Breaches in the Oral Mucosa
Oral mi­crobes re­side in sa­liva and den­tal plaque and on and within the oral mu­cous mem­branes and ep­i­the­lium of the gin­gi­val crev­ice. Oral mi­cro­ or­gan­isms en­ter sys­temic com­part­ments when a breach is cre­ated by den­tal pro­ce­dures or in­ju­ries, frank trauma, or in­fec­tions of the oral soft tis­sues. In healthy in­di­vid­u­als, for ex­am­ple, cer­tain mi­crobes from den­tal plaque may in­vade the gin­gi­val ep­i­the­lium and pro­mote ul­cer­a­tive le­sions in the gin­gi­ val crevicular ep­i­the­lium. Similarly, ul­cer­a­tive le­sions form on the mu­cous mem­branes dur­ing her­pes­vi­rus in­fec­tions in oth­er­wise healthy peo­ple or in as­so­ci­a­tion with un­re­mark­able in­fec­tious agents in im­mu­no­com­pro­mised in­di­vid­u­als. Through these breaches in mu­co­sal in­teg­rity, oral mi­crobes en­ter the blood, re­sult­ing in polymicrobial bac­ter­emias, fungemias, or vi­re­mias.

Transport and Translocation of Microbes
Oral mi­crobes can also en­ter the sys­temic com­part­ment when pro­cessed and trans­ported by the mu­co­sal im­mune sys­tem. The mu­co­sal im­mune sys­tem is char­ac­ter­ized by sur­veil­lance and pro­tec­tive mech­a­nisms in sa­ liva, mu­co­sal ep­i­the­lial cells, den­dritic cell lin­e­ages, B and T cells, and other lym­phoid el­e­ments that protect against in­gested en­vi­ron­men­tal path­o­gens.
Ingested oral pathogens and commensal microorganisms also travel to the gut, where interactions with the intestinal mucosa are a normal activity.
Oral mi­crobes are con­tin­u­ously swal­lowed at 105 cells per ml of sa­liva per min­ute and en­ter the gut, where par­tic­u­late and sol­u­ble an­ti­gens are pro­ cessed in the or­ga­nized lym­phoid tis­sues or Peyer’s patches. Mucosal im­ mune pro­cess­ing can stim­u­late or sup­press a sys­temic an­ti­gen-specific im­mune re­sponse. Proximal to the Peyer’s patches, the spe­cial­ized ep­i­the­lial
M cells can trans­lo­cate an­ti­gens and whole bac­te­ria from the in­tes­ti­nal lu­ men to phago­cytic cells on the sys­temic side for im­mu­no­log­i­cal pro­cess­ing.
Some fea­tures of the Peyer’s patches and M cells are found in the oro­pha­ ryn­geal ton­sils, whereas the oral mu­cosa largely lacks or­ga­nized lym­phoid tis­sues. The con­tig­u­ous and more ex­ter­nal oral mu­co­sae, how­ever, rely on sens­ing by ep­i­the­lial and den­dritic cells to re­spond to sur­face bac­te­ria and sol­u­ble an­ti­gens and sig­nal­ing for pro­tec­tion through in­nate and adap­tive im­mune mech­a­nisms. In the oral cav­ity, trans­lo­ca­tion of mi­crobes would oc­cur pri­mar­ily though breaches in the ep­i­the­lium. The mu­co­sal im­mune sys­tem there­fore fa­cil­i­tates en­try of whole or pro­cessed mi­crobes into the sys­temic com­part­ment as part of a mech­a­nism to fos­ter sys­temic de­fense.

THE POTENTIAL OF COMMENSAL BACTERIA
TO BEHAVE AS PATHOGENS
Hundreds of spe­cies of bac­te­ria re­side in the oral cav­ity. Most are be­nign, caus­ing no known dis­ease in the mouth and sur­round­ing tis­sues and co­ex­ist­ing peace­fully with the host as com­men­sals. Indeed, co­evo­lu­tion be­tween harm­ less com­men­sal mi­cro­or­gan­isms and the host may pro­vide pro­tec­tive ac­ tiv­ity, lim­it­ing in­tru­sions of harm­ful en­vi­ron­men­tal path­o­gens into the oral mi­cro­bial com­mu­nity. However, oral bac­te­ria en­ter­ing the blood and other sys­temic com­part­ments pos­sess a com­ple­ment of genes that are suf­ fi­cient to en­gen­der path­o­ge­nic­ity un­der per­mis­sive con­di­tions. In some cases, ex­pres­sion of en­vi­ron­men­tally reg­u­lated genes may in­crease path­o­genic po­ten­tial or cause a harm­less com­men­sal to be­have as a path­o­ gen. The ge­netic pro­gram that al­lows an oth­er­wise com­men­sal bac­te­rium to sur­vive in a for­eign en­vi­ron­ment may have the “un­in­tend­ed” con­se­ quence of pro­mot­ing virulence and pathogenicity.
Conventional wis­dom holds that most blood­stream mi­crobes orig­i­ nat­ing from oral foci are cleared un­event­fully by the re­tic­u­lo­en­do­the­lial sys­tem, sug­gest­ing that their vir­u­lence in sys­temic com­part­ments is in­suf­ fi­cient to over­come the an­ti­mi­cro­bial de­fenses of a healthy host. To sur­ vive in a stress­ful nonoral en­vi­ron­ment, the oral bac­te­ria typ­i­cally lo­cate and col­o­nize a rel­a­tively hos­pi­ta­ble niche in an ab­nor­mal or in­jured an­a­ tomic site or an im­mu­no­com­pro­mised host. Consequently, oral mi­cro­ or­gan­isms of­ten cause sys­temic op­por­tu­nis­tic in­fec­tions by in­fect­ing ab­nor­mal an­a­tomic sites, such as dam­aged heart valves, or by avoid­ing im­mune rec­og­ni­tion and clear­ance in im­mu­no­de­fi­cient in­di­vid­u­als. Hence, the as­so­ci­a­tion of oral microbiota with sys­temic dis­eases of­ten re­quires the co­in­ci­dence of tran­sient bac­ter­emias and un­der­ly­ing an­a­tom­i­cal ab­nor­ mal­i­ties and im­mu­no­de­fi­ciency. Oral mi­cro­bial in­fec­tions of healthy cells and tis­sues in the sys­temic com­part­ment could also cause sub­clin­i­cal pa­thol­ogy or su­per­im­pose sec­ond­ar­ily on other sub­clin­i­cal dis­ease. Oral bac­te­ria, such as the Streptococcus spe­cies S. gordonii, S. san­gui­nis, S. oralis, S. mutans, and S. salivarius, have been iso­lated from nonoral an­a­tomic lo­ca­tions in sys­temic in­fec­tions. Oral strep­to­cocci can also serve as ac­ces­sory path­o­ gens, in­creas­ing the vir­u­lence of known path­o­gens with which they can in­ter­act in het­ero­typic mi­cro­bial com­mu­ni­ties. Given these sce­nar­ios, oral bac­te­ria can use sev­eral mech­a­nisms to be­come vir­u­lent and cause dis­ease upon in­fec­tion of sys­temic or­gans and tis­sues.

Microbial Chameleons: Changing Gene Expression in Response to Environmental Signals
The oral en­vi­ron­ment en­coun­ters both sub­tle and pow­er­ful threats to the mi­cro­bial niche. What we eat, drink, or use to brush our teeth all­ chal­lenge the sta­bil­ity of oral mi­cro­bial com­mu­ni­ties. To adapt to a chang­ing eco­ log­i­cal niche, bac­te­ria have evolved the ca­pac­ity to mod­ify the pro­files of ex­pressed genes. Environmentally reg­u­lated genes are mod­u­lated by in­tra­ cel­lu­lar sig­nal­ing path­ways that are ac­ti­vated when bac­te­ria sense en­vi­ ron­men­tal sig­nals via biochemically spe­cific re­cep­tors. Altered ex­pres­sion of some genes may in­crease the vir­u­lence and path­o­ge­nic­ity of bac­te­ria. Gene ex­pres­sion in oral bac­te­ria is reg­u­lated by many fac­tors, in­clud­ing changes

For ex­am­ple, most sites in the mouth are slightly acidic (pH ∼6.5), and in den­tal plaque the pH can dip be­low 5.0. However, in blood and other tis­sue flu­ids, the pH is ∼7.4. Other genes reg­u­ lated by changes in en­vi­ron­men­tal pH may also change oral bac­te­rial vir­u­lence. When the pH in­creases from 6.2 to 7.3, S. gordonii upregulates ex­pres­sion of msrA, which en­codes the en­zyme me­thi­o­nine sulf­ox­ide re­duc­ tase. Methionine sulf­ox­ide re­duc­tase pro­tects or­gan­isms against ox­i­da­tive dam­age and has po­ten­tial roles in bac­te­rial mul­ti­pli­ca­tion, stress re­sis­tance, and sur­vival. Hydrogen per­ox­ide pro­duced by com­pet­ing bac­te­ria, or host cells such as plate­lets at sites of in­tra­vas­cu­lar in­jury, will re­duce the com­pet­ i­tive­ness of sen­si­tive or­gan­isms that are not ex­press­ing pro­tec­tive, an­ti­ox­i­ dant fac­tors. In the tran­si­tion to a more al­ka­line en­vi­ron­ment, the oral strep­to­cocci also change the spec­trum of pro­te­ases they pro­duce. As the pH rises from 5.5 to 7.5, oral strep­to­cocci in­crease ex­pres­sion of throm­bin-like ac­tiv­ity. Since throm­bin pro­motes po­ly­mer­i­za­tion of fi­brin­o­gen into fi­brin and trig­gers plate­let ag­gre­ga­tion, oral strep­to­cocci ex­posed to an al­ka­line shift in pH may be­come in­creas­ingly thrombogenic. The thrombogenicity of cer­tain oral bac­te­ria may shield the bac­te­ria from phago­cytes and fa­cil­i­tate in­fec­tion and le­sion for­ma­tion on dam­aged heart valves dur­ing IE.
Still other en­vi­ron­men­tal fac­tors change bac­te­rial gene ex­pres­sion.
For ex­am­ple, when en­do­the­lium be­comes dam­aged, ex­tra­cel­lu­lar ma­trix (ECM) pro­teins be­come avail­able for oral strep­to­cocci to ad­here and re­ spond to. When ex­posed to the ECM pro­teins lam­i­nin and col­la­gen, such as on a dam­aged heart valve, oral strep­to­cocci ex­press new or mod­i­fied pro­teins and pro­tein­ases. Differential gene ex­pres­sion can be an­tic­i­pated to be wide­spread among the oral bac­te­ria. For ex­am­ple, the an­aer­o­bic peri­odon­tal path­o­gen Porphyromonas gingivalis reg­u­lates ex­ pres­sion of its ma­jor fim­brial adhesin in re­sponse to tem­per­a­ture. More­ over, the mi­cro­or­gan­ism reg­u­lates ex­pres­sion (both up and down) of al­most
500 pro­teins when ex­posed to host ep­i­the­lial cells. As we learn to model en­vi­ron­men­tally spe­cific stress­ors and iden­tify spe­cific and glob­ally reg­u­ lated genes, we may be ­able to ex­plain bet­ter how oral mi­crobes be­have as en­dog­e­nous path­o­gens.

HOST DEFENSES
Microorganisms have evolved many mech­a­nisms to adapt to chang­ing en­ vi­ron­men­tal con­di­tions, but the host is not de­fense­less. Since the oral cav­ity sus­tains large in­fec­tious and an­ti­genic loads, the mu­cous mem­branes have evolved mech­a­nisms to dis­tin­guish be­tween path­o­genic or com­men­sal mi­cro­or­gan­isms and food mol­e­cules. The squa­mous ep­i­the­lial cells and res­i­ dent intraepithelial and lam­ina pro­pria im­mune cells form a so­phis­ti­cated sys­tem of path­o­gen rec­og­ni­tion, bar­rier pro­tec­tion, and im­mune re­sponse.
When mi­cro­or­gan­isms en­ter the mouth, they en­coun­ter a num­ber of non­ spe­cific de­fense mech­a­nisms that help pre­vent them from bind­ing to and pen­e­trat­ing the oral mu­cosa (see Chapters 2, 3, and 10). Saliva has a flush­ ing ef­fect, phys­i­cally re­mov­ing mi­crobes to the gut by swal­low­ing. Saliva also con­tains a sup­ply of non­im­mune and im­mune fac­tors, in­clud­ing mu­cin gly­co­pro­teins, sal­i­vary ag­glu­ti­nin, and spe­cific se­cre­tory IgA an­ti­bod­ies, which bind and clear bac­te­ria more ef­fi­ciently via swal­low­ing.

Antimicrobial pep­tides such as histatins, ly­so­zyme, and lactoferrin are abun­dant in sa­liva. Other an­ti­mi­cro­bial pep­tides are re­leased into the sa­ liva by ep­i­the­lial cells and neu­tro­phils. The ep­i­the­lium- and neu­tro­philderived an­ti­mi­cro­bial pep­tides are pres­ent in higher con­cen­tra­tions on the mu­co­sal sur­faces, where they may be most ac­tive. Examples of these in­nate im­mune mol­e­cules in­clude the defensins, which pro­tect against harm­ful col­o­ni­za­tion by a wide va­ri­ety of mi­cro­or­gan­isms. In the oral cav­ity, kera­ tinocytes also ex­press an­ti­mi­cro­bial pro­teins such as calprotectin and cat­ helicidin (LL37), which ap­pear to pro­tect the oro­pha­ryn­geal mu­cosa from in­va­sion by path­o­gens such as C. albicans and P. gingivalis.
The mu­co­sal ep­i­the­lium also pro­tects the un­der­ly­ing con­nec­tive tis­ sues against mi­cro­bial en­try by form­ing a tight and strong phys­i­cal bar­ rier. The ker­a­tins cornify cells as they ma­ture, mak­ing an abra­sion-resistant ep­i­the­lium, and the su­per­fi­cial mu­co­sal ep­i­the­lial cells are shed by des­ qua­ma­tion and re­placed by cells from un­der­ly­ing lay­ers. Bathed in an­ti­ mi­cro­bial sa­liva, these cells also ex­press in­trin­sic in­nate an­ti­mi­cro­bial mech­a­nisms, in­clud­ing the pro­duc­tion of an­ti­mi­cro­bial pro­teins/peptides (as above), to pro­tect against in­vad­ing bac­te­ria and fungi. Nonetheless, the su­per­fi­cial keratinocytes con­tain oral bac­te­ria that have in­vaded the cells. By shed­ding the ma­ture su­per­fi­cial cells, the mu­cosa lim­its col­o­ni­za­ tion and in­va­sion by path­o­genic mi­cro­or­gan­isms.
In part to re­sist mi­cro­bial pen­e­tra­tion into spaces be­tween the ep­i­the­lial cells and into the con­nec­tive tis­sues, mu­co­sal ep­i­the­lial cells form spe­cial­ ized in­ter­cel­lu­lar at­tach­ments and junc­tions. These at­tach­ments and junc­ tions also pre­vent the pas­sage of wa­ter, sol­utes, and sur­face mi­crobes be­tween the oral cav­ity and in­te­rior tis­sue com­part­ments. Healthy mu­co­ sal ep­i­the­lium tends to pro­tect against in­ter­cel­lu­lar pas­sage and transcytosis of mi­crobes, lim­it­ing the abil­ity of in­va­sive path­o­gens to pen­e­trate deeper into the tis­sue.
When in­fec­tious agents pen­e­trate the mu­co­sal sur­face and in­vade, the ep­i­the­lial cells se­crete proinflammatory cy­to­kines such as in­ter­leu­kin 1 (IL-1), IL-6, IL-8, and mac­ro­phage chemoattractant pro­tein 1 (MCP-1/
CCL-2). These cy­to­kines re­cruit phago­cytic cells to the site of bac­te­rial in­va­sion, and IL-1α upregulates the pro­duc­tion of calprotectin by neigh­ bor­ing ep­i­the­lial cells, aug­ment­ing re­sis­tance to mi­cro­bial in­va­sion. Blood mono­cytes, neu­tro­phils, and tis­sue mac­ro­phages in­ter­nal­ize (phago­cy­ tose), kill, and di­gest whole mi­cro­or­gan­isms, pro­tect­ing the un­der­ly­ing tis­sues by cre­at­ing a phago­cytic bar­rier against fur­ther mi­cro­bial in­va­sion.
Dendritic cells and Lang­er­hans cells also in­ter­nal­ize bac­te­rial cells, break­ ing down the com­po­nent pro­teins into dis­crete an­ti­gens for pre­sen­ta­tion to T cells and stim­u­la­tion of an adap­tive im­mune re­sponse. Collectively, these phago­cytes of the in­nate im­mune sys­tem re­act to a wide va­ri­ety of path­o­gens with­out­ re­quir­ing prior sen­si­ti­za­tion.
How do mu­co­sal keratinocytes rec­og­nize in­fec­tious agents? Microbes ex­press cer­tain highly con­served mo­lec­u­lar struc­tures that are dis­tinct from self (the host). These path­o­gen-associated mo­lec­u­lar pat­terns (PAMPs) are rec­og­nized by the in­nate im­mune sys­tem. For ex­am­ple, the gen­eral struc­ ture of li­po­po­ly­sac­cha­ride (LPS) is com­mon to all­ Gram-negative bac­te­ria.
Recognition of LPS by a host cell re­cep­tor would al­low any Gram-negative bac­te­rium to be de­tected. In ad­di­tion to LPS, com­mon PAMPs in­clude pep­ti­ do­gly­can, LTA, man­nans, bac­te­rial DNA, dou­ble-stranded RNA, and glu­cans.

Infective Endocarditis
IE is per­haps the best-known ex­am­ple of a life-threatening sys­temic in­ fec­tion that can be caused by oral bac­te­ria. More than 15,000 new cases are di­ag­nosed an­nu­ally in the United States. IE is al­most al­ways a dis­ease of in­jured or ab­nor­mal heart valves, which are typ­i­cally asymp­tom­atic un­til blood-borne in­fec­tion oc­curs. The oral strep­to­cocci, Staphylococ­ cus au­re­us, and Enterococcus faecalis are the most com­mon eti­o­log­i­cal agents.
There seem to be two main mech­a­nisms for de­vel­op­ment of IE. One is ini­ti­ated by mi­cro­bial cell at­tach­ment to dam­aged en­do­car­dium. Dam­ age could be the re­sult of chronic rheu­matic heart dis­ease or pre­vi­ous IE, ab­er­rant blood flow across a con­gen­i­tal de­fect, or in­ser­tion of a pros­thetic valve. The dam­age ex­poses un­der­ly­ing en­do­the­lial com­po­nents (e.g., ECM pro­teins and stro­mal cells), and these in turn trig­ger the de­po­si­tion of ster­ ile fi­brin-platelet clots. Bacteria in­tro­duced into the blood­stream through a tran­sient bac­ter­emia may then ad­here to clot con­stit­u­ents, at­tract­ing and ac­ti­vat­ing mono­cytes, which bind and ac­ti­vate tis­sue fac­tor to ini­ti­ate the co­ag­u­la­tion cas­cade. Activation of the co­ag­u­la­tion cas­cade fur­ther pro­ motes in­cor­po­ra­tion and ac­ti­va­tion of plate­lets into the grow­ing sep­tic veg­ e­ta­tion (or throm­bus). Several dif­fer­ent spe­cies of oral strep­to­cocci have been found to pro­duce pro­teins, such as ser­ine-rich pro­teins (men­tioned above), that in­ter­act di­rectly with plate­lets. Atherosclerosis
Atherosclerosis is the chief pre­cur­sor to car­dio­vas­cu­lar dis­ease (CVD) and is char­ac­ter­ized by the ac­cu­mu­la­tion of lip­ids and fi­brous el­e­ments within me­dium and large ar­ter­ies. The first step in the for­ma­tion of an ath­er­oma is ac­ti­va­tion of the en­do­the­lial cells that line the ar­te­rial lu­men. Upon ac­ti­ va­tion, these cells upregulate ex­pres­sion of sev­eral sur­face pro­teins, such as vas­cu­lar cell ad­he­sion mol­e­cule-1 (VCAM-1), which bind pass­ing mono­ cytes and T lym­pho­cytes, re­cruit­ing them to the ar­te­rial wall. These in­flam­ ma­tory cells pass be­tween en­do­the­lial cells (termed di­a­pe­de­sis) into the un­der­ly­ing tu­nica in­tima layer. Here, mac­ro­phage col­o­ny-stimulating fac­ tor (M-CSF) in­duces mono­cytes to trans­form into lip­id-rich foam cells.
The foam cells de­fine the ini­tial ath­ero­scle­rotic le­sion, form­ing a so-called
“fatty streak.” Under in­flam­ma­tory con­di­tions, the le­sion can ex­pand and de­velop into a more com­plex le­sion (clin­i­cal ath­ero­scle­ro­sis). Enzyme ac­ tiv­ity, by prin­ci­pally ma­trix metalloproteinases, breaks down the plaque (ath­er­oma) and rup­tures the fi­brous cap. The dis­rup­tion per­mits bleed­ing from the ves­sel wall, which leads to throm­bo­sis and block­age of the cor­o­nary ar­tery. This can cause is­che­mia (re­stric­tion of blood sup­ply to an or­gan) and, when pro­longed, a heart at­tack (myo­car­dial in­farc­tion).
There is good ev­i­dence that oral bac­te­ria are ­able to ini­ti­ate or pro­mote pro­gres­sion of ath­ero­scle­ro­sis. Epidemiological stud­ies have sug­gested a cor­re­la­tion be­tween the in­ci­dence of peri­odon­ti­tis and CVD, and cor­re­la­ tions have been found be­tween the pres­ence of se­rum an­ti­bod­ies to peri­ odon­tal path­o­gens and the oc­cur­rence of CVD. Several re­ports have also dem­on­strated the pres­ence of DNA from peri­odon­tal path­o­gens within hu­ man ath­ero­scle­rotic plaques. Oral bac­te­ria as­so­ci­ated most fre­quently with ath­er­o­mas in­clude Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Trepo­ nema denticola, Prevotella intermedia, and P. gingivalis. Adverse Pregnancy Outcomes
Epidemiological and in­ter­ven­tional stud­ies have im­pli­cated poor peri­ odon­tal sta­tus in ad­verse preg­nancy out­comes, in­clud­ing pre­term de­liv­ery, low birth weight, mis­car­riage, and pre­eclamp­sia. Rheumatoid Arthritis
Rheumatoid ar­thri­tis (RA) is a sys­temic au­to­im­mune dis­ease re­sult­ing from loss of self-tol­er­ance, chronic in­flam­ma­tion, and de­bil­i­tat­ing joint de­struc­tion. A con­di­tion in which bone loss is driven by dysregulated lo­cal in­flam­ma­tion, RA shares path­o­phys­i­o­log­i­cal fea­tures with peri­odon­tal dis­ease. Indeed, sev­eral ep­i­de­mi­o­log­i­cal stud­ies have re­ported that oc­cur­ rence of these two dis­eases is as­so­ci­ated. Patients with RA dis­play an in­ creased in­ci­dence of peri­odon­tal dis­ease rel­a­tive to con­trol sub­jects and vice versa, even when ad­just­ing for po­ten­tial con­found­ing fac­tors such as smok­ing. Likewise, pa­tients with RA have greater ti­ters of IgG di­rected against P. gingivalis than do healthy con­trols. Such stud­ies do not dem­on­ strate com­mon cau­sal­ity of peri­odon­tal dis­ease and RA. Nonetheless, po­ ten­tial mech­a­nisms by which peri­odon­tal dis­ease could pro­mote RA have been pro­posed, with a fo­cus on P. gingivalis and anticitrullinated pro­tein an­ti­bod­ies (ACPAs).
ACPAs are pres­ent in the se­rum of 70% of RA pa­tients and tar­get citrullinated pro­teins (i.e., pro­teins that con­tain the un­usual amino acid cit­rul­line). ACPAs can be de­tected sev­eral years be­fore the on­set of symp­ tom­atic dis­ease, thus serv­ing as a use­ful di­ag­nos­tic marker for RA. AntiACPA ti­ters cor­re­late strongly with dis­ease se­ver­ity. Citrullinated pro­teins are syn­the­sized by hu­man peptidyl-arginine deiminase (PAD). PAD sub­ sti­tutes an amine res­i­due for an ox­y­gen group on the amino acid ar­gi­nine to form cit­rul­line. In ge­net­i­cally sus­cep­ti­ble in­di­vid­u­als, how­ever, citrul­ linated pro­teins such as fi­brin­o­gen or α-enolase may serve as autoanti­ gens. These autoantigens stim­u­late B cells to pro­duce ACPAs. ACPAs then bind citrullinated pro­teins to form im­mune com­plexes, which in­duce lo­ cal in­flam­ma­tion within the joints. P. gingivalis ex­presses a peptidyl-arginine deiminase known as PPAD.

Diabetes
Diabetes mel­li­tus is a col­lec­tive term for a group of dis­or­ders af­fect­ing the me­tab­o­lism of car­bo­hy­drates, lip­ids, and pro­teins and is char­ac­ter­ized by el­e­vated blood glu­cose lev­els. Its in­ci­dence is rap­idly in­creas­ing in as­so­ci­ a­tion with ris­ing obe­sity rates. Type 1 di­a­be­tes is typ­i­cally found in chil­ dren and ad­o­les­cents and re­sults from au­to­im­mune de­struc­tion of pan­cre­atic is­let cells, lead­ing to to­tal loss of in­su­lin se­cre­tion. Type 2 di­a­ be­tes is the pre­dom­i­nant form and is com­monly seen in adults, al­though its prev­a­lence in youn­ger in­di­vid­u­als is in­creas­ing in con­junc­tion with child­hood obe­sity. This form arises from im­paired uti­li­za­tion of en­dog­e­ nously pro­duced in­su­lin (in­su­lin re­sis­tance), to­gether with the in­abil­ity of pan­cre­atic β cells to se­crete suf­fi­cient in­su­lin to com­pen­sate.
Individuals with di­a­be­tes are more likely to suf­fer se­vere peri­odon­tal dis­ease than those with­out­, while di­a­bet­ics with peri­odon­tal dis­ease were shown to be at a 6-fold higher risk of poor gly­ce­mic con­trol than those with­out­. It has been pro­posed that these ef­fects are re­lated to the abil­ity of peri­odon­tal path­o­gens to per­pet­u­ate a sys­temic in­flam­ma­tory state. In­ sulin re­sis­tance in type 2 di­a­be­tes is as­so­ci­ated with high se­rum lev­els of
C-reactive protein (CRP), IL-6, and fi­brin­o­gen. Elevated se­rum lev­els of these mol­e­cules are also seen in in­di­vid­u­als suf­fer­ing peri­odon­ti­tis. Cyto­ kines TNF, IL-6, and IL-1β are known in­su­lin an­tag­o­nists, all­ of which play ma­jor roles in the path­o­gen­e­sis of peri­odon­ti­tis. Taken col­lec­tively, these data im­ply that, through the chronic re­lease of proinflammatory me­di­a­tors into the blood­stream, peri­odon­tal dis­ease is ­able to sig­nif­i­cantly pro­mote the de­vel­op­ment of in­su­lin re­sis­tance and type 2 di­a­be­tes.

Obesity
Several stud­ies have iden­ti­fied obe­sity as a risk fac­tor for peri­odon­ti­tis. As in di­a­be­tes, the mech­a­nisms un­der­ly­ing this ef­fect are thought to be as­so­ci­ ated with an al­tered sys­temic in­flam­ma­tory re­sponse. The health care com­ mu­nity also now ap­pre­ci­ates that the hu­man gut microbiota may in­flu­ence the prev­a­lence of obe­sity. Changes in the gut microbiota can af­fect the over­all met­a­bolic ac­tiv­ity and im­pact an in­di­vid­u­al’s abil­ity to ex­tract en­ ergy (cal­o­ries) from the diet. The gut microbiota may also pro­mote obe­sity through chronic low-grade stim­u­la­tion of sys­temic in­flam­ma­tion, lead­ing to a sub­se­quent in­crease in host in­su­lin re­sis­tance and weight gain. The oral microbiota may also be as­so­ci­ated with the gut microbiota and obe­sity. The com­po­si­tion of the oral microbiota al­ters in par­al­lel with an in­di­vid­u­al’s tran­si­tion from lean to obese. Whether this is in re­sponse to the chang­ing host en­vi­ron­ment or rep­re­sents a causal re­la­tion­ship re­mains to be de­fined.

Certain Cancers
Solid tu­mors are in­creas­ingly ap­pre­ci­ated to be af­fected by the in­ter­sti­tial in­flam­ma­tory re­sponse pro­vided by the tu­mor-infiltrating lym­phoid cells (TILs). When tu­mors con­tain a dense pop­u­la­tion of TILs, the prog­no­sis of the pa­tient is gen­er­ally bet­ter than when the in­fil­trat­ing lym­phoid cells are sparse. Cells in the sparse in­fil­trate are un­able to launch a ro­bust an­ti­tu­mor im­mune re­sponse. Dense pop­u­la­tions of TILs can re­spond bet­ter to tu­mor an­ti­gens and con­trol the de­vel­op­ing tu­mor. So why is the TIL pop­u­la­tion dif­fer­ent in sim­i­lar tu­mors from dif­fer­ent pa­tients? Tumor cells can ex­press mark­ers such as PD-L1, which en­gage PD-1 re­cep­tors and ac­ti­vate im­mu­no­ sup­pres­sive T reg­u­la­tory cells (Tregs). Immunosuppressive my­e­loid-derived sup­pres­sor cells can also be stim­u­lated to dif­fer­en­ti­ate from gran­u­lo­cyte pre­cur­sors, cre­at­ing ad­di­tional loss of TIL an­ti­tu­mor ca­pa­bil­i­ty.
Solid tu­mors, such as in head and neck can­cer and co­lo­rec­tal can­cer, are now known to ac­quire unique microbiomes. The tu­mor microbiomes can mod­u­late TIL ac­tiv­ity, and in­ter­est­ingly oral mi­crobes can be prom­i­ nent. For ex­am­ple, P. gingivalis can be a prom­i­nent mem­ber of the micro­ biome in head and neck tu­mors, whereas F. nucleatum is fre­quently iden­ti­fied in co­lo­rec­tal tu­mors. How these bac­te­ria travel from the oral cav­ity to dis­tal tu­mor sites is not clear, but sev­eral path­ways should be con­sid­ered. When the over­ly­ing ep­i­the­lium is ne­crotic, mi­crobes can in­ fil­trate the tu­mor di­rectly from the lo­cal en­vi­ron­ment. Fusobacterium, pos­si­bly pass­ing through the gas­tro­in­tes­ti­nal (GI) tract from its na­tive res­ er­voir in the oral cav­ity, can be iden­ti­fied in the co­lonic microbiota and in­cor­po­rates in the co­lo­rec­tal tu­mor microbiome. Oral bac­te­ria can also spread through the blood and/or the lym­phatic sys­tem to solid tu­mors.
Given that tu­mors show dif­fer­ent microbiomes, travel by these sys­temic routes sug­gests the pres­ence of an un­known traf­fick­ing and tar­get­ing sys­ tem so that cer­tain oral bac­te­ria translocate to spe­cific tu­mors. These tu­mor microbiomes are likely to be proinflammatory and en­hance the an­ti­ tu­mor re­sponses of the TILs.
Nonetheless, it re­mains un­clear whether the Fusobacterium-rich co­ lonic flora is caus­a­tive or a by­stander. Clearly the char­ac­ter of the lo­cal microbiome can drive the im­mune re­sponse against the can­cer, yet spe­cific mem­bers of the com­mu­nity may col­o­nize only af­ter a tu­mor de­vel­ops be­ cause the en­vi­ron­ment be­comes more fa­vor­able. Similarly, P. gingivalis is found in esoph­a­geal can­cer tis­sues and the prev­a­lence of in­fec­tion ap­pears to cor­re­late with can­cer pro­gres­sion and poorer over­all pa­tient sur­vival.
In vi­tro data show that P. gingivalis can af­fect host ep­i­the­lial sur­vival and pro­lif­er­a­tion, sug­gest­ing a bi­o­log­i­cally plau­si­ble mech­a­nism for a caus­a­ tive role; how­ever, the mat­ter re­quires fur­ther study. Conversely, prod­ucts from microbiomes may also be pro­tec­tive in can­cers, in­clud­ing com­mon
GI tu­mors. Clearly, how­ever, the oral microbiome may reg­u­late oc­cur­rence or se­ver­ity of solid can­cers lo­cally and in dis­tal sites, rep­re­sent­ing an im­ por­tant fron­tier for re­search into host-pathogen in­ter­ac­tions.

Heat Shock Proteins
Molecular mim­icry is among the most in­trigu­ing mech­a­nisms whereby com­men­sal bac­te­ria may con­trib­ute to sys­temic dis­ease. Bacteria, fungi, and mam­ma­lian hosts ex­press the heat shock pro­tein (HSP) fam­i­lies. These pro­teins are highly con­served, show­ing nearly iden­ti­cal pri­mary amino acid se­quences across spe­cies. In hu­mans, some HSPs are cryp­tic and thus hid­den from view of the adap­tive im­mune sys­tem. For ex­am­ple, HSP60 re­sides in mi­to­chon­dria and is nor­mally shielded from host im­mune sur­veil­ lance. Consequently, the mam­ma­lian im­mune sys­tem con­sid­ers HSP60 to be for­eign. Upon ex­po­sure to HSP60, an im­mune re­sponse is ini­ti­ated. Mi­ crobes that ex­press HSP60 ho­mo­logues may there­fore trig­ger an au­to­im­mune re­sponse upon in­fec­tion.
HSPs are found in mi­cro­or­gan­isms and host cells dur­ing nor­mal growth. Cellular ex­pres­sion in­creases in re­sponse to heat shock and other stress­ors. HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90 are the most sig­nif­i­cant HSP fam­i­lies.
During heat shock and other stresses, newly syn­the­sized pro­teins tend to un­fold, com­plex, and be­come in­sol­u­ble. After pro­tein syn­the­sis and trans­lo­ca­tion of pro­teins into in­tra­cel­lu­lar com­part­ments, HSPs bind or chap­er­one new un­folded or par­tially folded pro­teins. The oral mi­cro­bial en­vi­ron­ment can also in­ter­fere with the adap­tive im­mune re­sponse by ac­ti­vat­ing a large num­ber of T cells in­de­pen­dent of their spec­i­fic­i­ties. Superantigens sub­vert the or­dered T cell re­sponse by non­spe­cif­i­cally ac­ti­vat­ing T cells. The lymph nodes clog with the high den­sity of pro­lif­er­at­ing T cells, which lack the spec­i­fic­i­ties needed to di­rect B cells to­ward an ef­fi­cient an­ti­body re­sponse.
The large ul­cer­a­tive le­sions seen in the oral mu­cosa of pa­tients with Be­ hçet’s dis­ease ap­pear to re­sult from lo­cal po­ly­clonal ac­ti­va­tion of T cells by oral strep­to­coc­cal superantigen-like mol­e­cules. Some non­spe­cif­i­cally ac­ti­vated T cells ac­ti­vate spe­cific B cells. When superantigens ac­ti­vate T cells that are spe­cific for lo­cal self antigens or HSPs, the ac­ti­vated T helper cells can di­rect the pro­duc­tion of au­to­an­ti­bod­ies and cy­to­toxic T cells. If di­rected against HSPs, au­to­an­ti­bod­ies and cy­to­toxic T cells may de­stroy
HSP-expressing ep­i­the­lial cells. The pres­ence of bac­te­rial superantigens may aug­ment the self-di­rected im­mune re­sponse to de­stroy the ep­i­the­lial layer, cre­at­ing the pain­ful ul­cer seen in the oral cav­ity of pa­tients with Be­ hçet’s dis­ease.

Inflammation: A Link Between Local Dental Disease and Systemic Pathology?
Periodontal dis­ease is char­ac­ter­ized by re­peated ep­i­sodes of in­flam­ma­tion as­so­ci­ated with the pres­ence in the peri­odon­tal pocket of a wide range of mi­cro­or­gan­isms (e.g., P. gingivalis, Tannerella for­syth­ia, A. actinomycetem­ comitans, T. denticola, Filifactor alocis, Dialister pneumosintes, Mega­ sphaera spp., and Desulfobulbus spp.). Many of the bac­te­ria as­so­ci­ated with peri­odon­tal dis­ease are Gram-negative, and LPS can dif­fuse into ul­ cer­ated gin­gi­val tis­sue in the gin­gi­val sul­cus or peri­odon­tal pocket. Mac­ rophages are among the early im­mune cells that re­act to this lo­cal pres­ence of bac­te­ria. LPS is an es­pe­cially po­tent ac­ti­va­tor of mono­cytes/macrophages, which re­spond by se­cret­ing sol­u­ble me­di­a­tors of in­flam­ma­tion such as
TNF and IL-1β. When ac­ti­vated, mac­ro­phages also re­lease PGE2, an ar­a­ chi­donic acid me­tab­o­lite, which pro­motes va­so­di­la­tion of the lo­cal mi­ cro­vas­cu­la­ture, add­ing to the in­flam­ma­tion. Locally, IL-1β and TNF in­crease cel­lu­lar ad­he­sion mol­e­cule ex­pres­sion by en­do­the­lial cells, which fa­cil­i­tates bind­ing of neu­tro­phils to the ves­sel wall, a pro­cess re­quired for their ex­trav­a­sa­tion into the af­fected tis­sues. When lo­cal con­cen­tra­tions of
IL-1β and TNF are high, these sol­u­ble me­di­a­tors can en­ter the cir­cu­la­tory sys­tem. IL-1β and TNF in­duce the liver to pro­duce acute re­ac­tants, in­clud­ ing CRP (Fig. 4). Since CRP is not pro­duced in the gin­giva, its pres­ence in the gin­gi­val crevicular fluid is a con­ve­nient marker of endovascular or sys­ temic in­flam­ma­tion.

SUMMARY
Oral in­fec­tions have come un­der scru­tiny as pos­si­ble risk fac­tors for sys­ temic con­di­tions such as car­dio­vas­cu­lar dis­ease, rheu­ma­toid ar­thri­tis, and pre­term low-birth-weight in­fants. Several ep­i­de­mi­o­log­i­cal stud­ies sug­gest that there is an as­so­ci­a­tion be­tween den­tal health and car­dio­vas­cu­lar dis­ ease. Specific path­o­gens in den­tal plaque, how­ever, are more strongly as­ so­ci­ated with sub­clin­i­cal ath­ero­scle­ro­sis. People with poorer den­tal health are more likely to have car­dio­vas­cu­lar dis­ease. This as­so­ci­a­tion is ap­par­ ent in both ret­ro­spec­tive and case-con­trol stud­ies and is spe­cific for peri­ odon­tal dis­ease, not den­tal car­ies. Although ep­i­de­mi­o­log­i­cal ev­i­dence can be con­founded by the pres­ence of sim­i­lar risk fac­tors for peri­odon­ti­tis and car­dio­vas­cu­lar dis­ease, the strength of ev­i­dence in sup­port of a re­la­tion­ ship is in­creased by find­ings of causal re­la­tion­ships in an­i­mal mod­els. Similar ep­i­de­mi­o­log­i­cal ev­i­dence also ex­ists for an as­so­ci­a­tion be­tween peri­odon­tal dis­ease and pre­term de­liv­ery of low-birth-weight in­fants, and peri­odon­tal bac­te­ria have been iden­ti­fied in ges­ta­tional tis­sues in these cases. Likewise, stud­ies have shown as­so­ci­a­tions be­tween rheu­ma­toid ar­thri­tis or var­i­ous can­cers and periodontopathogens. A grow­ing body of ev­i­dence im­plies that the ca­pac­ity for these mi­crobes to mod­u­late the host im­mune re­sponse un­ der­pins the mech­a­nis­tic ba­sis for such cor­re­la­tions.

Oral bac­te­ria that are not usu­ally as­so­ci­ated with oral dis­eases can nev­er­the­less be­come path­o­genic when they gain ac­cess to en­vi­ron­ments other than the oral cav­ity. A breach in the in­teg­rity of the soft tis­sues of the oral cav­ity can be caused by den­tal pro­ce­dures, in­ju­ries, or in­fec­tions. Once in the blood (bac­ter­emia) and tis­sues, the new en­vi­ron­men­tal con­di­tions that are found sys­tem­i­cally in­duce changes in bac­te­rial gene ex­pres­sion, which may in­crease vir­u­lence.

Oral strep­to­cocci are of­ten caus­a­tive agents in IE. In the sys­temic com­part­ment, the strep­to­cocci lo­cate on dam­aged or de­vel­op­men­ tally ab­nor­mal heart valves. The val­vu­lar en­do­the­lium can then slough, leav­ing the un­der­ly­ing con­nec­tive tis­sues ex­posed.
Circulating plate­lets are ac­ti­vated to re­pair the wound, and the plate­lets ad­here to and spread on the ex­posed con­nec­tive tis­sues.
The strep­to­cocci then in­duce the plate­lets to bind fi­brin­o­gen, ag­gre­gate, and form a throm­bus or plate­let clot. The mass of mi­cro­or­gan­isms, plate­lets, and fi­brin on dis­eased or in­jured heart valves, or other en­do­car­dial sur­faces, con­sti­tutes a veg­e­ta­tion, which is char­ac­ter­is­tic of IE.

Local in­tra­vas­cu­lar in­flam­ma­tion caused by oral bac­te­ria may be im­pli­cated in the pro­gres­sion of sys­temic dis­eases such as ath­ero­scle­ro­sis and ad­verse preg­nancy out­comes. Periodontal bac­te­ria such as P. gingivalis may con­trib­ute more di­rectly to ath­ero­scle­ro­sis by in­vad­ing ar­tery en­do­the­lial cells and in­duc­ing in­flam­ma­tion. P. gingivalis can also in­duce the for­ma­tion of lip­id-filled foam cells from mac­ro­phages, a key step in ath­ero­scle­ ro­sis. Spread of peri­odon­tal bac­te­ria such as F. nucleatum and
P. gingivalis to ges­ta­tional tis­sues may dis­rupt lo­cal ho­meo­sta­sis and lead to pre­term de­liv­ery of low-birth-weight in­fants, whereas their con­tri­bu­tion to the tu­mor microbiome may af­fect the path­o­gen­e­sis of se­lected can­cers.

Cross-reactivity of an­ti­bod­ies to bac­te­rial HSPs with self HSPs may con­trib­ute to sys­temic dis­eases such as ath­ero­scle­ro­sis and
Behçet’s dis­ease. Similarly, P. gingivalis may trig­ger pro­duc­tion of
ACPAs, which in­duce an au­to­im­mune re­sponse within joints to ex­ac­er­bate rheu­ma­toid ar­thri­tis. Periodontal bac­te­ria may also mod­u­late the be­hav­ior of TILs and the pro­gres­sion of solid tu­mors.
