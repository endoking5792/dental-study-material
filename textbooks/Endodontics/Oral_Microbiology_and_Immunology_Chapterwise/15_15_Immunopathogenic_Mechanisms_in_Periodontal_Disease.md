INTRODUCTION
Gingivitis and peri­odon­ti­tis are the two most fre­quent forms of peri­odon­ tal dis­ease (Fig. 1A) and af­fect dis­tinct com­po­nents of the periodontium, i.e., the tis­sues that sur­round and sup­port the teeth (gin­giva, peri­odon­tal lig­a­ment, ce­men­tum, and al­ve­o­lar bone). In gin­gi­vi­tis, the in­flam­ma­tory pro­cess is re­vers­ible and is lim­ited to the gin­gi­val ep­i­the­lium and con­nec­tive tis­sue. In con­trast, peri­odon­ti­tis is char­ac­ter­ized by an immunoinflamma­ tory in­fil­trate of the deeper com­part­ments of the periodontium, re­sult­ing in loss of gin­gi­val tis­sue at­tach­ment to the tooth, deep­en­ing of the gin­gi­val crev­ice (called here the peri­odon­tal pock­et), and de­struc­tion of the peri­ odon­tal lig­a­ment and al­ve­o­lar bone. In the ab­sence of ap­pro­pri­ate ther­ apy, this de­struc­tive pro­cess can lead to tooth loss. Although a dense in­fil­trate of in­flam­ma­tory my­e­loid cells and lym­pho­cytes is in­vari­ably as­ so­ci­ated with gin­gi­vi­tis, this con­di­tion can re­main sta­ble with­out­ nec­es­ sar­ily pro­gress­ing to peri­odon­ti­tis, which ap­par­ently re­quires a sus­cep­ti­ble host.

Bacteria that col­o­nize the teeth be­low the gin­gi­val mar­gin are im­pli­ cated in peri­odon­tal dis­ease, al­though their per­ceived roles and mech­a­ nisms have been a mat­ter of de­bate and dif­fer­ent the­o­ries have been pro­posed over the years. Recent hu­man microbiome an­a­ly­ses and mech­a­ nis­tic stud­ies in rel­e­vant pre­clin­i­cal mod­els in­di­cate that peri­odon­ti­tis is not, strictly speak­ing, a bac­te­rial in­fec­tion. In the clas­si­cal sense of the term, in­fec­tions are caused by spe­cific ex­og­e­nous path­o­gens and con­form, broadly, to Koch’s pos­tu­lates. Periodontitis, on the other hand, is not caused by a sin­gle or even a se­lect few bac­te­rial spe­cies (tra­di­tion­ally known as periopathogens, the path­o­genic role of which could not be con­firmed as per
Koch’s pos­tu­lates). Rather, the mi­cro­bial eti­ol­ogy of peri­odon­ti­tis en­tails syn­er­gis­tic in­ter­ac­tions be­tween dif­fer­ent in­dig­e­nous spe­cies with dis­tinct roles in a dysbiotic mi­cro­bial com­mu­nity. Dysbiosis rep­re­sents an al­ter­ ation in the abun­dance or dy­nam­ics of in­di­vid­ual spe­cies within a poly­ microbial com­mu­nity (rel­a­tive to their abun­dance or in­flu­ence in health) lead­ing to dysregulated host-microbial in­ter­ac­tions and de­struc­tive in­flam­ ma­tion. IMMUNE PLAYERS IN PERIODONTAL DISEASE
The pre­dom­i­nant hab­i­tat of peri­odon­ti­tis-associated bac­te­ria is the peri­ odon­tal pocket, where the or­gan­isms are found in dis­tinct mi­cro­en­vi­ ronments: the tooth-associated biofilm, the fluid phase (GCF), and the ep­i­the­lium lin­ing the pocket. The bac­te­ria do not have to in­vade the un­der­ ly­ing con­nec­tive tis­sue to ac­ti­vate im­mune mech­a­nisms for re­cruit­ment of in­nate and adap­tive im­mune cells to the gin­giva. This is be­cause bac­te­ria re­lease immunostimulatory prod­ucts (e.g., li­po­po­ly­sac­cha­ride), of­ten pack­ aged in outer mem­brane ves­i­cles, that can read­ily pen­e­trate through the gin­gi­val junc­tional ep­i­the­lium. This bar­rier is ac­tu­ally highly po­rous, as the junc­tional ep­i­the­lial cells are in­ter­con­nec­ted by only a few des­mo­somes and oc­ca­sional gap junc­tions. Moreover, subgingival bac­te­ria can ac­ti­vate the gin­gi­val crevicular ep­i­the­lial cells, which can re­spond by re­leas­ing an­ti­ micro­bial pep­tides and che­mo­kines that, in turn, re­cruit neu­tro­phils to the gin­gi­val crev­ice or peri­odon­tal pocket. Overall, the subgingival en­vi­ron­ ment is re­plete with im­mune and in­flam­ma­tory me­di­a­tors. These in­clude cy­to­kines, che­mo­kines, an­ti­mi­cro­bial pep­tides, com­ple­ment pro­teins, and an­ti­bod­ies, which are pro­duced lo­cally by res­i­dent or re­cruited cells or are even de­rived from plasma ex­u­date into the pock­ets. This im­mu­no­log­ i­cally rich en­vi­ron­ment pro­vi­des chal­lenges but also op­por­tu­ni­ties for the res­i­dent bac­te­ria (see above and Fig. 1 and Fig. 2).
Periodontal health is char­ac­ter­ized by a con­trolled immunoinflam­ matory state that main­tains host-microbe ho­meo­sta­sis, thereby pre­vent­ ing de­vel­op­ment of clin­i­cally ev­i­dent dis­ease (for de­tails, see Chapter
10). However, as dis­cussed above, the im­mune re­sponse can be­come dys­ regulated, lead­ing to de­struc­tive in­flam­ma­tion while fail­ing to con­trol bac­te­rial out­growth. This dysregulation may oc­cur ei­ther be­cause the

The Page and Schroeder model of the 1970s re­mains fun­da­men­tally valid, al­though the sub­se­quent dis­sec­tion of cross talk in­ter­ac­tions be­tween in­nate and adap­tive leu­ko­cytes has of­fered a more nu­anced and mech­a­ nis­tic un­der­stand­ing of peri­odon­tal dis­ease path­o­gen­e­sis with pro­found im­pli­ca­tions for treat­ment (see Chapter 21). The neu­tro­phils, pro­tag­o­nists of the ini­tial le­sion and tra­di­tion­ally re­garded as merely an­ti­mi­cro­bial cells in acute in­fec­tions, are now ap­pre­ci­ated for their func­tional ver­sa­til­ity and crit­i­cal roles in chronic in­flam­ma­tion. Also, the role of com­ple­ment was un­cer­tain in the early 1970s, al­though it was sus­pected to play a role in the ini­tial le­sion. It is now well ac­cepted that com­ple­ment plays a key role in many as­pects of peri­odon­tal dis­ease path­o­gen­e­sis. Moreover, it is now firmly es­tab­lished that T lym­pho­cytes (as well as in­nate im­mune cell types) ex­ist in func­tion­ally spe­cial­ized sub­sets (see Chapter 2) with dif­fer­ent or even con­flict­ing roles in im­mu­nity and in­flam­ma­tion. Also well ap­pre­ci­ ated is that lo­cal tis­sues have a “reg­u­la­tory say” over the host in­flam­ma­ tory re­sponse through sev­eral mech­a­nisms, in­clud­ing lo­cal pro­duc­tion of ho­meo­static mol­e­cules, the ab­sence (or scar­city) of which can po­ten­tially pre­cip­i­tate or ex­ac­er­bate peri­odon­ti­tis. Finally, an en­tirely new field of re­search, osteoimmunology, has de­vel­oped, which stud­ies the ef­fects of immunoinflammatory events on the cells that me­di­ate bone re­sorp­tion (os­te­o­clasts), thus be­ing of cen­tral im­por­tance in peri­odon­ti­tis.

COMPLEMENT: MICROBIAL MANIPULATION
AND DESTRUCTIVE INFLAMMATION
Complement has been tra­di­tion­ally viewed as an an­ti­mi­cro­bial en­zyme sys­ tem op­er­at­ing in tis­sues, se­rum, and in­flam­ma­tory ex­u­dates, such as the
GCF, al­though now it is rec­og­nized as a key sys­tem in im­mu­nity and in­ flam­ma­tion (see de­tails in Chapter 10). Whereas com­ple­ment may con­trib­ ute to host-microbe ho­meo­sta­sis in clin­i­cally healthy periodontium, it ap­pears to be dysregulated in peri­odon­ti­tis, where it can ac­tu­ally con­trib­ute to tis­sue de­struc­tion.
Studies with sev­eral peri­odon­ti­tis-associated bac­te­ria in­di­cate that they in­ter­act with com­ple­ment in com­plex ways that in­clude both in­hib­i­ tory and stim­u­la­tory ef­fects. NEUTROPHILS: MORE THAN JUST ACUTE RESPONDERS
As a re­sult of im­mune stim­u­la­tion by the subgingival microbiota, neu­tro­ phils exit the gin­gi­val plexus of the postcapillary ve­nules and are che­mo­ tac­ti­cally re­cruited to the gin­gi­val crev­ice or peri­odon­tal pocket via the junc­tional ep­i­the­lium, which, un­der in­flam­ma­tory con­di­tions, is largely oc­cu­pied by tran­sit­ing neu­tro­phils. Although trans­mi­grat­ing neu­tro­phils ini­tially fol­low the che­mo­kine gra­di­ent de­pos­ited by the en­do­the­lium, they then have to move to­wards a gra­di­ent ex­ist­ing in the in­fected or in­flamed tis­sue. Such gra­di­ents may ad­di­tion­ally in­volve chemoattractants de­rived from bac­te­ria (N-formyl-methionyl-leucyl-phenylalanine) or from lo­cal com­ple­ment ac­ti­va­tion (C5a frag­ment).
Neutrophils make up the ma­jor­ity of leu­ko­cytes (≥95%) in the gin­gi­ val crev­ice, where they form a “de­fense wall” against the bac­te­ria, pre­sum­ ably to block their ac­cess to the un­der­ly­ing con­nec­tive tis­sue. Although there is per­sis­tent and heavy re­cruit­ment of neu­tro­phils to the peri­odon­tal pocket, they fail to con­trol a dysbiotic mi­cro­bial com­mu­nity de­spite their ca­pac­ity to elicit im­mune and in­flam­ma­tory re­sponses. This im­plies that peri­odon­ti­tis-associated bac­te­ria can largely es­cape neu­tro­phil-mediated kill­ing in an in­flam­ma­tory en­vi­ron­ment, which, as dis­cussed above, pro­vi­ des the bac­te­ria with pre­cious nu­tri­ents de­rived from in­flam­ma­tory tis­sue break­down. Because in­flam­ma­tion is im­por­tant for the per­sis­tence of peri­odon­ti­tis-associated bac­te­rial com­mu­ni­ties, bac­te­ria have de­vel­oped mech­a­nisms whereby they can un­cou­ple neu­tro­phil-mediated bac­te­rial clear­ance from neu­tro­phil-mediated in­flam­ma­tion. This con­cept is ex­em­ pli­fied by P. gingivalis, a peri­odon­tal key­stone path­o­gen that, as al­luded to above, se­lec­tively sup­presses the kill­ing and phago­cytic ca­pac­ity of hu­ man neu­tro­phils while pro­mot­ing their in­flam­ma­tory re­sponses (Fig. 5).

Given that nonresolving in­flam­ma­tion un­der­lies the path­o­gen­e­sis of many chronic dis­eases, in­clud­ing peri­odon­ti­tis, the abil­ity of the host to re­solve the in­flam­ma­tory re­sponse in a timely man­ner be­comes crit­i­cal.
The suc­cess­ful res­o­lu­tion of in­flam­ma­tion in­volves a dis­tinct se­ries of pro­ cesses, in­clud­ing downregulation of proinflammatory me­di­a­tors, ter­mi­na­ tion or sup­pres­sion of neu­tro­phil re­cruit­ment, clear­ance of ap­o­pto­tic neu­tro­phils through efferocytosis, and tis­sue re­pair. Efficient efferocytosis by mac­ro­phages not only pre­vents sec­ond­ary ne­cro­sis and in­flam­ma­tion but also re­pro­grams the tran­scrip­tional ac­tiv­ity of the re­solv­ing mac­ro­ phage, which then in­creases the ex­pres­sion of anti-inflammatory and reg­ u­la­tory cy­to­kines, such as TGF-β and IL-10. Although lim­ited ev­i­dence from an­i­mal mod­els sug­gests that M1-like mac­ro­phages rep­re­sent a sub­set that is as­so­ci­ated with peri­odon­tal dis­ease path­o­gen­e­sis, there are cur­rently no clin­i­cal stud­ies on the as­so­ci­a­tion of dif­fer­ent mac­ro­phage sub­sets with peri­odon­tal in­flam­ma­tion or its res­o­lu­tion.

ADAPTIVE IMMUNITY: MORE HARM THAN GOOD?
The fail­ure of the in­nate im­mune re­sponse to con­trol the subgingival mi­ cro­bial com­mu­nity can lead to re­cruit­ment and pro­lif­er­a­tion of T cells and
B cells, and the de­vel­op­ment of a B cell/plasma cell le­sion that char­ac­ter­ izes the es­tab­lished and ad­vanced stages of hu­man chronic peri­odon­ti­tis.
The ad­vanced le­sion is not re­stricted to the area around the gin­gi­val sul­cus but ex­tends both api­cally and lat­er­ally. The no­tion that the adap­tive im­ mune re­sponse in hu­man peri­odon­ti­tis is po­ten­tially de­struc­tive is read­ily sup­ported by stud­ies in an­i­mal mod­els. Indeed, se­vere com­bined im­mu­no­ de­fi­cient mice, which lack both T and B cells and can­not mount adap­tive im­mune re­sponses, de­velop re­duced P. gingivalis-induced bone loss com­ pared to im­mu­no­com­pe­tent mice. In the same model, MHC class II-re­ stricted CD4+ T cell-deficient mice ex­hibit re­duced P. gingivalis-induced bone loss as com­pared to mice de­fi­cient in MHC class I-restricted CD8+ T cells or nor­mal con­trols. Collectively, these stud­ies sug­gest in­volve­ment of
CD4+ T cells in peri­odon­tal tis­sue de­struc­tion. Specific im­pli­ca­tion of B cells is sup­ported by stud­ies show­ing that mice lack­ing B cells de­velop less bone loss than wild-type con­trols in mod­els of peri­odon­ti­tis in­duced by P.
gingivalis in­oc­u­la­tion or by place­ment of lig­a­tures around mo­lar teeth.
The struc­ture of the gin­gi­val sul­cus is con­du­cive to the de­vel­op­ment of adap­tive im­mu­nity, even in the ab­sence of bac­te­rial in­va­sion of the gin­gi­val con­nec­tive tis­sue. Bacterial an­ti­gens pen­e­trat­ing the highly per­me­able and nonkeratinized junc­tional ep­i­the­lium are cap­tured by an­ti­gen-presenting cells, such as den­dritic cells, which pa­trol the lam­ina pro­pria of the gin­gi­val con­nec­tive tis­sue. Upon cap­tur­ing an­ti­gen, den­dritic cells mi­grate to the re­ gional lymph nodes, where they pres­ent pro­cessed an­ti­genic pep­tides and costimulatory sig­nals to naïve an­ti­gen-specific CD4+ T helper cells, thereby reg­u­lat­ing their ac­ti­va­tion and dif­fer­en­ti­a­tion into dif­fer­ent sub­sets. Acti­ vated T cells of the helper sub­sets (i.e., CD4+ T cells) can then pro­vide help to an­ti­gen-specific B cells, which can thereby pro­lif­er­ate and dif­fer­ en­ti­ate into plasma cells. However, dur­ing the 1990s and early 2000s, when Th1 and Th2 cells were be­ing in­ten­sively in­ves­ti­gated for their in­volve­ment in peri­odon­ ti­tis, Th2 cells were sus­pected of sup­port­ing de­struc­tive B cell re­sponses in this oral dis­ease. The Th1/Th2 par­a­digm pro­vided a use­ful con­cep­tual frame­ work to un­der­stand T cell in­volve­ment in im­mu­nity and in­flam­ma­tion.
However, dis­eases of im­mu­no­log­i­cal eti­ol­ogy were oc­ca­sion­ally pi­geon­holed into Th1 or Th2 cat­e­go­ries with­out­ ad­e­quate sup­port­ive ev­i­dence. Like­ wise, de­spite over two de­cades of re­search, peri­odon­ti­tis can­not be read­ ily de­scribed in sim­ple Th1 and Th2 di­chot­o­mous terms. Studies in hu­mans and an­i­mal mod­els have de­scribed both pro­tec­tive and de­struc­tive ef­fects as­so­ci­ated with ei­ther sub­set. In some stud­ies, Th1-type cy­to­kines (IFN-γ and IL-12) were neg­a­tively cor­re­lated with peri­odon­tal dis­ease se­ver­ity, os­ten­si­bly at­trib­uted to the abil­ity of these cy­to­kines to pro­mote cell-me­ di­ated im­mu­nity and also in­hibit osteoclastogenesis. Other stud­ies, how­ ever, at­trib­uted de­struc­tive ef­fects to IFN-γ and Th1 cells in peri­odon­ti­tis, con­sis­tent with the abil­ity of ac­ti­vated Th1 cells to ex­press the osteoclas­ togenic fac­tor RANKL. Similarly, in cer­tain stud­ies, the pres­ence of Th2 cells was as­so­ci­ated with pro­tec­tive ef­fects at­trib­uted to the abil­ity of Th2 cells to se­crete IL-4 and IL-13, which can also re­strain osteoclastogenesis (Fig. 4). Although dif­fer­ent types of in­nate and adap­tive im­mune cell types are im­ pli­cated in peri­odon­ti­tis, plasma cells and their pre­cur­sors, B lym­pho­cytes, are dis­tin­guished by their im­pres­sively high abun­dance in ad­vanced peri­ odon­tal le­sions. Moreover, the in­creased pres­ence of plasma cells is as­ so­ci­ated with in­creased col­la­gen break­down and bone loss.
Despite their abun­dance in peri­odon­ti­tis, the pre­cise role of B-lineage cells in the path­o­gen­e­sis of this dis­ease re­mains un­cer­tain, al­though sev­ eral mech­a­nisms are plau­si­ble (Fig. 9). AGING AND THE HOST RESPONSE: INFLAMM-AGING
OF THE PERIODONTIUM
As dis­cussed above, the host in­flam­ma­tory re­sponse in peri­odon­ti­tis is mod­u­lated by a num­ber of fac­tors, in­clud­ing the ge­netic back­ground and immunoinflammatory sta­tus of the in­di­vid­u­als and the pres­ence of en­vi­ ron­men­tal stress­ors and sys­temic dis­ease. Another fac­tor that ap­par­ently in­flu­ences peri­odon­tal dis­ease ac­tiv­ity is ag­ing. In gen­eral, the el­derly ex­ hibit in­creased sus­cep­ti­bil­ity to a num­ber of in­flam­ma­tory, au­to­im­mune, or in­fec­tious dis­eases. The as­so­ci­a­tion of ag­ing with in­creased prev­a­lence and se­ver­ity of peri­odon­ti­tis might sim­ply re­flect the cu­mu­la­tive ef­fect of pro­longed ex­po­sure to the peri­odon­tal mi­cro­bial chal­lenge. However, it is well doc­u­mented that ag­ing per se causes quan­ti­ta­tive and qual­i­ta­tive al­ter­ations to the im­mune and in­flam­ma­tory sta­tus of the host, thereby pre­dis­pos­ing it to greater sus­cep­ti­bil­ity to in­fec­tions, neo­pla­sia, and in­ flam­ma­tory and au­to­im­mune con­di­tions. These age-related al­ter­ations that im­pair the proper func­tion of the im­mune sys­tem are col­lec­tively re­ ferred to as immunosenescence.

K E Y  P O I N T S

Periodontal health rep­re­sents a dy­namic bal­anced state where the proinflammatory and an­ti­mi­cro­bial ac­tiv­i­ties of the host are op­ti­mally reg­u­lated to con­trol the microbiota in the ab­sence of un­war­ranted tis­sue dam­age (ho­meo­static im­mu­nity). Periodontitis arises from the dis­rup­tion of ho­meo­static im­mu­nity, lead­ing to dysbiosis and de­struc­tive in­flam­ma­tion in sus­cep­ti­ble in­di­vid­u­als.

According to the re­cently pro­posed polymicrobial syn­ergy and dysbiosis model of peri­odon­tal dis­ease path­o­gen­e­sis, the peri­odon­ tal host re­sponse is ini­tially sub­verted by key­stone path­o­gens, the col­o­ni­za­tion and met­a­bolic ac­tiv­i­ties of which are as­sis­ted by ac­ces­ sory path­o­gens, and is sub­se­quently overactivated by pathobionts, lead­ing to de­struc­tive in­flam­ma­tion in sus­cep­ti­ble hosts.

A poorly con­trolled host im­mune and in­flam­ma­tory re­sponse is not only in­ef­fec­tive at re­strain­ing bac­te­rial out­growth, it is de­struc­tive to the peri­odon­tal tis­sues. Moreover, the de­struc­tive in­flam­ma­tory re­sponse is ex­ploited by peri­odon­ti­tis-associated bac­te­ria to ob­tain nu­tri­ents (tis­sue break­down prod­ucts).

Neutrophils can con­trib­ute to peri­odon­tal dis­ease path­o­gen­e­sis not only by over­ex­u­ber­ant ac­tiv­ity (by­stander in­jury dogma) but in­trigu­ingly also through their ab­sence from the af­fected tis­sue (as in leu­ko­cyte ad­he­sion de­fi­ciency), where they nor­mally per­form im­por­tant im­mu­no­reg­u­la­tory func­tions.

The his­to­pa­thol­ogy of peri­odon­ti­tis shows that the dis­ease in­cludes el­e­ments of both in­nate and adap­tive im­mu­nity, which form a com­plex net­work of in­ter­ac­tions lead­ing even­tu­ally to in­flam­ma­ tory bone loss. The fi­nal step de­pends largely on a triad of pro­teins con­sist­ing of the re­cep­tor ac­ti­va­tor of nu­clear fac­tor-κB li­gand (RANKL), its func­tional re­cep­tor RANK, and its de­coy re­cep­tor osteoprotegerin (OPG). Osteoclast mat­u­ra­tion and ac­ti­va­tion are pro­moted by the bind­ing of RANKL (ex­pressed pre­dom­i­nantly by ac­ti­vated T cells and B cells in in­flamed gin­giva) to RANK on os­te­o­clast pre­cur­sors, whereas OPG an­tag­o­nizes this pro­cess.

Regarding the role of T helper sub­sets in peri­odon­ti­tis, the dis­ease can­not be ad­e­quately de­scribed in sim­ple Th1 vs. Th2 di­chot­o­mous terms. The re­cent dis­cov­ery of the Th17 sub­set, which is a ded­i­cated osteoclastogenic sub­set, as well as of reg­u­la­tory T cells, which re­strain the ac­tiv­ity of the ef­fec­tor T helper sub­sets, has pro­vided a more nu­anced un­der­stand­ing of the role of T cells in peri­odon­tal dis­ease.

B-lineage cells (plasma cells and their pre­cur­sors, the B lym­pho­ cytes) make up the ma­jor­ity of leu­ko­cytes in the es­tab­lished and ad­vanced le­sions. They ex­press proinflammatory cy­to­kines, ma­trix metalloproteinases, and RANKL and, there­fore, can par­tic­i­pate in the deg­ra­da­tion of both soft and hard tis­sue com­po­nents of the periodontium.
