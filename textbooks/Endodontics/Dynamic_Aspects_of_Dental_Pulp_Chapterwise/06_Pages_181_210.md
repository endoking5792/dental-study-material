10.4 SOME SPECIAL CHARACTERISTICS OF PULPAL
CIRCULATION

The first important characteristic is heterogeneous flow distribution within the pulp. By applying both the 133Xe washout and 15 j.1m microsphere methods on the same tooth the regional blood flow in the pulp can be measured (Kim, 1985). The peripheral layer of the pulp, i.e.
around the pulp-dentine layer, has a flow four times higher than the central area of the pulp. The PBF in the coronal area of the pulp is almost twice that of the root area. In the coronal area of the pulp, the coronal tip has a higher flow than does the remaining coronal area (Meyer and
Path, 1980). Anatomical heterogeneity of the pulp vasculature has been well established using the corrosion resin vascular cast examined under the SEM (Takahashi et al., 1982). Thus, the functional heterogeneity has an anatomical foundation (Kim et al., 1984c).
The second interesting characteristic is arteriovenous shunting. The presence of arteriovenous anastomoses (A V A) in the pulp has been rather well established (Provenza, 1968; Kramer, 1960). Functional shunting can be easily studied using different size microspheres (Meyer and Path, 1979; Kim, 1981). The shunting of 9-j.1m micro spheres, using the 15-j.1m microspheres as non-shunting spheres, for the whole pulp was 33%. The shunting was more prevalent in the apical half (41 %) than the coronal half (25%) of the pulp (Fig. 10.8). The shunting of 8-j.1m spheres was even more drastic than that of 9-j.1m spheres. Thus, type I PBF response is characterized by vasoconstriction and reduction in flow. Since the resting vascular tone of the pulp is relatively low, the type I response may play the most important role in the physiology and pathophysiology of the pulp.

### 10.5.2 Type II

This type of PBF response is illustrated in the centre column in Fig. 10.6.
PBF responds in a biphasic manner to the various known vasodilators, such as isoproterenol (ISO), PG E2 and substance P (SP). ,Agents not included in the figure, which elicited the same response, are bradykinin (BK), papavarine and vasointestinal peptide (VIP). These agents are a category of inflammatory humoral substances, which are known to be powerful vasodilators. For instance, SP causes an immediate, powerful but transient increase in PBF. Within a minute, PBF decreases dramati:
cally and remains depressed for a long time (Kim et al., 1988). This biphasic flow response of pulpal microcirculation to known vasodilators can be explained by several possible mechanisms.
The first likely mechanism advanced by T~mder (1976) is the 'stealing' of perfusion pressure by tissues adjacent to the pulp, such as the gingiva, periodontal ligament and others. It is argued that, since a vasodilator is introduced ia into the maxillary artery some distance from the pulp, it will dilate all the tissues perfused by the maxillary artery including the pulp. The vasodilation of these tissues is significant enough to 'steal' the perfusion pressure, resulting in a net decrease in
PBF.
The second hypothetical mechanism is an increased arteriovenous shunt flow in the pulp microcirculation. The increased shunt flow causes decreased nutrient flow, since blood normally perfusing the capillaries is shunted. For this mechanism, although physiologically possible, there is as yet no experimental evidence.
The third mechanism is an adaptation to the low-compliance environment of the dental pulp. In this low-compliance system of rigid dentine, enamel and cementum structures, tissue expansion in response to vasodilation, for instance, causes changes in other physiological parameters. This is unique and is not observed in high-compliance systems, such as visceral organs. From studies in another low-compliance system, the brain, the Monro-Kellie doctrine was formulated. As in the brain, any rise in intra-organ tissue pressure as a result of vasodilation will be transmitted equally throughout the cavity. In the pulp, therefore, the lowest pressure vein will be affected first, as soon as the tissue pressure exceeds that of the vein, which on· average is 10 mm/Hg. This passive compression of the vein causes the reduction of pulpal blood flow (Fig. 10.12). The administration of the known vasodilawrs, ISO,
PG E2 and SP, causes an immediate but brief increase in PBF, which is followed by a prolonged period of flow reduction lasting longer than 10 minutes (centre column of Fig. 10.10). This hypothetical mechanism is further supported by microcirculatory studies using rat incisor teeth

10.6 CONCLUSION

Regulation of pulpal blood flow in health and disease seems to depend on a number of factors. First, the intrinsic nature of the vessels, such as sympathetic adrenergic receptors and other putative receptors on the vessel walls. Second, behaviour of pulpal vessels, which is not significantly different from other widely studied tissues such as the skeletal muscle, in response to various known vasoactive agents. The significant difference between the pulp and the other systems, however, is the lowcompliance system. In this system the pulp responds to vasoconstriction as other tissues do, but it responds entirely differently to vasodilation.
In the pulp vasodilation results in a sharp, transient increase in PBF followed by a dramatic, sustained decrease. This is not the case in other body systems. The biphasic flow response to vasodilating agents, which are generally inflammatory mediators, may play an important role in the pathophysiology of the pulp during inflammation. While vasodilation in other tissues is a defensive action for survival during inflammation, vasodilation in the pulp, because of its secondary vasoconstriction, more likely leads to the demise of the pulp.
Pathophysiological mechanisms of pulp disorder can be postulated on the basis of available experimental results. Again, the low-compliance system and the compartmentalization of the pulp are significant factors determining the outcome of initial inflammation.
The disease process involving the pulp is based on~lteration of the microcirculatory function caused by microbial, physical, chemical and mechanical insults. Thus, understanding of the fundamentals of the regulation of pulpal haemodynamics is the first step towards further understanding of the pulpal disease process.

In the low-compliant pulp, even modest changes in pulpal interstitial fluid volume (IFV) caused by altered transmicrovascular fluid flow will change interstitial fluid pressure (lFP). For example, a raised capillary pressure with enhanced net capillary filtration will be counteracted by a marked increase in IFP already with a modest increase in pulpal IFV. The low compliance of the pulp tissue is therefore also a favourable qualification allowing a safety factor and oedema-preventing mechanism. The drawback is, however, that a significant increase in IFP may seriously impede pulpal blood flow, both by compression of venules, thus raising venous vascular resistance, and also secondarily by reducing pulpal perfusion pressure.
Contrary to tissues with high compliance (Wiig and Reed, 1985), e.g.
skin and muscle, the IFP in the pulp is therefore an important variable in regard to transmicrovascular fluid regulation and blood flow.

### 11.4 OEDEMA-PREVENTING MECHANISMS

As already realized by Starling in 1896, the filtration and absorptive forces are interdependent. A number of local feedback mechanisms exist that may counteract large variations in interstitial fluid volume a:nd therefore tissue pressure in the pulp. These are the oedema-preventing mechanisms, which are indicated with dotted arrows in Fig. 11.4. These local automatic feedback mechanisms serve to prevent changes in interstitial fluid volume and are therefore inhibiting a rise and spread in tissue pressure.
There is considerable evidence that pulpal inflammation does increase
IFP (van Hassel, 1971; Stenvik et ai., 1972). However, it is also found that the pressure increase is a localized phenomenon that is not conveyed to the apical pulp (van Hassel, 1971, Tonder Heyeraas and
Kvinnsland, 1983). Thus the persistence of a localized increase in IFP without a general spread to the entire pulp chamber strongly indicates the interplay of these local automatic feedback mechanisms which tend to prevent changes in IFV (dotted arrows in Fig. 11.4).
As outlined in the figure, a local increased hydrostatic tissue pressure (IFP) will probably increase lymph flow (Chen et ai., 1976) and also facilitate fluid flow through the inflamed tissue so that net absorption takes place in capillaries in adjacent uninflamed areas (Heyeraas, 1985).
Further, there are two local interstitial feedback mechanisms that automatically counteract increased net capillary filtration:

1. The increased hyrostatic tissue pressure will in itself act as a negative feedback mechanism and counteract further filtration (so-called hydrostatic buffering). Thus, the pulp's inability to expand is favourable. In low-compliant tissues, such as the pulp, a rise in tissue pressure serves as a rapid and efficient oedema-preventing mechanism, quite different from tissues with high compliance (Wiig and
Reed, 1985).
2. Increased net filtration will tend to dilute the interstitial fluid protein concentration and thus reduce interstitial colloid osmotic pressure (so-called osmotic buffering). In tissues with relatively high compliance, such as skin and muscles, it is shown that the latter

In high-compliant tissues, where supplementary dilution takes place, it is calcluated that complete obstruction of lymphatics causes complete equilibrium of protein concentrations within one month (Altamirano et al., 1975). In other words, if protein is not removed from the interstitium, diffusion of proteins across the microvascular wall will continue until the concentration gradient is dissipated. This will result in equilibrium of the Starling forces, and interstitial fluid pressure will rise to the level of microvascular blood pressure, causing ischaemia and necrosis.
It therefore seems reasonable to conclude that removal of protein by lymphatics is the only way of keeping colloid osmotic pressure in tissue fluid lower than blood and thus break the vicious circle.
Our hypothesis is therefore that increased net filtration, leading to increased tissue pressure, will initiate increased volume lymph flow and washout of interstitial proteins. In this way the pulp may cope with the inflammatory induced increased filtration, owing to a new steady state of increased washout of proteins and fluid by the lymphatics. This means that water and protein removal by the lymphatics permits continuous net capillary filtration. In other words, as long as lymph is flowing, the sum of the Starling forces may give a net filtration pressure without leading to increased volume and spread of the increased tissue pressure.

## Concluding remarks

S. KIM

The dental pulp has been a 'black box' from the physiological perspective until recently. The early years of research on the pulp focused on structures, because methods sufficiently sophisticated to examine the dynamics of the pulp had not yet been developed. With advancements in electronics, radioisotope and laser technologies, however, pulp physiologists have slowly but steadily unscrambled the content of the
'black box'. It is generally acknowledged that the greatest advancement in pulp physiology research has occurred in the past decade, and this section on the pulp represents the finest collection of that research by key researchers. We are now in a position to make structural-functional correlations of the pulp in health and disease. A similar linkage must also be made between the microcirculatory and sensory systems of the pulp. The recent discovery of the presence of a host of neuropeptides in the pulp suggest that the two systems are highly interdependent, but a collective investigation on the neuro-vascular link is essential for the full elucidation of the dynamics of the dental pulp.
The chapter by Okabe, Todoki and Ito provides an interesting and important general description of the microcirculation as well as a description of the specific role of pulpal microcirculation in pulpal homeostasis. The reader will come away with a good understanding of the general microcirculation with this chapter. They then categorize the types of haemodynamic regulation using their own research data and offer a hypothetical pathophysiological mechanism of pulpal inflammation. These Jwo chapters reveal that heamodynamic regulation in the pulp is as complex as in other major organs and that an understanding of its regulation requires systematic and innovative approaches.

### 13.1 INTRODUCTION

Many of the vital reactions in cells, such as the synthesis of biological macromolecules, active transport across membranes and the generation of force and movement, are driven by the energy provided by the hydrolysis of ATP. Glucose is the principal food compound of many cells for the ATP production. By a sequence of reactions termed glycolysis, a glucose molecule with six carbon atoms is converted to two pyruvate molecules each with three carbon atoms, and finally two ATP molecules are produced. For most animal cells, glycolysis is only a prelude to subsequent reaction steps that occur in mitochondria: under aerobic conditions, one pyruvate molecule is eventually broken down into three carbon dioxide and three water molecules through the citric acid cycle (tricarboxylic acid cycle or Krebs cycle) and oxidative phosphorylation, yielding far more ATP molecules. Although the generation of ATP through glycolysis is relatively inefficient, it is the most fundamental and important pathway because: (a) it is the predominant and only metabolic pathway from glucose to pyruvate which can then be catabolized in the TeA cycle; (b) it produces ATP even in the absence of oxygen; and (c) it produces intermediates needed for the synthesis of fundamental biological materials such as amino acids and fatq acids.
