### 19.2 METHODS OF IDENTIFICATION OF THE

### CHOLINERGIC SYSTEM

The cholinergic system is widely distributed throughout the animal kingdom in the autonomic and the sensory nervous systems. The preganglionic parasympathetic and sympathetic neurons are both cholinergic (Dale, 1937), while the postganglionic parasympathetic neurons are generally cholinergic as well as a few of the postganglionic sympathetics (Gabella, 1976).

### 19.2.1 Cytochemical methods

The enzyme ChE has been accepted as a 'means of identification of cholinergics and/or adrenergics (implying that some fibres may carry both), and are also widely distributed throughout the animal kingdom.
These enzymes are usually divided into two groups: AChE and butyrylcholine esterase (BuChE). This is based on their substrate preferences, tissue localization sensitivity and selectivity to inhibitors. Thus the thiocholine method of Koelle and Friedenwald (1949) and the thiocholine ferricyanide method of Karnovsky and Roots (1964) have been used for many years with various modifications as the means to identify ChEcontaining cells and nerve processes at both the light and electron microscopic level. Methods have shown that the reaction depends on the enzyme hydrolysis of ACh that liberates thiocholine which in the presence of copper ions precipitates as colourless copper thiocholine at the sites of AChE activity. Copper thiocholine is then treated with dilute ammonium sulphide, to gain a precipitate of brownish copper sulphide as the final product. The Karnovsky and Roots method utilizes the ability of thiocholine to reduce, copper ferricyanide ions to the final reaction product of copper ferriCyanide. This eliminates the need for ammonium sulphide as used in the Koelle and Friedenwald method and

### 19.2.2 Chromatography and immunology

Interest in cholinesterases has increased in the past few years with the recognition that these two compounds appear in a variety of molecular forms with a characteristic localization inside and outside the cell, which perhaps relates to a functional role (Massoulie and Bon, 1982; Brimijoin,
1983). Another major development has been the increasing success in using affinity chromatography to gain highly purified cholinesterases for study (Mintz and Brimijoin, 1985). The most recent development is the application of immunology and molecular biology to the study of cholinesterases (Brimijoin, 1986).
Immunological work started with poorly delineated polyclonal antisera and has progressed to elaborately characterized monoclonal antibody. Benda et al. (1970) reported the first immunochemical study of
AChE in the electric eel. Polyclonal and monoclonal antibodies have been successfully used to localize cholinesterases at both the light and electron microscopic levels. Work has been focused on the four areas:

3. Quantification;

### 19.2.3 Cholinergic and adrenergic terminals

Methods other than AChE activity have been used to identify cholinergic nerves. The use of special fixatives and stains in conjunction with an ultrastructural study is one approach. Richardson (1966) found that potassium permanganate fixation enhanced the preservation of densecore granules over that of glutaraldehyde or osmium. According to
Hockfelt and Johnson (1968) an electron-dense precipitate of manganese dioxide is formed where noradrenaline is reduced by the permanganate.
Hand (1973) used potassium permanganate and was able to differentiate cholinergic from adrenergic nerve terminals in the parotid gland, as did Avery and Cox (1977) in the odontogenic region of mouse teeth.
They noted that nerve endings in this area were generally of two types:
one contained a majority of small, dense-core staining vesicles and were believed to be adrenergic, while another type were lighter-stained, appearing electron-lucent and were believed to be cholinergic.
Burnstock (1979) in an ultrastructural study compared the intra-axonal vesicle size and appearance of autonomic preganglionic and postganglionic cholinergic and adrenergic terminals. He reported the cholinergic to have a predominance of small granular vesicles 40-60 nm in diameter and a few (1 %) large granular vesicles 40-150 nm in diameter. On the other hand, adrenergic terminals were found to have a predominance of small granular vesicles 40-60 nm in diameter and a variable number (3-24 %) of large granular vesicles 60-120 nm in diameter. The cholinergic terminals thus differ from adrenergic in the density of the small vesicles and in that they have fewer large granular vesicles and are more variable in size. A third type of profile was reported which represents non-adrenergic!non-cholinergic nerves. These endings contain a predominance of large opaque vesicles (80-200 nm in diameter). These have been described as purinergic by Burnstock (1972) or peptidergic by
Baumgarten et aI. (1970) and Pearse et aI. (1977). Sensory nerve terminals were reported to be packed with small mitochondria and have few, if any, vesicles (Burn stock and Iwayama, 1971). There are also marked differences in vesicular populations depending on their location in the body. For example, there are nine different types of axonal profiles in the gastrointestinal tract (Burn stock, 1979).

Although there are numerous reports in the literature describing nerves and nerve terminals adjacent to odontoblasts, there are no reports describing synaptic specializations between the two cell types (Byers et al., 1982; Tsukada, 1987; Byers et al., 1987; Gunji and
Kobayashi, 1983; Calle, 1985; Bueltmann et al., 1972; Holland, 1975,
1976, 1980; Arwill, 1967; Arwill et al., 1973; Frank, 1968a,b; Lilja, 1979).

Cholinergic system and the dental pulp

These studies suggest that the close approximation of the nerve terminals to the odontoblast plasma membrane of 20-40 nm is a unique type of synaptic specialization found in the dental pulp (Byers et al., 1982), corneal epithelium (Hoyes and Barber, 1977) and in other peripheral nerves (Ochoa, 1976). Several studies have morphologically demonstrated nerve terminals which indent and coil around the odontoblast process (Avery and Cox, 1977; Holland, 1977; Gunji, 1982; Frank,
1968a,b; Tsukada, 1987). Functionally, these tortuous neuronal terminals could be deformed during movement of the odontoblast cell body or process depolarizing the terminal and initiating an action potential.
This type of dentinal sensory mechanism would be most compatible with the hydrodynamic theory of pain transmission from the teeth (Gysi, 1900; Brannstrom, 1962, 1968; Brannstrom and Astrom, 1972;
Anderson and Linden, 1972; Brannstrom et al., 1979) but would not obviate the direct neural (Stella and Fuentes, 1963; Scott, 1966) or transduction theory (Avery, 1963; Avery et al., 1980; Kroeger et al., 1961;
Winter et al., 1962; Dubner et al., 1978) either.
Modulation of the odontoblast by the autonomic nervous system and/ or sensory nervous system is an intriguing hypothesis and is based on the diversity of nerves and neurotransmitters found in the odontoblastic zone and coronal dental pulp. Recently, Avery et al. (1984) suggested a reflex arc hypothesis which could account for changes in odontoblastic activity after neural stimulation or denervation. This theory suggests an interaction between the sensory nerve supply to the odontogenic zone and the autonomic nerve supply in which the cholinergic receptor modulates adrenergic receptor activation through internuncial neurons in the central nervous system and postganglionic sympathetic neurons located in the superior cervical ganglion.
In summary, the complexity of the innervation to the dental pulp and the multiple morphological types of nerve terminals found in the dental pulp presents an unparalleled challenge to the modern-day pulp biologist. With the use of molecular probes, in situ hybridization and antibodies to specific neurotransmitters in conjunction with special fixatives, the answers to some of the above perplexing questions could be answered in the near future.
