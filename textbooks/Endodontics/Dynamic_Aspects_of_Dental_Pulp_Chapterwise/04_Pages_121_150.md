## 7

## Concluding remarks

K. TAKAHASHI

The odontoblastic process is a long structure derived from the odontoblast and embedded within the slender dentinal tubules. It may have a predominant role in metabolic and synthetic processes. As long as the tooth is vital the odontoblastic process will continue to grow and keep alive.
Although the odontoblastic process has the same fate as the tooth, it will sometimes be compressed and lose its vitality owing to narrowing of the dentinal tubule, which in turn is the result of apposition of peritubular dentine corresponding to age changes and external stimulation. Irreversible damage will occur in odontoblastic processes when a deep cavity preparation is carried out.
Up to this day, it is still a controversial issue whether the process extends to a distance equal to the thickness of dentine or just retreats back, releasing essential elements for dentine formation. The suggestion that the odontoblastic process embedded over the full length of dentinal tubule is an artefact of shrinking due to fixation is far from certain. A variety of new approaches have been used to elucidate these puzzles but with little progress. It has been reported that odontoblastic processes of young dogs extend over the full thickness of dentine to reach the cementum layer, indicating that they might have a different length under certain conditions. The peripheral matrix component of the odontoblastic process located just next to the predentine layer plays a predominant role in resorption and digestion and may have a role in mineralization. Observations on odontoblastic processes have revealed that some vesicles within them are endocytotic and some exocytotic. If we apply these findings to explain internal resorption in demduous dentition we can understand that resorption will occur from the lateral internal surface of a deciduous root owing to eruption of the permanent tooth. Once the resorption involves the root apex, the commencement of resorption from the inner wall of the root canal will occur. In other words, internal resorption will proceed and extend and spread over the

It is still uncertain why dentinal tubules located at the demarcation between primary dentine and secondary dentine change their 'direction.
One explanation is that the directional change is due to the bodily movement of the odontoblast or a change in angulation between the cell body of the odontoblast and its process. On the other hand, secondary dentine cannot be formed in deciduous teeth because of the rapid decreasing activity of odontoblasts at the end of the root completion stage. Once the switch for formation of primary dentine of deciduous teeth is turned off, it cannot be turned on again for secondary dentine formation. However, in the permanent dentition, immediately after the formation of primary dentine is switched off, formation of secondary dentine will be turned on spontaneously. It is believed that this very short time lag of the switch-over operation will cause bodily movement of odontoblasts, thus causing a change of direction of the dentinal tubules in permanent teeth.
Owing to the fact that dental pulp is confined within a limited space by the surrounding hard tissues, the exact nature of the microcirculation occurring in this encapsulated compartment still remains a confusing issue. In order to investigate the vasculature of the dental pulp and its architecture, a wide variety of approaches have been used by many investigators, but with conflicting results. By fabrication of micro corrosion casts studied under scanning electron microscopy, we investigated the fundamental architecture of the vasculature in the dental pulp and its changes with age, as well as the morphological changes corresponding to inflammation. The observations indicated that despite the differences between animal species, within their life span, the fundamental vasculature of dental pulp located underneath the dentine can be classified into three patterns: (1) a terminal capillary network formed within the odontoblastic layer; (2) a capillary network alfgned perpendicular to the former one; and (3) a venular network located in the deeper portion of the dental pulp. Rodent incisor teeth keep on forming throughout the whole life span.
This fact is evidenced by the wide open orifice of the apex, from which the dentine is formed. However, when the mineralization begins in the predentine the terminal capillary network will become arrayed close to the odontoblasts, with no space in between. Calcium ions coming out from vessels will first diffuse into the odontoblastic region and then into the odontoblasts. It has been shown that a close contact between the vessels and cells facilitates transport of metabolic substances to the odontoblasts.
As long as secondary dentine is formed in dog pulp, the three major vascular networks will undergo remodelling, resulting in disappearance of most of the capillary network and venular network. On the other hand, vascular reactions are found to occur mainly in postcapillaries or venules. Since vascular reactions in response to inflammation are supposed to be one of the protective systems of our body, the decrease in number of venular networks, due to continuous reconstruction with age, might lead to a lower resistance of pulp as we become older. The clinical implication of this view is that the vitality of dental pulp of elderly persons may be more susceptible to external stimuli than in young individuals. Therefore, when dental treatment is goin-g to be carried out, the dental age factor should be taken seriously into consideration.

8.1 SUMMARY

The dental pulp is richly innervated by both myelinated (A-fibres) and unmyelinated (C-fibres) axons. A great majority of them belong to the
C-fibre group. During their course from the root apex to the coronal pulp, individual axons branch extensively and finally form free nerve endings, mostly in the pulp-dentine border area. Only the inner 100200l-1m of dentine is innervated.
Recent electrophysiological studies have shown that intradental Aand C-fibres are functionally different. A-fibres are responsible for dentine sensitivity and are probably activated by the hydrodynamic mechanism. Accordingly, fluid flow in dentinal tubules and the subsequent mechanical distortion of the tissue in the pulp-dentine border area is the final stimulus for the A-fibre endings. C-fibres are polymodal and respond to various stimuli which reach the pulp proper. They have high thresholds and are activated by thermal, mechanical and chemical irritants, for example, by bradykinin and histamine. Their functional characteristics indicate that they may play a significant role in the development of the pain symptoms in pulpal inflammation.

8.2 INTRODUCTION

Pain sensations of varying qualities and intensities are induced in response to activation of intradental nerves in man (Mumford, 1982).
With electrical stimulation close to perception threshold level, afso nonpainful (prepain) sensations can be induced (Mumford and Bowsher,
1976; McGrath et al., 1983). However, there is little evidence for nonpainful sensations in response to natural stimuli. Drilling of dentine with a turbine bur or drying of the exposed dentine surface with air blasts typically induce sharp, piercing pain, while the pain experienced

The quality and intensity of the sensation in response to tooth stimulation is dependent on the number and the type of activated nerve fibres on one hand and on the pattern of firing of individual nerve units on the other (McGrath et al., 1983; Virtanen et al., 1987). 1986). In this chapter the characteristics of intra dental sensory units will be described. The results of the animal experiments will be compared to those of human studies and to clinical findings in various cases of dental pain.

### 8.4 FUNCTION OF INTRADENT AL NERVE UNITS

Because the possibilities of recording intradental nerve responses in human teeth are limited, much of the information on the pulp nerve function originate from experiments performed on experimental animals, mostly on cats and dogs. However, using the dentine recording method where electrodes are placed in dentinal cavities a few studies have successfully been performed also on human teeth (Edwall and Olgart, 1977; Ahlquist et aI., 1984, 1986). The dentine recording technique was originally developed for recording from canine teeth in cats (Scott and Tempel, 1963). Although it has the limitation that the action potentials of only the fast conducting (A-type) intra dental nerves can be recorded (Haegerstam, 1976b), a great deal of new information on the function of the A-fibre group has been obtained in various studies on cats, when this technique has been employed (Edwall and Scott,
1971; Haegerstam et aI., 1975; Haegerstam, 1976a; Haegerstam and
Edwall, 1977; Ahlberg, 1978a,b; Olgart, 1974, 1979, 1986). One of the most important findings in human experiments using this technique was that intradental nerve activity induced by external stimulation of the tooth crown is related to the pain sensation experienced by the subject (Edwall and Olgart, 1977; Ahlquist et aI., 1984, 1986).
The only way of recording the responses of single intradental nerve units is the dissection of fine nerve filaments from the nerve bundles entering the teeth (Pfaffmann, 1939). Usually the nerve filaments are dissected from the inferior alveolar nerve after exposing the mandibular canal.
