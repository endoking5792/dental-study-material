### 5.7 AGE CHANGES

Dentine undergoes a number of post-maturation changes; some apparently predetermined age changes and others in response to outside factors such as attrition and caries. The process plays a role in all of these changes. The tubules themselves become narrowed by the formation of so-called peritubular dentine, which would be more accurately termed intra tubular as it forms a lining on the walls of the tubule.
Peritubular dentine appears to be a normal age change that can be accelerated by outside stimuli. Mendis and Darling (1979) found that the thickness of peritubular dentine was only 20% greater in tubules beneath areas of attrition. Weber (1974) has suggested that the peritubular formation in intact teeth be termed 'physiologic', and that beneath caries where direct intra tubular mineralization is combined with hypercalcification of the peritubular dentine it be termed 'pathologic'. The boundary between the two may not be entirely clear. The peritubular dentine lacks a fibrillar matrix, although some organic material is present. One view is that it is formed after the odontoblast process has retreated and thus lacks the collagen matrix provided for the intertubular dentine. A number of studies revealing intra tubular mineralization in tubules still containing odontoblastic processes (Fra}lk and Voegel,
1980; Thomas, 1983; Hawkinson and Eisenmann, 1983) would seem to refute this idea. Under cavity preparations the material occluding the tubules may, in fact, be collagenous (Ten Cate et al., 1985). A contribution from saliva has also been suggested by Brannstrom and Garberoglio

Irregular secondary dentine may perhaps more properly be called reparative dentine (Scott and Weber, 1977) as it is more commonly found under dentine that has received some kind of external stimulus such as caries attrition or cavity preparation. It seems that different odontoblasts form irregular secondary dentine from those that form primary dentine (Mjor, 1984), a new generation differentiated from subodontoblastic mesenchymal cells. The irregularity is presumably due to the fact that the odontoblasts never achieve the same degree of morphodifferentiation and alignment that primary odontoblasts do, but presumably (and it is only a presumption) the role of the process is otherwise similar to its role in primary dentine. The continuity of irregular and primary dentinal tubules has not been studied with the same thoroughness as between regular secondary and primary dentine, but it appears that there is a more complete barrier between the two such that primary dentine external to irregular secondary dentine would be expected to be acellular.

### s.s SENSORY RECEPTION

The odontoblast process is not the sole occupant of the dentinal tubules.
In some areas many of the processes are accompanied for part of their course by sensory axons (Fearnhead, 1961; Frank, 1968). The most favoured explanation of dentine sensitivity, the so-called hydrodynamic mechanism, does not require the presence of nerves or receptors in the dentine.
Frank (1968) showed a complex relationship between a nerve-like structure and an odontoblast process and described it as an intimate functional relationship between a connective tissue cell process and a sensory nerve fibre: a 'unique neurosensitive complex'. Arwill (1968) described somewhat similar structures and remarked on their synapselike appearance. Enthusiasm for this special relationship has not thrived. Byers (1977) examined junctions betw~en intra dentinal axons labelled in the rat by autoradiographic detection of axon ally transported radioactive amino acids. She found 'wide appositions' between nerves and odontoblast processes, but these junctions are unlike any junction that could be considered synapse-like. Close examination of similar axon-odontoblastic contacts recognized in the cat (Holland, 1984) also indiCates that these are non-synaptic. Apposed membrctnes sometimes appear locally thickened, but little other specialization is evident. It is interesting that the only other recent report of synapse-like junctions, in this case gap type possibly electrotonic junctions, was made on human tissue. (Koling and Raske-Anderson, 1983). ### 6.1 INTRODUCTION

The study of blood vessels of the pulp in the teeth of human and experimental animals has been done by using various methods and techniques. It is believed that in 1901 Lepkowski was the first investigator to show that some branches of the inferior alveolar artery entered the proximal papilla of human fetus by injection of 'Berlin blue' into the blood vessels, and Hopewell-Smith (1918), Welling (1925) and Kamrin (1934) gave a systematic account of the arrangement and structure of the blood vessels in the dental pulp. In 1942, Boling made an attempt to study the vascular arrangement of the pulpal vessels in three dimensions by making graphic reconstructions from serial sections of the jaws of dogs and cats using injections of 'Prussian blue' and clearing the specimens. Kramer (1951) studied the pulpal vasculature of freshly extracted human teeth using the unique Indian ink suction technique.
Saunders (1957) and Rapp and Fenton (1984) injected a radiopaque material and Indian ink into the pulpal vessels of extracted human teeth using modified Kramer's technique. Kindlova (1959) tried to study three-dimensional vascular arrangement by constructing a latex cast of rat pulpal vessels and observing it under the light microscope.
Although numerous studies demonstrated the vascular network arrangement of pulpal vessels, they presented only a small portion of the pulpal vascular architecture. Thus, the vascular arrangement in the pulp was not clearly understood and many questions re1(lained unanswered. In 1975, Kishi et al., presented the detailed vascular arrangement of the pulpal vessels in a three-dimensional picture using corrosive resin cast observed under a scanning electron microscope (SEM). Also in 1982 and 1985, a change of vascular architecture with growth was clearly demonstrated by Takahashi and co-workers in the pulpal vessels of dog tooth.

### 6.2.2 Root formation stage

This is the period during which the root is forming and tooth eruption starts. Figure 6.11 represents a pulpal vascular cast during root formation. The superficial layer of the vascular network has been removed.
Within the mesial root canal, main arterioles and venules run tortuously along their length through the canal, with the arterioles being slightly less tortuous. In contrast to that in the mesial root, the arterioles and venules in the distal canal run almost straight towards the coronal area.
As a result of the above, the distal root seems to mature earlier than does the mesial root.
The capillary network is distributed in the superficial layer of the whole pulp, particularly in the roof of the coronal pulp, and has a thickness of 400-500 fJ-m. This superficial capillary network diminishes in thickness in the root canal direction. Moreover, the formation of the capillary network on the floor of the pulp chamber is less well developed.
