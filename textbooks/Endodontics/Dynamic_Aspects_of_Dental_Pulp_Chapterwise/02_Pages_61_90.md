### 3.1 INTRODUCTION

Fibroblast cell lines are used routinely as research tools, investigating a variety of functions. The ease of isolation and successful growth in culture are two reasons for the utilization of this cell as an experimental tool. This ease of isolation has thus contributed to the large literature now available concerning the isolation, maintenance and properties of fibroblasts isolated from a variety of tissues and species.
The fibroblast is generally regarded as an elongated cell with numerous long cytoplasmic processes and an oval nucleus with smooth contours, often containing a nucleolus (Ross, 1968). There is an unusually abundant rough endoplasmic reticulum and a well-developed
Golgi complex. It is not surrounded by basement membrane.
In his excellent book on the biology of pulp and dentine, Baume (1980) discusses 'pulp fibroblasts' and suggests that the term 'fibroblast' designating pulpal cells is misleading if not incorrect. This conclusion is reached by virtue of the fact that the embryonic induction of pulp cells is different from other fibroblasts (Hall,1978) and that differences in cytostructure and function are also found (Han et al., 1965; Griffin and
Harris, 1966; Harris and Griffin, 1967). In view of this situation the terms pulpoblast and pulpocyte were suggested; while accepting the specialized nature of these cells and their lineage, the terms mesenchymal cells and pulp fibroblasts will be used to show their isolation from dental papilla and dental pulp respectively.
Tissue culture has been a valuable tool with which to observe the behaviour of cells ill vitro. The developing tooth therefore presents a unique opportunity for the isolation and study of cells at a particular stage of development, in the hope that cells isolated at anyone point will reflect the in vivo activity in vitro. ### 3.2.2 Dental pulp fibroblasts

A number of workers have successfully cultured fibroblasts from dental pulp either from explants (Magloire et al., 1981) or as sin~le-cell suspensions (Shuttleworth et al., 1980; Miller et ai., 1976b). The major problems with the use of cell suspensions to establish primary cultures are the difficulties experienced in disaggregating the tissue and the relatively low viability of the single cells obtained.
Pulp fibroblasts have been isolated from a number of species; in the case of human and bovine teeth the separation of pulp from dentine invariably occurs through the cell-free layer of Wei I (Miller et at., 1976b;
Pincus, 1950), leaving the primary odontoblasts attached to the dentine shell and the pulp free of these cells. While a number of enzymes have been used to isolate dental pulp fibroblasts (Miller et a/., 1976b) I have found the following procedure to work well with rabbit, bovine and human dental pulps.
Isolated dental pulps are placed in phosphate buffered saline; contamination can be avoided by the use of appropriate antibiotics - penicillin, streptomycin (100 j.Lg/ml), gentamycin (50 j.Lg/ml), tylosin (60 j.Lg/ml). After incubation at 37'C for 15 minutes the pulps are digested with collagenase and hyaluronidase (1 mg/ml) at 37°C for 45 minutes, trypsin was added (0.05%) and the incubation continued for a further
30 minutes. Th~ fibroblast-containing residue was collected by centrifugation, suspended in an appropriate volume of medium containing 15% fetal calf serum and aspirated. Released cells could then be collected and plated. While most fibroblasts thrive in these conditions, cells from human teeth require 15-20% serum in the initial medium to allow them to establish. The karyotype and other normal properties are generally quite stable in human dental pulp fibroblast cultures; aneuploidy is relatively rare and transformation due to causes other than oncogenic viruses is essentially non-existent. Morphologically, dental pulp fibroblasts appear similar to other fibroblast lines in that after subculturing the cells spread out and appear polygonal or stellate-shaped. On achieving confluence they become spindle-shaped and assume the well-known parallel arrangement of fibroblasts in tissue culture.
While the morphology is similar, fibroblasts from different organs in culture have been shown to exhibit subtle differences, supporting the contention that similar cell types from different organs are distinguishable on biochemical, if not morphological, grounds (Griffin et ai., 1976).
For example, lung and skin fibroblasts differ in terms of volume, doubling time, saturation densities, life span and RNA/DNA and proteinlDNA ratios (Schneider et ai., 1977); similarly dental papilla cells in culture have different growth characteristics to gingival mesenchymal cells (Thesleff, 1986).
It is also becoming increasingly apparent that there is considerable heterogeneity in the fibroblast population isolated from a particular organ with respect to life spans (Smith and Hayflick, 1974), sizes (Cristafalo and Kritchevsky, 1969) and interdivision times (Absher and
Absher, 1976). Indeed, it has been proposed that human fibroblasts are genetically heterogeneous, and a process of clonal selection governed
·by environmental ligands occurs, and that the tissue composition observed in both normal and diseased tissue is in part a consequence of the mixtures of clones present (Narayanan and Page, 1983). It remains to be seen how these heterogeneities in isolated populations are reflected in altered expression of the major differentiated functions. This type of heterogeneity highlights the importance of using primary cultures and
-early-passage cells, since clonal selection may be a feature of latepassage cells and consequently any changes seen are ascribable to cell selection and not a change in the function of the cell population as a whole. While heterogeneities in cell population are a feature to be aware of, the control of culture conditions is essential to maintain stable phenotype.

### 3.3 STABILITY OF PHENOTYPIC EXPRESSION OF

### CULTURED HUMAN DENTAL PULP FIBROBLASTS

The rationale for using cells in culture is that they maintain their phenotype in vitro, and many studies have shown that this holds true for a wide variety of isolated fibroblasts (Bradley et al., 1980; Sodek and
Limeback, 1979; Sjoberg and Fransson, 1977; Coster et al., 1979). In particular, work on gingival fibroblasts (Narayanan and Page, 1983) has shown that the types and proportions of collagens synthesized by fibroblasts from normal gingiva are similar to that found in normai tissue. In addition, fibroblasts from inflamed gingiva have a uniqqe phenotype, as do those cells isolated from drug-induced or spontaneously occurring gingival hyperplasia, and these characteristics persist indefinitely in vitro.
Recent work suggests that collagen production in primary cultures of chick tendon fibroblasts decreased when compared with intact tendon.
The co-ordinated regulation of a1 and a2 chains was not always maintained and message levels of a1 to a2 were four times greater in primary culture cells than in tendons or freshly suspended cells (Quinones et al., 1986). Caution should therefore be exercised when extrapolating primary culture data to explain ill vivo events. It is not clear if other collagen message levels are similarly affected when cells are isolated or indeed if these changes would be reflected in altered protein synthetic profiles.
Caution must also be exercised when comparing data from fibroblast cultures, in view of the finding that there is a decrease in protein synthesis with increasing donor age Oohnson et al., 1986). While this is unlikely to be a consideration when comparing mesenchymal cells isolated from the dental papilla, dental pulp fibroblast origin should be investigated, although it is unlikely that cells can be easily isolated and cultured from old teeth.
Fibroblast functions are regulated by a variety of factors, and the culture conditions used will largely determine the synthetic pattern seen. Most notable of these factors is serum. There are a number of factors in serum that are responsible both for attachment of cells and modulation of cell growth (Holley, 1975; Gospodarowicz and Moran,
1976). Some aspects of serum regulation will be considered in section 4.

### 3.5 DEVELOPMENTAL ASPECTS

The regulatory role of the dental papilla in tooth development is well documented (Kollar, 1972); it is also the only known precursor cells for odontoblasts and this, therefore, provides a means of assessing the morphogenetic capacity of isolated molecules by examining their ability to cause differentiation in the dental papilla. The developmental aspects of tooth formation and odontoblast differentiation are discussed in more detail in the chapter by Professor Ruch; readers are therefore advised to consult this chapter for a detailed discussion of odontoblast formation.
The specific phenotype of the odontoblast is not completely elucidated and controversy exists as to whether odontoblasts are able to differentiate from isolated dental papillae in the absence of an interacting epithelium or basal lamina (Ruch, 1985). While the majority of studies highlight the importance of epithelial/mesenchymal interactions (Ruch and Karcher-Djuricic, 1975; Ruch et a/., 1982; Thesleff et a/., 1983),
Magloire and Dumont (1976) succeeded in culturing developing human premolars which were capable of developing into cells that in transmission electron micrographs had characteristics of polarizing odontoblasts. The fact that some cells of the dental papilla maintain their unique characteristics ill vitro suggests that there is a potential source of mesenchymal cells in the dental pulp which has the capacity to differentiate into odontoblasts. Under normal circumstances in the formed tooth this would only occur when reparative dentine is formed, but suggests the intriguing possibility of manipulating the cells in culture and examining in isolation possible factors responsible for reparative dentine formation.

Dental pulp cells present an attractive alternative for use in tissue culture experiments since it is the pulp that many of the endodontic materials will be in contact with. Bovine dental pulp fibroblasts were suggested for cell culture experiments (Miller et ai., 1976a) and the response of these cells to endodontic materials has been explored (Miller and Munaco, 1976). 3.7 CONCLUDING REMARKS

Tissue culture has been a valuable tool in research and is being used increasingly to probe the factors responsible for tooth formation. The defined stages in tooth formation enables cell populations to be isolated from a particular stage in development, allowing cell characteristics to be determined.
Maintenance of a stable phenotype is an important requirement for using cell culture experiments to probe the nature of extracellular macromolecules. Changes in the extracellular matrix may be important signals for differentiation, and cell culture is a useful means of addressing not only this question but also the nature of the matrix macromolecules themselves.
The use of human dental pulp fibroblasts as an in vitro means of assessing endodontic materials remains an alluring possibility. Its ease of application suggests it should find greater acceptance than appears the case at the present time.

## Concluding remarks

C. A. SHUTTLEWORTH

In health, the pulp maintains its identity throughout life and much work has been directed to an understanding of what gives pulp tissues their highly specific function of producing dentine. Neural crest cell migration and differentiation gives rise to dental papilla cells and subsequently odontoblasts, aspects of which are reviewed in the chapter by
Dr Ruch.
Epithelial-mesenchymal interactions are major features of tooth development, and depending on the stage of development either epithelia or mesenchyme may serve an instructive function. What is not clear, however, is whether the instructive interactions are solely responsible or if developmental progress is self-generated and expressed in the presence of permissive factors.
The potential of dental papilla cells to develop into different cell types raises questions about the ability to form odontoblasts. Are all dental papilla cells potential odontoblasts, and is selection related to specific signals or are there distinct odontoblast and pulp cell populations?
Terminal differentiation of odontoblasts highlights the importance of basement membrane-mediated cell interactions. Odontoblast and ameloblast developmental processes are co-ordinated in an as yet unexplained manner. Extracellular-intracellular communication appears to have a role in a number of aspects of cellular organization.
Considerable advances have been made in our understanding of odontoblast development, but as pointed out by Dr Ruch the molecular nature of 'tooth specificity' remains unknown. Further work will be directed at the mechanism of induction which permits suppression or amplification of gene expression and their regulation on odontogenesis.

## Role of the odontoblast process

G.R. HOLLAND

### 5.1 INTRODUCTION

The odontoblast is a member of a family of secretory connective tissue cells. Unlike other members of the family, its cell body does not become embedded in the tissue it forms but retreats as new material is deposited, leaving a single narrow extension from which the components of the forming tissue are released. It is this cell process that makes the odontoblast and the tissue it forms, the dentine, anatomically unique.
Embryologically, odontoblasts originate from the neural crest and migrate to the developing jaw (Ruch, 1985). Once in the dental papilla, mitosis is followed by polarization and after an initial burst of secretory activity the process forms and the cell migrates centrally as dentine is deposited.
