### 15.2 COLLAGEN

The term collagen originally referred to the cross-striated fibrous elements seen in appropriately stained sections; in recent years it has become apparent that it describes a family of closely related proteins (for reviews see Burgeson and Morris, 1987; Kuhn, 1986; Mayne and Burgeson,
1987).
Members of the collagen family have certain structural similarities, including Gly-X-Y triplets, presence of hydroxyproline and the ability to form extracellular fibres/fibrils. The specific properties that determine the individual organizational patterns seen in tissues are not known, but they presumably account for the different structures, and hence functions, that the various collagen molecules exhibit. At the present time 12 different collagen types have been identified in mammalian tissues and, as techniques improve, so other collagenous molecules may be identified. Indeed type XII collagen was initially characterized from a short cDNA sequence (Gordon et al., 1987) and has subsequently been partially characterized and isolated from embryonic chick tendons (Dublert and van der Rest, 1987).
Classification of collagen types can be approached in a number of ways; in this review we will consider the major interstitial collagens as one group and all the other collagens as another group.

Cromorugghe and Schmidt, 1987). In general, these molecules form cross-banded fibres with a regular 67 nm repeat and are in large part responsible for the rigidity of connective tissues.

All these collagens contain a major triple helix (300 nm in length) and all are initially synthesized as a procollagen molecule containing extensions at the N- and C-terminal ends of the molecule (Fessler and Fessler,

In general these globular domains must be removed prior to fibril formation; failure for this to occur in type

While the processing of type I and II procollagens is rapid and complete, the extracellular processing of type III procollagen often results in retention of the N-terminal propeptide sequences (Kivirikko and Myllyla, 1984). The significance of this to the interaction with the type I collagen fibrillar system or to tissue extensibility is at present not clear.

These changes in collagen with age in the dental pulp have recently been reviewed (Nielsen, 1987; Bernick, 1987).

the importance of other glycoproteins to matrix functioning is only recently being appreciated. Extracellular glycoproteins are involved in many functions, ranging from a structural to a regulatory role in the morphology and differentiation of cells.

### 15.5 MATRIX GL YCOPROTEINS IN DENTAL PULP

Little information is available concerning the glycoproteins in dental pulp apart from fibronectin. Most studies show fibronectin to be present in dental pulp and papilla (Linde et al., 1982; Thesleff et al., 1979; Lesot et al., 1985), although in one case no fibronectin could be detected in pulp by immunohistochemical localization (Corvetti et al., 1984). In addition fibronectin can be extracted from pulp (van Amerongen et al.,
1984). Dental pulp fibroblasts in culture have been shown to synthesize and secrete fibronectin (Shuttleworth et al., 1982), whereas dental papilla cells synthesize fibronectin but do not deposit it in culture (Thesleff et al., 1987). This inability to deposit endogenous fibronectin was not due to an alteration in cell surface receptors for fibronectin;
these have been shown to be expressed in monolayer cultures (Lesot et al., 1985).
The specific role of fibronectin in dental pulp is unclear. A role in odontoblast differentiation is implicated by the loss of fibronectin staining from the dental mesenchyme during odontoblast differentiation (Thesleff et al., 1979; Lesot et al.i 1981b). Structurally fibronectin appears to be associated with argyrophilic fibres in the dental pulp (van
Amerongen et al., 1984), and its close association with collagen is reflected in the increased concentrations of these two macromolecules in the apical part of the tooth (van Amerongen et al., 1983).
Fibronectin binds with relatively high affinity to collagen and, while binding is most effective against denatured collagens, a number of native collagen types bind to fibronectin, in particular type III. The high levels of type III collagen in dental pulp and its interaction with fibronectin are presumably important determinants in dental pulp structure and function.

### 15.6 CONCLUDING REMARKS

Dental pulp is described as a loose connective tissue, whose extracellular matrix reflects its functioning in tooth formation and nutrition. The low collagen content and the high levels of type III collagen found are a reflection not only of the tensional forces it is subjected to, but also the compressive forces due to its location within the hard tissue of the tooth.
Little is yet known about the organization of type I and III collagen in pulp or, indeed, if mixed fibres of I and III exist. Functional significance of the varied collagen distribution throughout dental pulp is also unclear, as is the role of the minor collagen types V and VI. It remains to be seen if changes in collagen gene expression are a reflection of differentiation or are a consequence of it.
The microfibrillar components and glycoproteins of dental pulp present major research challenges. Glycoproteins are being shown to have important functional roles in a number of connective tissues; it is unlikely that dental pulp will be an exception. The importance of glycoproteins in controlling a variety of cell processes implicates them as determinants in odontoblast differentiation, but little information is currently available to describe their role in dental pulp maintainence and functioning.

### 16.1 INTRODUCTION

Proteoglycans are important constituents of the extracellular matrix of connective tissue, consisting of protein molecules to which glycosaminoglycan chains are linked. Glycosaminoglycans are long, unbranched polysaccharide chains constructed from repeating disaccharides and containing many acidic, carboxylate and/or sulphate groups (for a review, see Varma and Varma, 1982). Normally they do not occur as free chains in vivo, but as proteoglycans. The glycosaminoglycans have been widely investigated in a variety of tissues and organisms. Important functions are ascribed to these macromolecules in processes of differentiation, morphogenesis, growth, maturation, ageing, normal physiology and pathology (Rosenberg and Varma, 1982).
In the dental pulp, glycosaminoglycans are thought to playa role during dentinogenesis as postulated from histochemical observations (Wislocki and Sognnaes, 1950) and from biochemical analyses carried out by Linde (1973b). Hyaluronic acid and the sulphated proteoglycans have a great capacity to retain water in tissues (Ogston, 1966), which is especially of interest for pulp since its water content is as high as 88% (van Amerongen et al., 1983). It is suggested that the glycosaminoglycans are involved with electrolyte distribution during mineralization on account of their ion-binding properties (Bowness, 1968). The influence on collagen fibrillogenesis has been demonstrated by Obrink (1973), while the relation to fibronectin is described by Yamada et al.
(1980). Studies on the amount and composition of glycosaminoglycans in dental pulp have been carried out by several investigators (Linde,
1972; Linde 1973a,b; Orlowski, 1974; Embery, 1976; Sakomoto et al.,
1979; Shuttleworth et al., 1982). The variety of methods used for isolation, separation and determination of pulpal glycosaminoglycans
