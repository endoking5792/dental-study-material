## Concluding remarks

T. KUDO

Part Eight consists of three chapters dealing with dental pulp from the viewpoint of pathological dynamics.
Chapter 28 by Drs Byers and Taylor describes the reactions of dental sensory innervation in response to injury and inflammation. They observed injured and inflamed dental pulp over a long period and drew the conclusions that in the dental pulp the concentrated innervation of sensory nerves, especially that containing calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), in the underlying pulp, pre dentine and dentine may cause pain sensations that correspond well to the clinical symptoms, such as hypersensitivity of exposed dentine. It is recently known that CGRP coexists with substance P in sensory nerve in the pulp (see Ch. 21). In addition, CGRP-positive nerves are unmyelinated chemosensitive Cfibres that are generally considered not to be involved in dentine sensitivity (see Ch. 8). In this connection, their conclusions may require further examination. Their considerations, however, appear to be valid and are very interesting to consider in relation to the mechanisms of toothache. They also suggest that pulpitis without symptoms may occur when fibrotic changes in the pulp form a barrier between the lesion and the nerve fibres. Since fibrotic changes may develop chronically and be a healing stage of the pulpitis, one cannot accept that only formation of a fibrotic mechanical barrier will prevent sensory nerves from being excited by various stimuli, such as bradykinin, a pain-producing substance, and fluid movement across the dentine according to the hydrodynamic theory. However, it should be stated that fibroblasts in the pulp which form the barrier contain enkephalins, endogenous antiinflammatory and analgesic peptides and in addition, the peptides are produced from their precursor proteins when the fibroblasts in culture are stimulated by bradykinin (Kudo et al., unpublished data). The findings by Byers and Taylor on silent pulpitis may be explained in this context.

Concluding remarks

Chapter 29 by Drs Kudo, Wei and Inoki describes the possible physiological role of proteases, especially enkephalin-processing enzymes, in the dental pulp activated by bradykinin. They suggested that the enzymes are lysosomal cathepsin Bl and carboxypeptidase B, although these enzymes have not yet been identified in the pulp. In addition, they also suggested that there is an endogenous regulatory system of pain or inflammation in the pulp. Their findings are related to the pulp tissue in acute inflammation and may be difficult to reconcile with the findings by Drs Byers and Taylor. Additionally, it is not likely that substance P-containing fibres are directly involved in the activation of enkephalin-processing enzymes. However, substance P may play an important role in vasodilatation, followed by increased permeability of blood vessels in the pulp. It has recently been suggested that bradykinin causes the release of substance P from sensory nerve endings in the hind paw of the rat (Yonehara et al., personal communication, 1988). Therefore, in order to re-examine any role of substance P and/or CGRP in activation of enkephalin-processing enzymes in the pulp, in vivo experiments are required.
In Ch. 30, Drs Dohi, Okamoto and Tsujimoto present an excellent review of arachidonic metabolism and antiseptics. They describe the mechanism of action of phenolic dental medicaments used for treatment of pulpal pain and of dentine hyperalgesia. They conclude that these compounds inhibit the activities of cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase, and that this inhibition is not due to non-specific denaturant action of protein but is specific for the oxygenases. Furthermore, they conclude that topical application of these compounds produce an antiinflammatory effect. They emphasize that eugenol is especially valuable for clinical use, because it is the most potent inhibitor for both cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase. However, there is much evidence that prostaglandin E2(PGE2) administered peripherally or systemically has anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects. In addition, it has recently been reported that PGE2, like hydrocortisone and lidocaine, shows a stabilizing effect on lysosomal membrane of the dental pulp cells, resulting in reduced activities of BANA-degrading and enkephalin-processing enzymes, as shown partly in Ch. 29 (Kudo et al., 1990). Therefore, the action of arachidonic acid metabolites in inflammation may need to be re-examined.
From Part Eight, we may understand that pain mechanisms and inflammation in the pulp involve multiple factors acting in a dynamic interplay. It is our hope that further understanding of these reactions will be gained from the continuous work and co-operation of many new scientists and the present investigators in the years to come.
