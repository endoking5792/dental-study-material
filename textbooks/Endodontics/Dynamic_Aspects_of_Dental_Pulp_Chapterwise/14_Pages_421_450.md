### 28.1 INTRODUCTION

Research on dental innervation since 1960 has greatly improved our understanding of the distribution and function of sensory nerve endings in pulp and dentine, but many questions still remain. For example, various structural techniques have found that dental nerves in animal and human teeth branch extensively in the crown to form beaded fibres in the peripheral plexus (of Raschkow), in the odontoblast cell layer, in predentine, and in the inner 0.1 mm of dentine (Fearnhead, 1961, 1967;
Frank, 1968; Arwill et aI., 1973; Byers and Kish 1976; Gunji, 1982; Byers and Dong, 1983; Byers, 1984, 1990; Johnsen, 1985; Maeda et aI., 1986,
1987). The nerve terminals are concentrated near the pulp tip with a decreasing density towards cervical regions and very little in the roots.
There are also some sensory and sympathetic nerve fibres along blood vessels deep in pulp (Pohto and Antila, 1972; Edwall et aI., 1985). Many of the nerve fibres in the odontoblast layer, predentine and dentine are closely associated with the odontoblasts, but exactly what type of interaction (if any) occurs between nerves and odontoblasts is not clear.
Evidence for synaptic or gap junctional connections between nerve fibres and odontoblasts is lacking (Frank et aI., 1972; Arwill et aI., 1973;
Corpron and Avery, 1973; Byers, 1977; Byers et aI., 1982); but electron microscopy suggests that there may be some kind of adhesive junctions between nerve fibres and odontoblasts (Frank, 1968; Gunji, 1982; Byers,
1984).
Physiological experiments during the same period have found that there are several different sensory systems in teeth, but exactly which one correlates with which group of nerve endings is still unclear. Nerve

Physiological studies show that neuropeptides such as CGRP and substance P can be released by sensory nerve fibres and can affect blood flow, vasodilation, protein extravasation, leukocyte and macrophage migration and function, fibroblast proliferation and angiogenesis (e.g., Gazelius et al.,
1987; Payan et ai., 1987; Maggio, 1988). Given their complex tissue actions it is likely that neuropeptides have important effects on pulpal and dentinal biology, but these have not yet been proven. Do they also interact (synergistically or antagonistically) with other nerve fibres to modulate sensory properties, as has been found in other tissues (Lundberg and Hokfelt 1986); and what are the sensory functions of peptidergic fibres in normal, injured or inflamed teeth? Finally, if C-fibres only react to pulpal stimulation, why are many terminals of CGRP peptidergic unmyelinated axons found in dentine? Possibly the high thresholds of C-fibres make them resistant to dentinal hydrodynamic stimulation.
Clinical dental pain problems have so far been difficult to correlate with specific pulpal pathology and neural reactions (Tyldesley and
Mumford, 1970). These conditions include hypersensitive dentine, silent (painless) pulpitis, episodic toothache and difficult anaesthesia of inflamed teeth (Brown, 1981; Seltzer, 1988). The poor correlation between clinical pain and specific pathological lesions can make pulp vitality testing and diagnosis particularly difficult (Trowbridge, 1985).

One way to gain information about the function of different types of dental nerve fibres is to study teeth that have been injured in standardized ways. Extensive work by Brannstrom and his colleagues on human teeth correlated outward dentinal fluid movement (caused by air-drying of exposed dentine) with sharp pain (for review see Brannstrom, 1981,
1986). If the dentinal cavity dried too much, sensitivity was lost, presumably due to loss of fluid connection with inner dentine or pulp.
Interestingly, in those cavities left untreated for 7-16 days the underlying odontoblasts were completely destroyed by infection, yet pain sensitivity was enhanced (Lilja et al., 1982). One might conclude from those experiments that nerve--odontoblast associations and intradentinal nerve fibres are not required for the sharp pain response to dentinai stimulation. Similar kinds of experiments on animal teeth show unaltered reactions of A-fibres to repeated dentinal stimulation for several hours, in spite of damage to the cell processes in the underlying dentine or pulp-dentine border by the first stimulus (Hirvonen et al.,
1984; Hirvonen and Narhi, 1986). Another explanation for these results would be that the responding fibres are located in normal regions that surround the injured areas.
A different approach was used by Berger et al. (1983) studying denervated rat molars. They found that regeneration of trigeminal nerve fibres to the coronal pulp-dentine border area and to dentine had occurred in conjunction with the return of the jaw-opening response to dentinal electric monopolar stimulation. Since the jaw-opening reflex depends on fast-conducting A-fibres (Greenwood et al., 1972), their results suggested that reinnervation of the pulp-dentine border was important for A-fibre-dependent tooth sensitivity. The studies of
Ohman (1965) on replanted human teeth reached similar conclusions.
Combined morphological and functional studies of reinnervated cat canines suggest that sensory properties of reinnervated dentine are not normal (Robinson, 1981; Holland et al., 1987). A recent immunocytochemical study (Wakisaka et al., 1987b) found that peptidergic nerve fibres reinnervated dentine at the same rate as found using the axonal transport labelling techniques (Berger and Byers, 1983). In physiological studies of A-fibre responses to dentinal stimulation in cats, sensory properties were altered by pretreatment with anti-inflammatory drugs (Ahlberg, 1978), suggesting that the fast-conducting myelinated fibres are affected by inflammation.

28.2 NORMAL DENTAL INNERVATION OF RAT MOLARS*

The use of axonal transport labelling and immunocytochemistry (e.g.,
Byers, 1984, 1990; Maeda et al., 1986, 1987; Akai and Wakisaka, 1988) have confirmed Fearnhead's descriptions (1961, 1967) of the innervation of teeth: including the many nerve fibres in dentinal tubules around the tip of the pulp horn, decreasing incidence of nerves in mid-coronal and cervical dentine, and only rare innervation of radicular dentine. An example of autoradiographic mapping of trigeminal nerve fibres in normal rat molars is shown in Figs 28.1-28.3. In addition to the dentinal innervation, Fearnhead described a marginal plexus of branching fibres in predentine and the odontoblast layer, and a peripheral plexus (of
Raschkow) below the cell-free zone. In a recent detailed description of dental innervation, Gunji (1982) used the term 'marginal plexus' for the nerve fibres in the plexus of Raschkow plus the odontoblast layer, and differentiated it from the pre dentinal and dentinal innervation. We prefer Fearnhead's concept, because the cell-free zone seems to be an important separation between deeper pulp and the odontoblast layer.
Regardless of the terminology, it is important to recognize that many nerve fibres in pre dentine and the odontoblast layer are oriented perpendicular to the dentinal tubules and may be different from those that enter dentine.

28.3 CORONAL DENTINAL INJURY

Several histological studies by Brannsti'om and his colleagues (Brannstrom, 1981) found extensive aspiration of odontoblasts into dentine under desiccated cavities. Electron microscopic work by Lilja et al. (1982) found that odontoblasts and nerve-like structures were much further into dentine under such cavities than their normal distribution.
Several years ago we began a series of experiments to test the effect of cavities plus etching and desiccation on underlying pre-labelled trigeminal nerve fibres in rat molars. A typical example of the results that were seen immediately after cavity preparation is· shown in Fig. 4. Labelled nerve debris and odontoblasts were distributed along tubules under the cavity, and could be completely aspirated out of the tooth at the deepest injury. In electron microscopic autoradiographs of similar samples, some isolated labelled nerve fibres were found near pulp (Fig. 28.5), suggesting that odontoblasts can be pulled away from nerve fibres by this type of stimulus. The isolated nerve fibres did not apparently survive since none was found 5-7 days after injury (Byers et al., 1988).
In another group of rats, the cavity preparations at the occlusal surface (drilled, acid-etched, air-dried and resin-filled) were made one week before fixation and the nerve fibres were labelled by axonal transport of radioactive protein during the last day. In this series, the label shows surviving nerve fibres following a week of pulpal reactions, which included new reparative dentine production. When [3H]-amino acids are injected into the trigeminal ganglion, some label always leaks into the blood and is available for about 0.5-1 hours for uptake by rapidly synthesized tissues (such as reparative dentine) (see Byers, 1984 and
1990 for further discussion of technique). In the autoradiographs of rat molars injured 7 days (Figs 28.6 and 28.7), the newly synthesized reparative dentine blocked entry of nerve fibres into dentine and also was radioactively labelled. Heavily labelled nerve fibres were present in most surviving dentinal tubules adjacent to the occluded ones. In some of the innervated tubules, radioactivity occurred abnormally far into dentine (Byers et al., 1987, 1988).

28.4 CERVICAL DENTINAL INJURY

Hypersensitivity of exposed dentine is a common clinical problem, especially for exposed roots. The neurobiological basis for that hypersensitivity is not entirely clear, although opening of the exposed dentinal tubules seem to promote hydrodynamic activation of nerves, since tubule occlusion with resins or other substances can relieve the sensitivity (Brannstr6m, 1981; Pashley, 1985). In order to study the effects of cervical dentine exposure on molar peptidergic innervation (immunoreactive for antibodies to calcitonin gene-related peptide,
CGRP) (Figs 28.8 and 28.9), we drilled cavities into the anterior cervi<;:al dentine of maxillary and mandibular first molars of rats. In normal molars, CGRP fibres branch in the crown to form many fibres in the plexus of Raschkow, in the marginal plexus and in dentine (Uddman et al., 1986; Silverman and Kruger, 1987). They form dense dentinal innervation around the pulp tip, much less in mid-coronal regions, and only rarely enter root dentine. However, by 4 days after dentinal injury of the upper root the CGRP-immunoreactive (IR) fibres had proliferated underneath the cavity. The new fibres not only sprouted into the intact odontoblast layer, but also some fibres entered the injured radicular dentine. In this group of teeth no bacterial infection or necrosis occurred. The neural changes were temporary, with sprouting diminished by
7-10 days and gone by 21 days. Electron microscopic immunocytochemistry found that in addition to the CGRP-IR fibres, non-CGRP nerve fibres also sprouted under the mild injury (Taylor et al., 1988).

### 28.6 IRREVERSIBLE PULPITIS

When extensive pulpal exposures were made on the occlusal surfaces of rat molars, the teeth exhibited an irreversible pulpitis, with complete necrosis of the pulp by 3-5 weeks post-injury (Kimberly and Byers, 1988;
Khayat et al., 1988). Analysis of those teeth at various times post-injury showed persistent sprouting of CGRP-IR nerve fibres in the surviving vital pulp, and deformed nerve fibres at the vitalnon-vital interface (Fig.
28.12). In a few cases a core of surviving pulp and bundles of CGRP-IR fibres passed through avascular coagulated tissue and made nerve sprouts in patches of surviving coronal pulp (Fig. 28.13). These results are consistent both with axonal transport studies which found blockage of axonal transport at the vital non-vital interface (Byers et al., 1987) and with electron microscopy showing cores of surviving nerve fibres in inflamed pulps (Tomeck, 1977). In our experiments using immunocytochemistry for CGRP-IR, nerve sprouting was prolific and persistent in surviving pulp, but it was progressively eroded since the pulpitis was irreversible in all teeth with occlusal pulp exposures. Each part of the pulp therefore went through sequential changes of: (a) CGRP-IR sprouting; (b) inflammatory cell infiltration and neural degeneration; followed by (c) necrosis. These teeth differ from those described in sections 28.4 and 28.5 since bacterial infection and food impaction injury were chronic after pulp exposure.

### 28.8 CONCLUSIONS

Our experiments and those of others cited above have found evidence for the following conclusions:

1. When dentine is injured by drilling, followed by acid etching and desiccation, the hydrodynamic forces pull nerve fibres and odontoblast cell bodies into dentine; this aspiration would be lethal for nerve fibres torn away from pulpal axons, or for odontoblasts that move away from pulp.
2. Newly formed reparative dentine blocks reinnervation of injured dentine; but adjacent surviving dentinal tubules are heavily innervated.
3. Nerve fibres rapidly sprout under injured cervical dentine when the odontoblast layer is not destroyed. Sprouting CGRP-IR nerve fibres can temporarily innervate dentine of the roots, which normally is not innervated. The sprouting (which was maximal at 4 days, diminished

When cervical cavities cause destruction of the underlying odontoblast layer, local inflammation occurs in the pulp; that pulpitis is usually reversible, with dentine bridge formation and pulpal healing by 21-35 days. The teeth with limited pulpitis often have fibrous pulp tissue intervening between the inflamed area and the sprouting nerve fibres, suggesting that its formation may be a critical first step for pulp healing.
5. In rat molars with irreversible pulpitis (induced by occlusal pulp exposures) the necrotic tissue usually comes close to the sprouting nerves, with a narrow band of inflammatory cells in between. A fibrous tissue barrier is usually absent, and the tips of the nerve fibres often look abnormal or are degenerating. The sprouting of nerve fibres persists in adjacent vital pulp, shifting location towards the root apex with the progressive destruction of the pulp.
6. In a few cases, a core of pulp survives, surrounded by coagulated tissue, with some vital nerve bundles located in the core.
7. Early periapical lesions appear to coincide with sprouting of CGRP nerve fibres. That sprouting persists and is extensive around later lesions when pockets of inflammatory cells advance from necrotic teeth into the periodontium. Recriprocal neuro-immune interactions may occur in zones of sensory nerve sprouting and may be vital for tissue defence and healing.

These results, along with those of others (e.g., Yamaura et aL, 1988) suggest that the following correlations between clinical pain and dental neurobiology might be valid. Hypersensitive exposed dentine may have elevated numbers of nerve fibres in the underlying pulp and dentine.
Silent pulpitis may occur when fibrotic changes in the pulp form a barrier between the lesion and the nerve fibres. Coexistence of vital pulp and periapical lesions may be caused at least in part by CGRP nerve sprouting and the effect of their released neuropeptides on bone and periapical tissue. Difficult anaesthesia for inflamed teeth may be due to increased numbers of nerve fibres adjacent to the inflammation; if cytochemical changes in the affected nerve fibres extend all the way to the ganglion, that might contribute to reduced effectiveness of nerve blocks. Episodic toothache may depend on cycles of tissue destruction with adjacent nerve sprouting, followed by local healing with fibrous sequestration of the lesion creating a barrier between the inflammation and nerve fibres. At the present time these are interesting possibilities for which much more experimental confirmation is needed. The availa-

### 29.1 INTRODUCTION

It is well known that from 1860 to 1900 some investigators had already noted the existence of proteases and peptone in pus. Leukotaxin, which induces increased permeability in blood vessels and the taxis of neutrophils, was separated from the inflammatory exudate by Menkin (1940,
1950). He showed that leukotaxin was a polypeptide and suggested a possible role of proteases in inflammation.
Biochemical mechanisms of inflammation involving proteases, especially their specific characteristics and the cleaved products, are still important in studies on inflammation, despite recent interest in eicosanoids, cytokines, platelet-activating factor (PAF) and active oxygen.
However, there are few reports on proteases in inflamed dental pulp, although eicosanoids and bradykinin are dealt with in this book (see
Chs 25 and 26).
When the dental pulp is stimulated noxiously, plasma kinin-forming enzymes and plasma kininogens leak from blood vessels into the interstitial space owing to an increased permeability and kinins may be produced there (see Ch. 25), generating acute inflammation. Moreover, it has recently been found that the released kinins activate proteases in the pulp, followed by the production of enkephalins, which act as endogenous anti-inflammatory substances (Kudo et aI., 1983a,b,c). This chapter describes the mechanism of such activation of proteases in the inflamed dental pulp.

Bradykinin is well known as a pain-producing substance or an inflammatory mediator. Bradykinin is a nonapeptide cleaved from high

It has been suggested that camostat mesilate (FOY-305) (Tamura et al., 1977), an inhibitor of serine proteinases, inhibits the production of bradykinin in inflamed dental pulp (Kudo et al., 1983a, 1986b; Inoki and Kudo, 1986).

29.3.1 Enkephalin-processing enzymes

Enkephalins were first found as an endogenous morphine-like substance in porcine brains by Hughes et al. (Hughes, 1975; Hughes et al.,
1975). They determined the primary molecular structures of two enkephalins: methionine enkephalin (met-enkephalin) and leucine enkephalin (leu-enkephalin). The former is a pentapeptide with methionine at the C-terminus and the latter is one with leucine at the

Met-enkephalin is processed from a precursor protein (proenkephalin) by sequential digestion with both trypsin-like and carboxypeptidase Blike enzymes in bovine adrenal medulla (Steiner et al., 1974; Evangelista et al., 1982; Hook et al., 1982), which contains proenkephalin concomitantly with adrenaline (Viveros et al., 1979), although those enzymes are not yet identified (Mizuno and Matsuo, 1987).
Met-enkephalins have been detected in dental pulp of the dog canine tooth by an immunohistochemical technique (Ch. 25; Kudo et al., 1981,

### 29.3.2 BANA-degrading enzymes

Na-Benzoyl-arginine-r>-naphthylamide (BANA) is a synthetic substrate for trypsin-like enzymes, serine proteinases (Roth, 1963). However,
BANA is degraded by other enzymes, such as cathepsin Band H, lysosomal cysteine proteinases (Knight, 1980; Barrett and Kirschke,
1981; Schwartz and Barrett, 1980), into r>-naphthylamine, which can be measured spectrofluorophotometrically at an excitation wavelength of
338 nm and an emission wavelength of 410 nm.
BANA has been used as a synthetic substrate for enkephalinprocessing enzymes in in vitro experiments on dental pulp of the rat.
Using whole pulp tissue or pulp homogenate as the enzyme source,
BANA-degrading enzyme activity of the pulp was markedly enhanced by bradykinin in a dose-dependent manner (Kudo et al., 1987, 1988b), similar to the increased production of enkephalin from its precursor by bradykinin (see Ch. 25; Kudo et al., 1986a,b).
Little is known about proteases in the dental pulp, especially in the inflamed dental pulp, although lysosomal proteases, such as cathepsins, are known to be involved in inflammation (Hayashi, 1975; Ishida and
Hayashi, 1980). Therefore, a possible mechanism of increased BANA degradation by bradykinin may be explored using the dental pulp as a model for protease activity involved in inflammation.

### 29.4.1 Limited proteolysis

Proteases are classified into two main groups that are distinguished by their different actions on proteins. One is an exopeptidase group that cleaves proteins and peptides from its C- or N-terminus to produce the corresponding amino acids, such as carboxypeptidases, aminopeptidases and dipeptidylpeptidases.
