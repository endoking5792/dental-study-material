### 1.1.5 Nerve fibre development in teeth

The tooth, compared to other sensory organs, is relatively late in maturing. For example, neural development for sight and smell development is almost entirely prenatal. Taste organs have continued nerve development postnatally. The tooth is functional for a relatively long period before neural development nears completion. ### 1.2.1 Types and sizes of pulpal nerve fibres

The innervation of the pulp includes both afferent neurons that conduct sensory impulses and efferent autonomic fibres that provide principally neurogenic modulation of blood flow in the pulp. Myelinated axons are mostly in the 1-4 fLm diameter range (3-10 fLm circumference), consistent with a pain sensory function. However, a small percentage of myelinated axons is larger than the expected size for pain sensory fibres;
the function of these nerve fibres is unresolved. Myelinated axons have a relatively fast conduction velocity, relatively low stimulation threshold, and are thought to convey impulses perceived as sharp and penetrating (Trowbridge, 1983).
Unmyelinated nerve fibres include both pain sensory axons of trigeminal origin and autonomic fibres from the superior cervical ganglion.
Unmyelinated fibres are round to oval in cross-section; most of the unmyelinated axons in the radicular pulp of a mature tooth are 0.5 fLm or less in diameter. Pain sensory unmyelinated nerve fibres are thought to have a relatively slow conduction velocity, a relatively high stimulation threshold, and are thought to conduct impulses perceived as dull and lingering (Trowbridge, 1983).

### 1.2.4 Comparisons of primary and permanent teeth

Innervation characteristics of primary and permanent teeth are of biological and potential clinical significance. Tooth innervation (as shown in the cat) appears to follow a parallel course for the primary and permanent incisors up to the point of root resorption; significant axon degeneration occurs as soon as root resorption is evident (Johnsen and
Karlsson, 1977; Fried and Hildebrand, 1981b) (Fig. 1.18). However, development occurs over a much shorter time period in the primary tooth.
Numbers of myelinated and unmyelinated nerve fibres entering primary anterior teeth have been established (Johnsen and Karlsson,
1974; Johnsen and Johns, 1978; Fried and Hildebrand, 1981a,b) (Figs 1.19 and 1.20). Fully developed primary canines have more myelinated and unmyelinated nerve fibres entering than do mature permanent canines and permanent incisors, but fewer than premolars. The fully developed primary canine thus has approximately the same capacity to transmit impulses as do single-rooted permanent teeth. The primary incisor has significantly fewer myelinated and unmyelinated axons entering at the apex than the tooth types just mentioned. With the initiation of root resorption, the number of nerve fibres decreases Significantly (Fig. 1.21).
The question of pain reception, transmission and perception for primary compared to permanent teeth does not have a simple answer.
One major consideration is tooth type because of the lack of a clear difference in innervation levels between primary and permanent teeth.
The unanswered questions of possible differences in the receptor mechanism(s) and the interpretation of impulses as painful by the child compared to the adult put limits ·on further discussions of sensitivity comparisons.

### 2.1 INTRODUCTION

Histological, cytological and functional organization determine the identity of odontoblasts. These cells are most often aligned in a single layer at the periphery of the pulp. Fully differentiated odontoblasts are tall, columnar, postmitotic cells with a polarized distribution of their cytoplasmic organelles. These cells may be divided into the cell body and the odontoblastic cell process, which probably extends to the dentineenamel junction. Functional odontoblasts synthesize and secrete collagen type I (and type I trimer), collagen type V and non-collagenous components: proteoglycans, glycosaminoglycans, -y-carboxyglutamatecontaining proteins and phosphoproteins. Two levels of secretion probably exist: most components are exocytosed into the predentine, whilst other components (some phosphoproteins, osteocalcin) appear to be released at the mineralization front. Odontoblasts are connected by junctional complexes; however, these do not completely seal the interodontoblastic space.
In this chapter we shall discuss where the odontoblasts are coming from and how environmental factors may inflect or generate the progression of their development. Developmental aspects concerning the whole of odontogenesis have been reviewed several times (Kollar,
1983; Ruch, 1987; Ruch et al., 1983; Slavkin, 1974; Slavkin et al., 1981;
Thesleff and Hurmerinta, 1981).

### 2.3 ESTABLISHMENT OF ODONTOBLAST SPECIFICITY

Generation of cell diversity may be lineage-dependent and/or
'induction'-dependent (Holtzer et al., 1985; Stent, 1987). In complex vertebrates and mammals the fate of the cells in the earliest developmental stages is most often assumed to be indeterminate. Embryonic precursor cells will be stepwise committed, i.e. the developmental potential of pluripotent cells will be restricted to generate specific descendants.
At a developmental level 'tooth specificity' includes the capacity of neural crest derived cells to: (a) initiate and control dental lamina formation; (b) regulate tooth-specific histomorphogenesis of the enamel organ; and (c) give rise to functional odontoblasts and dental papilla cells (see Ruch, 1987 for review).
Several authors (Johnston and Listgarten, 1972; Cassin and Capuron,
1979) have strongly suggested that in amphibia the capacity of cranial neural crest to form teeth depended on interaction with stomodeal endoderm during their migration. Gerber et al. (1974) isolated mandibular arches from mouse embryos of 9 days gestational age and explanted them for periods up to 24 days in testis of homologous adult hosts. In
15 grafts, 27 teeth developed, comprising 17 incisors, 6 molars and 4 non-identified bell stages. The significantly higher frequency of incisors might result from a time-spatial segregation of 'incisor'- and 'molar'- specific neural crest cells. Osborn (1978) also suggested the existence of distinct dental clones, one each for the incisors, canines and all the molars. Noden (1982) provided experimental evidence of the existence of predetermined chicken mesencephalic neural crest cells. More recently, Lumsden (1985) explanted premigratory mouse cranial neural crest cells and grafted them in the anterior eye chamber of homologous adults, either alone or in combination with mouse mandibular or limb bud ectoderm and, in recombinations with mandibular ectoderm, teeth differentiated. Lumsden concluded that mammalian neural crest has an odontogenic potential which is expressed when crest cells interact with

### 2.4 KINETIC AND PHENOTYPIC ASPECTS OF DENTAL

### PAPILLA CELLS

The crown size and morphology of a specific tooth are mainly a result of the number of postmitotic odontoblasts (and ameloblasts) and of their spatial distribution. The metabolic activities of these cells, which control the amount of dentine and enamel, also play important roles. It seems likely that genetic factors determine the number of functional cells as well as their metabolic activity.
The percentage-labelled mitosis technique was used to estimate the cell cycle parameters during mouse odontogenesis (Osman and Ruch,
1978). During dental lamina formation (day 11) the average cell cycle time (TC) of presumptive dental papilla cells is 8.9 h. Phase G1 (TG1) lasts 2.1 h. At day 14, TC of mouse first lower dental papilla cells is 11.8 h and TG1 2.4h. At day 16, TC is 13.9h and TG1 4h. Between dental lamina formation and the appearance of the first postmitotic odontoblasts in the first lower mouse molar (day 18) a maximum of 14-15 cell cycles may exist. It is generally assumed that odontoblasts are diploid cells; however, radioautographic and cytophotometric data (Olive and
Ruch, 1982) suggested that the DNA content of these cells is close to tetraploid values. The genetic programme controlling the cell kinetics of dental cells is maintained in vitro (Ahmad and Ruch, 1987); however,
TC is significantly longer in vitro, explaining delayed appearance of postmitotic, functional cells. Intradental control mechanisms exist:
serum factors may affect the duration of the cycle and transferrin seems to be required (Partanen and Thesleff, 1987).

### 2.5 TERMINAL DIFFERENTIATION OF ODONTOBLASTS

The terminal differentiation of odontoblasts occurs in each tooth according to a specific temporo-spatial pattern. For example, in the first lower molar of laboratory-raised Swiss mice, the first overtly differentiated odontoblasts are found on day 18 (of gestation) at the tip of the principal cusps, and progressively functional odontoblasts emerge toward the basal (apical) parts (Fig. 2.1). The terminal differentiation of these cells lasts a few hours and is characterized by the following steps. Odontoblasts withdraw from the cell cycle, polarize and secrete predentine.

### 2.5.1 Epigenetic control mechanisms of odontoblast

### terminal differentiation

The terminal differentiation of odontoblasts raises several questions:

1. Is the shift of differentiation between progenitor odontoblasts and postmitotic odontoblasts the result of lineage-dependent alterations or, alternatively (complementarily?), are epigenetic signals involved in driving the preodontoblast to a terminal phenotype?
2. Is the dental papilla composed of distinct odontoblast and pulp cell progenitors or are all the dental papilla cells potential odontoblasts, but will only some of them, according to a specific spatial distribution of epigenetic signals, overtly differentiate?

Preliminary data concerning the production of monoclonal antibodies, directed against different dental papilla antigens, did not allow the identification of a specific odontoblast cell lineage (Zidan et al., 1987).
On the other hand, many complementary investigations support the conclusion that epigenetic control mechanisms exist. Preodontoblasts of dental papillae, isolated by treatment with proteolytic enzymes, cultured in vivo or in vitro never become functional (Kollar, 1972; Kollar and
Baird, 1969; Ruch and Karcher-Djuricic, 1971b). The mammalian heterograft experiments by Huggins et al. (1934) suggested that the dental epithelium plays a role in odontoblast terminal differentiation.

(a) Epithelial dependence of dentaI.papilla cells to differentiate into functional odontoblasts

### 2.6 ODONTOBLAST DIFFERENTIATION IN PRIMARY

### CULTURES AND AFTER PULPOTOMY

At a histological level, localization, cytological specificity, synthesis and polarized excretion of predentine-dentine components determine the identity of odontoblasts. True odontoblasts will not differentiate when isolated dental papillae or dental papilla fragments are cultured as monolayers in conventional media (Magloire and Dumont, 1976;
Magloire et al., 1985; Pourtois 1965, 1966). Cells of such cultures synthesize collagen type I, type III and fibronectin. Some odontoblastlike cells were observed in dental papillae cultured on Millipore filters, coated with dentine proteins (Lesot et al., 1986) and dental papilla matrix (Cam et al., 1986/7).
In vivo the formation of tubular dentine has frequently been observed after pulpotomy in rodents and during pulp healing in capped human teeth (Baume, 1980; Schroder, 1985; Yamamura, 1985). After capping with calcium hydroxide, pulp cells, endothelial cells and pericytes adjacent to the amputation differentiate after several cell cycles into functional odontoblasts. The sequence of tissue reactions following capping was summarized by Schroder (1985):

First there is vascular and inflammatory cell migration and proliferation to control and eliminate the irritating agent. Secondly the repair process occurs with migration and proliferation of mesenchymal and endothelial pulp cells and formation of collagen. When the pulp is protected from irritation the tissue formation decreases, odontoblasts differentiate from pulp cells and the tissue formed assumes the appearance of dentine.

This sequence lasts several weeks. The formation of dentine follows the deposition of a collagen-rich fibrodentine, which might play a role similar to that of the basement membrane during differentiation of 'first-

2.7 CONCLUSIONS

Tooth size, depending mainly on the number and spatial distribution of functional odontoblasts, appears to be a polygenic autosomal trait (Sofaer, 1975). The X and Y chromosomes seem to influence enamel formation, whereas Y chromosome affects the dentine thickness (Townsend et al., 1988).
The questions of how much information is encoded in the unknown genes involved in odontogenesis and how much epigenetic factors modulate the gene expression are still challenging.
The apparent behaviour of odontogenic cells fits with a cell lineagedependent interpretation (Osborn, 1978).
The summarized experimental data support some conclusions:

1. The molecular nature of 'tooth specificity' remains unknown;
2. The establishment of tooth 'specificity' depends on epigenetic factors, i.e. specific cell interactions. It is not known whether instructive interactions are involved or if the developmental programme is selfgenerated and expressed in the presence of permissive factors. The cellular and molecular nature for these mechanisms require further investigations. The existence of pre-patterned dental progenitor cells cannot yet be ruled out;
3. The dental papilla gives rise to different cells, but all or most of them might be potential odontoblasts;
4. It is not known whether developing dental papilla cells demonstrate typological hierarchy and how many steps are involved in the commitment of odontoblasts;
5. The terminal differentiation of odontoblasts, localized at the periphery of the pulp, is cell kinetic-dependent and requires specific basement membrane-mediated cell interactions. The operational basement membrane is placed in position according to a precise timing and spatial pattern as a function of changing, cell kineticdependent, ep~thelio-mesenchymal interactions. Odontoblast and ameloblast developmental processes are co-ordinated (Ruch, 1987).
6. Direct and/or indirect transmembrane links between matrix components and cytoskeleton components exist.
7. It is not known whether junctional complexes (gap junctions) playa role in the establishment and/or maintenance of odontoblasts' spatial pattern.
8. Further investigations should include the study of the role of cell adhesion molecules and autocrine and paracrine factors in odontoblast patterning and kinetics.
