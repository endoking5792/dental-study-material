8.S THE MECHANISMS OF DENTINE SENSITIVITY

As already mentioned, it seems probable that intradental A-fibres are activated by the hydrodynamic mechanism (Gysi, 1960; Brannstrom,
1963, 1966, 1981). Accordingly, fluid flow in dentinal tubules causes distortion of the tissue in the pulp-dentine border, which results in activation of the nerve units. Thus the A-nerve endings would be mechanosensitive. In fact in experimental animals A-fibres have been shown to respond to direct mechanical irritation of the pulp surface with von Fray hairs (Narhi et al., 1982a,c).
The common feature of many of the stimuli which activate A-fibres is that they can induce fluid flow in dentinal tubules as measured in vitro (Anderson, 1963; Brannstrom, 1963, 1981). Because of the high capillary pressure in the fine tubules rapid outward flow is induced whenever fluid is extracted by any stimulus from the outer ends of the tubules (Brannstrom, 1981). Although several different stimuli can activate intradental A-fibres, the final stimulus for the nerve endings with all of them is the same: mechanical distortion of the tissue in the pulp-dentine border.
As already mentioned, the sensitivity of dentine in human subjects and the responsiveness of the nerve units in experimental animals are to a great extent dependent on the condition of the exposed dentine surface with either blocked or open dentinal tubules (Brannstrom, 1965,
1981; Hirvonen et al., 1984). Blocking of the tubules with composite resin or with potassium oxalate (which precipitates as calcium oxalate crystals into the dentinal tubules) prevents the fluid flow (Pashley, 1986), which results in desensitization of dentine (Brannstrom, 1981; Hirvonen et al.,
1984). The tubules can be blocked at any place on their way from the

Probably the action potentials are initiated somewhere deeper on the pulpal side of the pulp-dentine border.
The independence of nerve activation on the existence of nerve fibres in dentine gives further support to the concept that mediation of the effects of external irritation from the dentine surface to the pulp nerves must be indirect. Because the odontoblasts are also destroyed, these results are in disagreement with the suggestion that the odontoblasts would act as receptor cells and be responsible for dentine sensitivity.

### 9.1 INTRODUCTION

The primary purpose of microcirculation is to transport nutrients and oxygen and to remove metabolic waste products from the tissue. It is also well known that the fundamental mechanism for vascular control is the local regulation of the basal vascular tone, which is reinforced by blood pressure and counteracted by tissue metabolites. Thus, the wellbeing of the tissue depends on the circulatory transport process, which is governed by many functional parameters of the microcirculation, such as blood flow, blood volume, intravascular and extravascular pressures, and capillary permeability (Aukland and Nicolaysen, 1981; Rothe and
Friedman, 1976; Kety, 1960); and therefore the microvasculature is the site of control of tissue perfusion, blood-tissue exchanges and tissue blood volume. Each of these functions can be associated with specific microvascular segments. Because intravascular pressure measurements demonstrate that a major fraction of total pressure dissipation occurs in precapillary microvessels, the arterioles are designated as resistance vessels. At any given moment local and extrinsic stimuli impinge on the wall of the arteriole and exert control over the calibre of the microvessel.
In so doing these signals modulate the blood flow through the tissue.
The capillaries are the major exchange vessels; across the surface of the microvessels flow all the nutrients required to sustain the cells of the body. In most tissues only a fraction of the capillaries (precapillary sphincter) are perfused under normal conditions and exert cos.trol over the number of perfused capillaries. Finally, the venules are classified as capacitance vessels because most of the tissue blood volume is localized in these microvessels.

Whatever the chemical change may be, more blood flows to the tissue and/or increased cellular metabolism subsides. Thus, changes in substrate and certain metabolite or chemical concentrations are reversed, inhibition or attenuation to the microvascular smooth cells is removed, and an appropriate local vasoconstriction then takes place, restoring blood flow to normal. The purpose of this chapter is to provide an overview of the control mechanisms that modulate microcirculatory dynamics in dental pulp.
Although it is often difficult in biological systems to define the controlled variables, we emphasize the role of humoral and chemical substances in local regulation. The effect of vasoactive substances and its antagonists is considered mainly in the context of pulpal blood flow measured by laser doppler flowmetry.

9.2 LOCAL MICROCIRCULATORY CONTROL SYSTEM

Many tissues of the body are endowed with the capacity to regulate their own microvasculature after removal of the organ from the organism and elimination of all nervous and hormonal influences. These local or intrinsic microvascular control systems allow the microcirculation to respond to the specific needs of the tissue that it subserves. Local vascular reactions can be elicited by applying a number of stresses to the isolated tissue. The most commonly used perturbations are alterations in arterial pressure, venous pressure, arterial oxygen concentration and parenchymal activity.

9.2.1 Autoregulation of blood flow

Local control phenomenon brought about by changes in arterial perfusion pressure elicit local resistance responses that tend to stabilize flow, in spite of altered input pressure (Norris et al., 1979), is known as autoregulation of blood flow. In recent years a great deal of attention has been given to autoregulation and its presence in various organs of the body. We now know, for example, that virtually all organs of the body show some degree of autoregulation. In certain organs the response is extremely pronounced; flow is virtually constant in brain and kidney over a range of perfusion pressure from 90 to 160 mmHg (Crawford et al., 1959). Among the several mechanisms proposed to explain this phenomenon, the following would seem to have most experimental support (Johnson, 1964):

1. A direct myogenic contractile response to stretch of the arterial smooth muscle.
2. Metabolic factors acting on the arteriolar smooth muscle.
3. Changes in tissue pressure related to changes in capillary filtration rate.

9.2.3 Hypoxemic hyperaemia and functional hyperaemia

In some tissues perfusion with blood containing a lower than normal concentration of oxygen elicits an intrinsic vasodilation; the resultant flow increase is termed hypoxemic hyperaemia. The rate of blood flow is enhanced in most isolated organs when the functional activity of the tissue is increased; this local control phenomenon is known as functional hyperaemia. There is no evidence for the presence of the two phenomena mentioned above in dental pulp itself.

### 9.3 REMOTE CONTROL OF THE MICROCIRCULATION

Some of the control signals impinging on the smooth muscle cells of microvessels originate outside of the region subserved by a particular microvascular bed. These signals, in general, originate from brain and endocrine glands. These nervous and hormonal control systems act on pre- and postcapillary microvessels in an effort to stabilize a systemic variable, such as blood pressure, which is of primary importance for the survival of the whole organism. Therefore, remote control systems tend to ignore the needs of the individual tissues subserved by individual microvascular beds.

### 9.3.1 Nervous control

Autonomic nervous influence on the microvasculature is most evident in the arterioles, which have the most completely developed muscular coat of the resistance vessels and are usually richly innervated. Through counterpoised actions producing either vasoconstriction or vasodilation, these influences provide a mechanism for exquisite control of local vascular resistance by the central nervous system. Since arterioles usually supply blood to several capillary networks in the microvasculature, the control exerted on the arterioles by the autonomic nervous system at the microcirculatory level extends to relatively broad blocks of tissue, in that increasing or decreasing flow through an arteriole increases or decreases the hydrostatic pressure and the blood available to a number of downstream metarterioles and capillaries. Arterioles are probably capable of complete closure, in contrast to larger upstream arteries which are not.
Sympathetic nerves have been identified along vessels in the dental pulps of many mammals, including man (Pohto and Antila, 1968). In the sympathetic nerve terminal located several hundred angstroms from the smooth muscle cell, norepinephrine is synthesized from tyrosine and stored in membrane-bound vesicles (von Euler, 1972). The ratelimiting reaction is catalysed by tyrosine hydroxylase. Regulation of transmitter synthesis is achieved via feedback inhibition of tyrosine hydroxylase by norepinephrine (von Euler, 1972). Thus, a reduction of the norepinephrine pool in the terminal leads to a stimulation of transmitter synthesis and a return of norepinephrine to control levels.
The release of norepinephrine into the neurovascular gap is initiated by the arrival of an action potential at the terminal. Upon entering the neurovascular gap, the transmitter may (1) bind to the smooth muscle receptor, (2) re-enter the terminal via an active transport mechanism, (3) enter the vascular smooth muscle cell for inactivation, and (4) diffuse away from the gap into the blood capillaries (FoIkow et al., 1967).

The role of neural control of microcirculatory function in whole-body homeostasis can best be appreciated by considering the microvascular reactions elicited via the baroreceptor, chemoreceptor and volume reflexes. As can be seen in Fig. 9.4, a reduction in systemic arterial pressure causes a decrease in baroreceptor firing rate, which in turn initiates a neurogenic constriction of peripheral arterioles and precapillary sphincters (Mellander and Johansson, 1968). Consequently, capillary pressure and filtration area fall, fluid is absorbed into the capillaries, blood volume rises, and cardiac output is accelerated. Arteriolar constriction also leads to an elevation of total peripheral resistance. The increases in cardiac output and total peripheral resistance allow the systemic arterial pressure to return toward control. The pulpal sympathetic vasoconstrictor fibres are controlled through the carotid baroreceptor reflex (Tonder, 1975).

### 9.3.2 Endocrine control

The major endocrine organs involved in cardiovascular homeostasis are the adrenal medulla, the posterior pituitary gland and the kidney.
Unfortunately, there are few reports about remote endocrine influence on pulpal microcirculatory haemodynainics. Circulating norepinephrine or epinephrine exerts less vasoconstrictor influence than activation of the appropriate sympathetic nerves. Thus, the influence of circulating catecholamines released from adrenal medulla are small in pulpal microcirculation (Edwall, 1980).

Intra-arterial infusion of the vasodilators (acetylcholine, histamine, isoproterenol and bradykinin) in dogs produces a decrease in pulpal blood flow with marked increase in external carotid arterial blood flow (Fig. 9.5). These effects, except that of bradykinin, are inhibited by the specific antagonists pretreated via the external carotid artery. Heyeraas (1985) has proposed an explanation for the reduction of pulpal blood flow instead of the increase in blood flow in response to vasodilators, i.e. the stealing of blood flow by the,adjacent tissues undergoing severe vasodilation, suggesting that blood flow in the dental pulp may be controlled by mechanisms located outside the pulp. This indicates that changed blood flow in neighbouring tissues which are supplied by the same feeding arteries or drained by the same veins as the dental pulp may alter the prepulpal or postpulpal pressure drop and thereby change blood flow (Heyeraas, 1985).

### 9.5 AXON REFLEX

Arwill (1958) found two types of unmyelinated nerve fibres in the human dental pulp, but only one of them was supposed to be of autonomic origin and located in the vascular wall. A close connection of sympathetic fibres with pulpal vessels and a cholinesterase-positive nerve fibre network on some blood vessels have been demonstrated (Pohto and Antila, 1968). It has also been demonstrated that histamine participates in the vasomotor regulation in pulpal circulation (Pohto and
Antila, 1970; Edwall et al., 1973), and that electrical stimulation of the inferior alveolar nerve produces an atropine-resistant vasodilation and a release of substance P immunoreactivity in the pulp (Rosell et al.,
1981; Gazelius and Olgart, 1980). This effect, which is probably mediated via pain fibres, seems to be related to the axonal reflex mechanism.
Such mechanism may contribute to vasodilation in the dental pulp (Gazelius and Olgart, 1980; Edwall, 1980; Okabe et al., 1988). The significance of this mechanism of microcirculatory function in dental pulp remains to be examined.

### 9.6 ENDOTHELIUM-DEPENDENT FUNCTION

Furchgott and Zawadzki (1980) discovered that the relaxation by acetylcholine of isolated preparations of rabbit thoracic aorta and several other large and medium-sized precontracted arteries is dependent on the presence of endothelium. This endothelium-dependent relaxation is mediated through the release of an unstable endogenous vasodilator, endothelium-derived relaxing factor (EDRF) (Furchgott, 1983), which has been shown to be nitric oxide (Palmer et al., 1987). The loss of a relaxation response observed in conduit and muscular arteries to the agents summarized in Table 9.2 coincides with endothelium disruption.
Two mechanisms of release may be important in the regulation of resistance vessel tone since EDRF is known to be released from the endothelium of conduit vessels either basally (Griffith et al., 1984) or as a result of pharmacological stimulation (Furchgott and Zawadzki, 1980;
Furchgott, 1983). Basal EDRF activity is enhanced by fluid flow (Rubanyi et al., 1986) and influences constrictor and relaxant responses in conduit vessels (Pohl and Busse, 1987). Such basal activity modulates myogenic tone in resistance vessels (Griffith et al., 1987). The interaction of basally released EDRF with myogenic responses in an intact vascular network might be one of its major physiological functions. ### 10.1 INTRODUCTION

The dental pulp is a complex organ, in which microcirculation and sensory nerves are encased in a rigid structure of dentine and enamel creating a low-compliance environment. In the study of pulp haemodynamics two important questions are asked: how is flow perfusion regulated in this low-compliance system, and what is the link between the flow regulation and pathophysiology of the pulp? It is a well-known fact that microcirculation plays an important role in the initiation and progression of pulpal disorders and that the alteration in microcirculatory function is one of the first signs of inflammation. In this chapter we present some of the research findings addressing these two questions.

### 10.2 CIRCULATORY METHODS FOR DETERMINATION OF

### PULPAL HAEMODYNAMIC PARAMETERS

Various methods have been used in the past to measure pulpal blood flow (Table 10.1). In our studies we used two approaches: microcirculatory and macrocirculatory methods. In macrocirculatory investigations pulpal blood flow (PBF), in millilitres per minute per 100g tissue, is measured in canine teeth of young mongrel dogs with 133Xe washout and 15 fl-m radioisotope-labelled microsphere injection methods, and relative PBF changes are measured by laser Doppler flowmetry (Kim et al., 1983, 1986; Meyer, 1970; Gazelius et al., 1986; Liu, 1987) (Fig. 10.1).
In microcirculatory studies, vessel diameters and red cell velocities are measured in rat incisor teeth and are used to calculate volumetric flow rates (Kim et al., 1984a) (Figs 10.2 and 10.3). In a number of experiments intravital fluorescence microscopy is used to assess the permeability characteristics of pulpal microvessels.

10.2.1 133Xe. washout method

A solution of 0.2 ml of 133Xe suspended in saline is injected into the external carotid artery via a three-way stopcock. Radioactivity from the pulp is monitored with a scintillation probe from the tooth surface and the activity is displayed on a multichannel analyser. At la-second intervals the activities are fed into a PDP 11123 minicomputer, which is programmed to plot the activities on a semi-logarithmic scale against a linear time scale and to resolve this activity-time curve into two components. The exponential rate constant of the 133Xe washout curve (k) is then determined from each component. The blood flow per unit

### 10.2.2 The radioisotope-labelled microsphere injection

### method

Radionuclides 57CO, 113Sn, 103Ru, 95Nb and 46SC labelled micro spheres (MS) in 10% dextran solution with sphere sizes 15.0 ± 1.0 j.Lm are used.
The MS suspension is injected into the left ventricle via a cardiac catheter immediately after thorough mixing. While an arterial reference blood sample is drawn from the abdominal aorta, donor whole blood is infused at the same rate with an infusion-withdrawal pump. At the end of the experiment the pulp is removed and weighed. The tissue specimen is then placed in a counting vial and its radioactivity is determined by a gamma counter. The weight of each tissue sample is entered into a PDP 11/23 minicomputer run by an RSX 11-M operating system. It is programmed to resolve the radioactivity of each isotope and to calculate the radioactivity per 100 g tissue sample (Ct), the flow rate per 100 g of tissue sample (PBF) and the cardiac output (CO) with equations given below:

The laser Doppler flowmeter (LDF) was developed by applying the unique characteristics of laser light to the Doppler effect (Holloway,
1983). The Doppler effect occurs when a frequency of an electromagnetic

Reflected laser light from the stationary tissues (reference beam) and moving red blood cells (Doppler-shifted beam) are collected by a receiving fibre-optic, which runs parallel 1 mm from the transmitting fibre-optic. The resulting photocurrent of the reflected laser light, which is proportional to the instantaneous velocity of the moving red cells, is collected on the surface of a photodetector and measured indirectly by a process called optical heterodyning.
The present LDF does not provide absolute quantitative pulpal blood flow values; rather it measures relative flow changes instantaneously and continuously. The main advantages of the method is its noninvasiveness, the continuous and instantaneous feedback of relative flow changes and its potential for use in humans for clinical purposes (Gazelius et al., 1986). As shown in Fig. 2, an LDF recording from an intact human central incisor corresponds well with EeG, indicating that
LDF recording represents the true. In animal experiments PBF recordings in response to heat registered an increase, while there was no change in systemic blood pressures (Fig. 3). Furthermore, LDF is an excellent method with which to measure PBF and sensory nerve activity in cat teeth simultaneously (Markowitz et al., 1988).

### 10.2.4 Intravital microscopy

Briefly, the incisor tooth of an anaesthetized rat is prepared with burs and hand instruments using copious water spray. The tooth is prepared to within 25-30 I-lm remaining dentine covering the pulp. The animal is placed under an intravital microscope apparatus for direct observations and measurements of haemodynamic parameters by both transillumination with a xenon lamp and epi-illumination with dual fibre-optics (Fig.
10.4). Two of the haemodynamic parameters measured are: vessel diameter, which is determined by an image-shearing monitor (Intaglietta and Tompkins, 1973); and red cell intravascular velocity, which is measured on-line by a variation of the 'two-slit' photometric technique (Tompkins et al., 1974, Wayland and Johnson, 1967). From the vessel diameter and the mean red cell velocity the volumetric flow rate, in cubic millimetres per second, can then be calculated.
