## Concluding remarks

L. M. OLGART

When reading the two previous chapters one is struck by the growing number of putative mediators of blood flow regulation in the pulp. The concept that pain fibres have a 'motor' function may seem strange to many of us. Nevertheless, they can bring about powerful vascular effects when activated, mainly in connection with a painful and/or noxious stimulation of the tooth. It is therefore of particular interest that tachykinin/CGRP-immunoreactive fibres are present in the pulpdentine border as shown by Drs Akai and Wakisaka. This finding indicates that not only A-fibres but also C-fibres are located in a position to receive information from external stimuli affecting the dentine and odontoblasts. This strategic position of C-fibre nerve endings may explain previous observations that stress forces applied to a cat tooth, causing mechanical deformation of dentine, result in prompt vasodilation in the pulp (Olgart et al., 1988) similar to that seen when electrical pulses are delivered to the sensory nerves or the tooth crown. So far, most evidence shows that motor effects exerted by the sensory nerves are induced by external stimuli. However, there are indications (Olgart and Gazelius, 1988) that the sensory peptides are intermittently released in the pulp, i.e. without any obvious insult. Thus, it is possible that the tachykinins exert a continuous influence on pulpal vessels and interact with other regulatory systems in maintaining an appropriate haemodynamic function during 'normal' conditions. Further evidence for this unorthodox view is needed. In this context, it must be remembered that in addition to their vasomotor role the peptide-containing sensory nerves may play other local roles. Several years ago Dr Avery and coworkers suggested a neural influence on dentine formation (Avery,
1975). They found some evidence for this idea by comparing tissue responses to external irritation in IAN-denervated and normal teeth in rabbits. It is possible that their results could partly be explained by trophic effects exerted by neuronal peptides on adjacent cells. ## Neurotrophic substances

J.P. NAFTEL

### 24.1 INTRODUCTION

Interactions between peripheral tissues and the neurons that innervate them involve more than the mechanisms of sensory and motor transduction. There are also mutual trophic dependencies regulating structure and function of both partners. The term neurotrophism has been used to denote such influences both on and by peripheral neurons (Drachman, 1974), but the present discussion will focus on only one of the two directions - regulatory influences of target tissues on the development and maintenance of peripheral neurons. Evidence of trophic influences by dental nerves on cells of the pulp have been considered elsewhere (see Chiego et ai., 1986; Dubner et ai., 1978;
Silverman and Kruger, 1987).
Since the discovery of nerve growth factor (NGF) almost 40 years ago (Levi-Montalcini and Hamburger, 1951) the idea that peripheral tissues express trophic factors regulating development and maintenance of their innervation has gained substantial support. It must be acknowledged that the present understanding of the functions of these neurondirected proteins has come from studies of systems other than the tooth and its innervation, but many of the findings elsewhere can be expected to apply to the pulp. Indeed, a dependence on target-produced factors has been most clearly demonstrated for two functional types of ganglionic neurons that are prominent in the innervation of the pulp, i.e.
postganglionic sympathetic and small-diameter, neuropeptidecontaining sensory neurons. A considerable portion of this chapter will comprise an overview of the current understanding of factors mediating peripheral regulation of neurons in sensory and sympathetic ganglia.
Evidence for dental pulp influences on the neurons that innervate the pulp will then be reviewed, and finally results of initial attempts to identify neuronal growth factors in the pulp will be discussed.

### 24.2 GANGLIONIC NEURONS AND TROPHIC FACTORS

### 24.2.1 Functions of NGF

Although other proteins promoting survival and growth of ganglionic neurons have been identified, NGF is currently the only such factor for which physiological functions have been clearly demonstrated and partially-defined. Consequently, NGF has come to be regarded as the model neuronotrophic factor (Varon and Skaper, 1983). Since the details of its chemical structure, molecular biology and biological effects are available in numerous excellent reviews (e.g., Levi-Montalcini, 1987;
Shooter et al., 1981; Varon and Skaper, 1983), the emphasis here will be on recent advances bearing on the proposed functions of NGF in the biology of ganglionic neurons.

(a) Regulation of ganglionic neuron survival

Most populations of central and peripheral neurons proceed through a characteristic sequence of stages during development (Oppenheim,
1981):

1. Mitosis of neuroblasts produces an excess of immature neurons.
2. Axons extend from the developing neurons into target tissues.
3. A substantial number of neurons (up to 70%) die.
4. Neuron numbers and peripheral, nerve fibre numbers stabilize.

A prevalent view of the mechanism of this sculpturing process is that the targets of the developing neurons produce limiting amounts of essential trophic factors which are taken up by the developing axons and transported to the neuron somas. Death is the fate of those neurons that fail to compete for an adequate source of trophic support through their peripheral contacts (Berg, 1982; Clarke, 1985; Oppenheim, 1985).
Considerable evidence has accumulated in support of NGF as a trophic messenger molecule mediating target-controlled survival of developing sympathetic and sensory ganglion neurons. For example, ganglionic neurons that normally would be lost in the process of naturally occurring cell death can be rescued by administration of exogenous NGF (Levi-Montalcini and Booker, 1960a; Hamburger et al.,
1981). The essential role of NGF in regulating survival of ganglionic neurons during development has been verified by studies in which availability of endogenous NGF was blocked by presence of anti-NGF antibodies. Antibody-mediated NGF deprivation results in destruction of most sympathetic and neural crest-derived sensory ganglionic neurons during their respective periods of naturally occurring cell death

Several defined and undefined substances promote neurite growth in vitro, but do not directly support neuron survival. Many of these agents act by binding to the surface of the culture vessel, thereby creating a more adhesive substrate (Davis et al., 1985). Neurite growth-promoting activity has been detected in media conditioned by a variety of cell types in culture and in tissue extracts. The number of different agents involved in the activity from these sources is not yet known, but extracellular matrix components (most prominently laminin) have been implicated by their wide distribution and their demonstrated neuritepromoting effects in vitro (Davis et al., 1985).

### 24.2.3 Trophic dependence of trigeminal ganglion neurons

The available information on the response of trigeminal ganglion neurons to trophic factors is of particular interest, since it is a subpopulation of these neurons that provides sensory innervation to the pulp.
Although the embryonic origin of trigeminal ganglion neurons in mammals is still a matter of controversy (Altman and Bayer, 1982), avian trigeminal ganglion neurons are clearly derived from two sources - neural crest cells and ectodermal placode cells (D'Amico-Martel and
Noden, 1983; Hamburger, 1961). In embryonic trigeminal ganglia of the

In offspring of mothers with high titres of anti-NGF antibodies, trigeminal ganglia contain a mean of only 9400 neurons compared to 51000 in controls (Pearson et al., 1983). Spinal ganglia sustain a similar (80-85%) depletion in neuronal numbers under these conditions (Johnson et al., 1983). Some of the deficits accompanying the sensory neuron loss are particularly noteworthy. Lumsden and Davies (1983) have found that the outgrowth of neurites from embryonic mouse trigeminal ganglia in vitro is selectively directed toward regionally appropriate peripheral targets. This specificity is not diminished by blocking NGF action with antiserum, and further studies in vivo have revealed that synthesis of NGF in the maxillary process of the mouse and the expression of NGF receptors by trigeminal neurons begins after, rather than before, the first developing axons reach the maxillary area (Davies et al., 1987; Davies and Lumsden, 1984). Thus, it appears that NGF acts as a selective maintenance factor mediating target-controlled survival of trigeminal ganglion neurons, but factors other than NGF mediate target-directed guidance of pioneer trigeminal axons.

### 24.3.1 Special features of pulp innervation development

The protracted development of the dentition implies that the processes involved in development of the pulpal innervation continue after completion of corresponding events for other oral-facial structures (see
Ch. 1). Fried (1982) found that, in the cat, development of the primary mandibular incisors and their innervation occurs long after the reduction and stabilization of inferior alveolar nerve fibre numbers. Fried (1982) concluded that the parent axons of intradental nerve fibres must be in place in the mandible long before there are dental territories to innervate. Thus, unlike neighbouring tissues, development of the intradental innervation does not correspond temporally to the period when other peripheral fields are being established by the trigeminal nerve. It seems, then, that the developing dental pulp must recruit its innervation from neurons that have long since survived the period of cell death associated with competition for peripheral target fields. This recruitment could be mediated by production of sprouting and guidance (tropic) factors by the developing pulp.
Similarly, expression of such factors in developing permanent pulps could induce the innervation changes that accompany the transition from the primary to the permanent dentition. At least some of the sensory nerve fibres that enter developing permanent teeth develop as branches ofaxons supplying deciduous teeth (Brenan, 1986). The capacity of developing pulps of permanent teeth to actively promote development of their innervation is convincingly demonstrated by the fact that they become innervated even when transplanted to ectopic sites such as the submandibular subcutis or the leg (Erdelyi et ai., 1987).

### 24.3.2 Reinnervation of denervated pulp

The readiness with which denervated pulps become reinnervated is consistent with a role of the pulp in promoting and guiding sprouting of damaged axons. Section of the inferior alveolar nerve in rats (Berger and Byers, 1983; Berger et ai., 1983) and in cats (Fried and Erdelyi, 1982;
Holland and Robinson, 1985, 1986; Robinson, 1980, 1981) is followed by regeneration of pulpal nerve fibres demonstrable by both morphological and electrophysiological methods. Such regeneration does not necessarily require an active role by the pulp since reinnervation might be attributed to an inherent drive of sectioned nerve fibres to regenerate

More compelling evidence of an active role of the pulp in promoting its innervation derives from observations that denervated pulps can become reinnervated even when regenerating nerve fibres are not available. This was convincingly demonstrated by Robinson and

Since this requires collateral sprouting ofaxons from uninjured nerves and establishment of new nerve pathways to the canine pulp, the reinnervation of the pulp under these circumstances cannot be fully accounted for by a central drive of regenerating axons or by guidance by degenerating nerve trunks.
Robinson (1986) has confirmed that the stimulus for reinnervation of the pulp by collateral sprouting has a peripheral rather than central origin. Trigeminal rhizotomy contralateral to the side of inferior alveolar nerve section does not prevent extension of collateral sprouts from nerves on the side of the rhizotomy to the denervated canine and incisors on the side of the inferior alveolar nerve section.
Still further indications that the pulp can promote its own innervation come from observations of reinnervation of mature teeth that have been extracted and then reimplanted or transplanted (Holland and Robinson,
1987a; Robinson, 1983).

The pulp has not been tested for content or synthesis of NGF, but a beginning has been made in identifying factors in the pulp that may function in regulating the neurons that provide its innervation. The approach has been to test pulp extracts for neuronotrophic activity using conventional in vitro bioassays based on ganglionic neurons from chick embryos. ### 24.5 CONCLUSION

Considering the evident importance of NGF and other neuronal trophic factors in the life history of both sympathetic ganglionic neurons and trigeminal ganglion neurons (most notably thin fibre neurons), it is somewhat surprising that there has been little direct investigation of neuronotrophic activity in the pulp. The relative isolation conferred by a hard tissue encasement and the relative absence of potentially confounding contributions by somatic motor, parasympathetic and large mechanoreceptive sensory fibres recommend the pulp for such studies.
The limited studies that have been done indicate that the pulp contains potent neuronotrophic activity for ganglionic neurons. The factors responsible for this activity may be involved in regulating development, maintenance and regeneration of the pulpal innervation, although their relevance to these functions remains to be established. Silverman and
Kruger (1987) have recently argued convincingly that the functions of intradental afferent nerve fibres must extend beyond nociception and blood flow regulation. This emphasizes the importance of seeking an understanding of the interactions between the pulp and its innervation.
It seems safe to predict that a future elucidation of the role of NGF and other neuronal trophic factors in the pulp will contribute to understanding some of the intriguing peculiarities of the pulpal innervation.

### 25.1 INTRODUCTION

Morphine, an opium alkaloid derived from the poppy, Papaver s011111iferum, has been one of the most commonly used analgesics in both clinical and experimental fields despite its addictive properties. In 1975, after a specific opiate receptor had been demonstrated, two endogenous opioid peptides, methionine enkephalin (Met-enk) and leucine enkephalin (Leu-enk), which bind specifically to the same receptor as do opiates such as morphine, were found in the mammalian brain. Since then, many new opioid peptides have been found not only in the central nervous system but also in peripheral organs such as the adrenal gland and intestines. In physiological and pharmacological studies of opioid peptides it has generally been found that these endogenous peptides have analgesic properties.
In 1981, we discovered Met-enk and Leu-enk by immunohistochemical techniques in the dental pulps of dogs and rats (Kudo et al., 1981).
Moreover, it has been demonstrated in our laboratory that the noxious stimulation of pulp leads to the formation of inflammatory mediators such as histamine, bradykinin and prostaglandin in the tissue.
Bradykinin, in particular, is well known as one of the most potent algesic (pain-producing) substances.
The purpose of this chapter is to illuminate the relationship between bradykinin and opioid peptides in dental pulp and their relation to dental pain.

### 25.5 PRECURSOR OF MET-ENKEPHALIN IN DENTAL PULP

Kudo et al. (1983a) further investigated the presence of the Met-enk precursor in rat incisor pulp using an indirect immunofluorescence antibody method.
A specimen of rat tooth pulp was digested with trypsin and then incubated with FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit serum to observe immunoreactive Met-enk-like peptides under a fluorescent microscope.
Dense fluorescence was observed in the trypsin-treated pulp; i.e. Metenk-like immunoreactivity in the trypsin-treated pulp was considerably higher than in the untreated pulp. This suggested that the precursor for the Met-enk-like peptide was localized in the protoplasm of pulp cells.
