However, three reaction steps are irreversible, i.e those catalysed by hexokinase, phosphofructokinase and pyruvate kinase, for in each of those reactions there occurs a large negative freeenergy change, and thus they are bypassed by alternative enzymecatalysed reactions. Thus, to control glucose metabolism - both glycolysis and gluconeogenesis - these irreversible steps must be precisely regulated. In fact, these enzymes are known to have allosteric properties and their activities are elegantly regulated by various modifiers, including metabolites in the glycolytic pathway and in the TCA cycle.
On the other hand, in tissues such as skeletal muscle which can function under anaerobic conditions and in mature erythrocytes which lack mitochondria, glycolysis can become a major source of cellular ATP.
In this case, pyruvate is converted to lactate by the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase. This reaction is thought to be essential to the glycolytic process by regenerating NAD+, which is required as a cofactor for the reaction catalysed by glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase. This is evidenced by the observation (Kitamura and Nishina, 1975) that mature erythrocytes with an extremely low level of lactate dehydrogenase activity accumulate abnormally high levels of glyceraldehyde-3phosphate, dihydroxyacetone phosphate and fructose-1,6-diphosphate, which would be expected to be observed in glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency.
In the following chapters we will review the detailed properties of phosphofructokinase, pyruvate kinase and lactate dehydrogenase of mammalian dental pulp and of other organs. Glycolysis in the dental pulp, which is surrounded by hard tissues, and the relationship between glycolytic activity and the development and growth of teeth are of particular interest, because dental pulp has been claimed to play an important role in eruption, resorption and mineralization of teeth (Siquara-Da-Rocha and Nicolau, 1980; Eccles, 1965; Furseth, 1968;
Tsuruta et al., 1974).

### 13.2.1 Characteristics of PFK

(a) Isozymes and molecular properties

The molecular weight of most mammalian PFKs is in the range of
300000-500000 daltons. The presence of PFK isozymes was first implicated by certain characteristics of an inherited muscular disease in which the patient lacks muscle PFK activity, but has the normal level of the enzyme activity in white blood cells and half of the normal level in erythrocytes (Tarui et ai., 1965; Layzer et ai., 1967). Thereafter, the nature of the isozymes has been the subject of many investigations.
Electrophoretic mobility, chromatographic behaviour, kinetic properties and immunochemical reactivity are used to identify the isozymes.
Now, three types of PFK subunits are known: they are M (or A) type,
L (or B) type and C (or X) type. Phosphofructokinase consists of four subunits, and the homo tetra me ric form of M (M4) and of L (L4) are the predominant isozymes in skeletal muscle and liver, respectively (Kemp,
1975). The C4 was the last isozyme type isolated in pure form (Foe and
Kemp, 1985) because the C subunit is not expressed alone but occurs only in hybrid mixtures with M (A) and/or L (B) (Tsai and Kemp, 1973;
Dunaway et ai., 1986). The molecular weights of L, M and C subunits were estimated to be 80000, 85000 and 87500, respectively, for rat PFK (Foe and Kemp, 1984), and 80000, 84000 and 86000, respectively, for rabbit brain enzyme (Dunaway et ai., 1986). In human, the location of the genes for M (A) type, L (B) type and C type subunits have been assigned to chromosomes 1 (V ora et ai., 1982), 21 (V ora and Francke,
1981) and 10 (Wei! et ai., 1980; Vora et ai., 1983), respectively.
Poormen et ai. (1984) analysed the amino acid sequences of rabbit muscle and bacterial (Bacillus strarothermophilus) PFK. From the sequence homologies between bacterial and rabbit muscle PFK and between amino- and carboxy-terminal halves of the muscle enzyme, they proposed the interesting hypothesis that the mammalian PFK evolved from a prokaryotic progenitor by gene duplication and divergence. Recently,
Lee et ai. (1987) isolated the gene for rabbit muscle PFK and determined the nucleotide sequence for all of the exons and most of the introns.
They examined the above hypothesis based on the gene structure;
however, it is difficult to interpret the evolutionary significance of the intron/exon organization in the rabbit muscle PFK gene since the origin of introns in eukaryotic genes is still speculative.

In vitro, PFK is demonstrated to be phosphorylated by the catalytic subunit of cAMP-dependent protein kinase, and the phosphorylation site has also been determined (Kemp et ai., 1981). It is of great interest to know whether phosphorylation of PFK can modify its catalytic properties. There have been several reports of hormonal regulation of
PFK by a mechanism that may involve a phosphorylation (Castano et ai., 1979; Hosey et a/., 1980; Furuya and Uyeda, 1980); however, this has not been confirmed (Claus et ai., 1982).

### 13.4.1 Characteristics of LDH

(a) Isozymes and molecular properties

Lactate dehydrogenase is a tetrameric enzyme of molecular weight of
135000 daltons. Initially, two types of subunits, M (or A) and H (or B);
were known. According to the possible combination of these subunits, there are five LDH isozymes: LDH-1 (H4), LDH-2 (H3M), LDH-3 (H2M2),
LDH-4 (HM3) and LDH-5 (M4). Lactate dehydrogenase and LDH-2 are major isozymes in heart, brain and erythrocytes; i.e. these organs are rich in the H subunit. On the other hand, skeletal muscle and liver are rich in the M subunit; i.e. LDH-5 and LDH-4 are the major isozyme.
Later, another type of isozyme, LDH-X, which consists of four C type subunits, was found to be present in testis and spermatozoa of mammals and birds (Holbrook et a!., 1975). It is thought that LDH genes evolution ally appeared in the order of M ~H
~C (Holbrook et a!., 1975).
Chromosomal location of human LDH genes for M and H subunits was assigned to chromosomes 11 and 12, respectively (Holbrook et a!., 1975).
Amino acid sequences of M (A) and H (B) type subunits were determined by Eventoff et al. (1977); and the C type sequence, by Pan et al.
(1980). Lactate dehydrogenase has been purified and crystallized from various sources as listed by Pesce et al. (1967). Holbrook et a!. (1975) discussed the precise three-dimensional structure of the enzyme. The amino acid sequence of human LDH-C has also been deduced from a cDNA sequence (Millan et a!., 1987). From the amino acid sequence homology that exists around the catalytic centres of LDH and mitochondrial and cytosolic malate dehydrogenases, Birktoft et a!. (1982) suggested that these three dehydrogenases are derived from a common ancestral gene and probably have similar catalytic mechanisms.
Most recently, Wistow et al. (1987) reported the interesting discovery that E-crystallin, a lens-specific protein in many birds and crocodiles, is an active LDH (LDH-1 (H4)). It appears that LDH has been recruited, unchanged, to an extra role as a structural protein in the lens without gene duplication and sequence divergence (Wistow et aI., 1987).

### 13.4.2 LDH of dental pulp

Since LDH is a rather stable enzyme and can be easily assayed biochemically, and also detected and roughly quantified by histochem\cal methods (Goggins and Fullmer, 1.966; Szakal and Han, 1967), this enzyme has been the most well-studied one among those in dental pulp.
The studies on dental pulp LDH can be separated into two main categories: (a) those for clarifying the properties of dental pulp LDH and the characteristics of glycolytic metabolism in the tissue (Linde and
Ljunggren, 1970a,b, Siquara-Da-Rocha and Nicolau, 1980; Bell and
Larmas, 1978); and (b) those for examining the effect on tissues or cells of various agents such as antibiotics (Szakal and Han, 1967), formaldehyde-containing drugs (Mejare et al., 1976), calcium hydroxide (Gordon et al., 1985), local anaesthetics (Engstrom and Rockert, 1980), as well as the effect of cavity preparation and insertion of medicaments into cavities (Hasselgren and Tronstad, 1977). In the latter category,
LDH is used as a marker to estimate the effect or toxicity of the drugs.

### SUMMARY

Rabbit odontoblast-like cells of an established cell line (RP) were shown to secrete substantial amounts of latent collagenase in both serumsupplemented and serum-free cultures. Purified RP cell collagenase revealed three bands of molecular weight 57kD, 52.5kD and 45kD in
50S-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Hybridomas were prepared according to a standard procedure and four monoclonal antibodies (2A6,
F9, 2A2 and 2E2) against purified RP cell collagenase were consequently isolated and characterized by various criteria. Western blot analysis of secreted RP cell collagenase with these monoclonal antibodies indicated that the 57kD, 52.5kD and 45kD proteins were all RP cell collagenase species sharing the same epitopes. The possible role of collagenase synthesized by odontoblast-like cells in the degradation of collagen of pathological dental pulp tissue was discussed. Potential applications for this RP cell culture model and the isolated monoclonal antibodies were reviewed.

### 14.1 INTRODUCTION

Degradation of collagen tissue appears to be mediated predominantly by an extracellular pathway involving collagenase. Various types of cells

produce collagenase under physiological and pathological conditions (Woolley, 1984). Collagenase from oral tissue was first demonstrated in the culture medium of human gingival tissue (Fullmer and Gibson,
1966). Since the destruction of collagenous tissue is a main pathological feature of periodontal diseases, the possible role of collagenase in the disease has been proposed (Birkedal-Hansen, 1980). Although a few microorganisms at the site of the lesions are known to produce bacterial collagenase, they are not believed to be of major importance in periodontal collagen breakdown. It has been demonstrated that both inflamed and healthy gingival tissues are able to display a substantial tissue collagenase activity in culture (for a review, see Birkedal-Hansen, 1980).
There is now growing evidence indicating that tissue collagenase is involved in the destruction of collagen tissue in periodontal diseases (Sakamoto and Sakamoto, 1988).
Although collagen turnover remains less studied in dental pulp compared to periodontal tissues, degradation of pulpal collagen tissue appears also to be mediated by tissue collagenase (Hutchins and
Mailman, 1983). Collagenase has been demonstrated in serum-free culture medium of bovine dental pulp explants (Kishi et al., 1979) and in inflamed human dental pulp (Morand et al., 1981). However, in these studies, the cellular source of dental pulp collagenase was not clear.
Kishi and Hayakawa (1985) demonstrated the synthesis of latent collagenase by fibroblast-like cells isolated from bovine dental pulp.
Sakamoto et ai. (1985) reported a high level of collagenase synthesis by odontoblast-like cells which had been initially cloned from rabbit dental pulp cells by Sato and Ozawa (1975).
In addition to the synthesis of collagenase, various tissues and cells simultaneously produce an inhibitor of this enzyme. It has been suggested that collagenase inhibitor is involved in regulating collagenase activity in vitro and in vivo (Sellers and Murphy, 1981). Indeed, collagenase inhibitor as well as collagenase have been found in the culture medium of dental pulp explants (Kishi and Hayakawa, 1984) and fibroblast-like cells isolated from bovine dental pulp (Kishi and
Hayakawa, 1985).
Recently, collagenase inhibitor isolated from dental pulp tissue has been studied extensively and monoclonal antibodies to this inhibitor have been established (Kodama et ai., 1987).
To provide a molecular insight into the degradation of tissue collagen in dental pulp, we have embarked upon a series of studies of col-
·lagenase synthesized by rabbit odontoblast-like (RP) cells (Sakamoto et al., 1985). This chapter presents the biosynthesis and secretion of collagenase by RP cells, the preparation of monoclonal antibodies against this enzyme and the characterization of secreted RP cell collagenase using these monoclonal antibodies.

### 14.2 EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

### 14.2.1 Rabbit-odontoblast-like (RP) cells

Rabbit odontoblast-like cells were a gift from Dr A. Sa to (Tokyo Medical and Dental University). The RP cell line was initially established by cloning cells from the odontogenic part of a rabbit incisor at a stage before dentine formation (Sato and Ozawa, 1975). During the early passages, RP cells in culture synthesized amorphous dentin-like mineralized tissue as well as dentine matrix (Sato and Ozawa, 1975). However, the RP cells used in this study had undergone more than 100 passages and did not present the above-mentioned phenotype. Rabbit odontoblastlike cells appeared to be spindle-shaped fibroblast-like cells during the logarithmic phase, but formed a thick and tight monolayer of extremely slender and cylindrical cells at confluency.

### 14.2.2 RP cell culture

Rabbit odontoblast-like cells were inoculated at 5 x 104cells/ml in 80-cm2 plastic flasks (Nunc) and grown in 10ml of Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (high glucose, Gibco Laboratories, Grand Island, NY) supplemented with 10% FBS (Irvine Scientific, Irvine, CA), 100 units/ml of penicillin and 100 J..Lg/ml of streptomycin, at 37°C in a fully humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2/air. Medium was changed at 2- and 3-day intervals. Alternatively, in some experiments confluent cultures were rinsed with Hank's BSS and incubation was continued with serum-free medium containing 0.2% lactoalbumin hydrolysates. Conditioned media were centrifuged, pooled and finally stored at - 20°e.

### 14.2.3 Purification of RP cell collagenase

Rabbit odontoblast-like cell collagenase was purified from the pooled medium by a series of chromatographic steps (Sakamoto et al., 1985).
Figure 4.1 depicts briefly the purification procedures that were used in this study.

### 14.2.4 SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis

Samples were diluted with an equal volume of electrophoresis buffer (125 mM Tris, pH 6.8), 4% SDS, 20% glycerol, 0.0025% bromophenol blue, with or without 10% 2-mercaptoethanol. Samples were electrophoresed using a 4% polyacrylamide stacking gel and a 12% polyacrylamide separating gel, pH 8.8, according to Laemmli (1970). Standard size gells (14 x 14 cm) and mini gels (7 x 8 cm) were alternatively used.

### 14.3 RESULTS

### 14.3.1 Production of collagenase by RP cells in culture

Rabbit odontoblast-like cells reached confluency at day 6 under the present culture conditions. Significant amounts of latent collagenase were found in the culture medium harvested at day 9 and enzyme secretion was maximal at day 12 (approx. 0.5 units/ml). When RP cells were maintained with serum-supplemented medium, the cultures continued to produce collagenase for up to 40 days. On the other hand, RP cell cultures incubated with serum-free medium produced comparable amounts of latent collagenase for only up to 6-10 days.

### 14.4 DISCUSSION

The major interest in the cells derived from dental pulp has usually been based on a view that these cells function as a reservoir of stem cells for primary or secondary dentinogenesis. In fact, odontoblasts are considered as the cellular components of dentine since connective and skeletal tissues are generally defined by both their cellular and extracellular components (Hutchins and Mailman, 1983). In this context, the capacity of odontoblast-like cells to produce substantial amounts of collagenase in vitro as presented by this study may give rise to a perplexing problem in dental pulp biology.
The production of collagenase by RP cells under the present experimental conditions appeared to have reached its maximum, since several recognized stimulating agents of collagenase synthesis such as TP A (Aggeler et a/., 1984), PTH and heparin (Sakamoto and Sakamoto, 1984) did not affect their capacity of collagenase synthesis.
