### 29.4.5 pH dependency of BANA-degrading enzymes

### activated by bradykinin

The pH dependency of BANA-degrading enzymes in the dental pulp activated by bradykinin was examined in vitro. It was shown that there were two peaks of bradykinin-induced BANA-degrading enzyme activity at both pH 7 and 8 in whole pulp tissue of the rats, suggesting that two kinds of enzymes exist in the pulp (Kudo et al., 1987). At pH 7 and
8, the enzyme activities cannot, however, be distinguished from each other in experiments using various enzyme inhibitors, as shown by the results described in section 4.3.

### APPENDIX

Recently, we have obtained some new findings on the charact~ristics of enkephalin-processing enzymes in dental pulp of the rat as follows:

1. The enkephalin-processing enzymes of the lysosomal fraction from the pulp tissue have been further investigated using the microsomal or supernatant fractions of the pulp tissue as a source of crude proenkephalin. When incubated with the intact lysosomal fraction, the production of enkephalins from the microsomal fraction was enhanced by des-Arg9-BK, but the enhancing effect was not observed when incubated with the soluble component of the lysosomal fraction.
These results suggest that intact structure of lysosomes is necessary for the activating effect of bradykinin and its analogues. On the other hand, cysteine activated both the enzymes of intact lysosome and its soluble component, indicating that the enkephalin-processing enzymes are cysteine proteinases (Kudo et ai., 1989).
2. The BANA-degrading enzyme activities in the soluble component of the lysosomal fraction were inhibited by E-64, but not by camostat and pepstatin, suggesting that the enzymes are cysteine proteinases.
Sephadex G 75 gel filtration of the soluble component showed that peaks of BANA-degrading and enkephalin-processing activities appeared in the same position (about Mr 23600) of elution. The partially purified BANA-degrading enzymes were markedly activated by cysteine and inhibited by E-64 in a non-competitive manner (Kudo et ai., 1989).
3. The enkephalin-processing enzymes have successfully been investigated immunocytochemically and radioimmunochemically in the cultured dental pulp cells. The cultured cells were fibroblast-like cells. Observation by indirect immunofluorescence method showed that the cells contain enkephalins and its precursor proteins, and the content of enkephalins was enhanced by des-Arg9-BK and cysteine.
The activating effect of des-Arg9-BK was also demonstrated by the radioimmunochemical examination using the cultured pulp cells (unpublished data).

### 30.1 INTRODUCTION

Eicosanoids - oxygenated, biologically active derivatives - are derived from 20-carbon polyunsaturated fatty acids by cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase. These include prostaglandins and leukotrienes, important both as bioregulators and as participants in pathophysiological states including inflammation. Eicosanoids are not stored in tissue cells and are newly synthesized as a result of membrane perturbations that cause the release of free fatty acids, generally arachidonic acid, from esterified phospholipid of membrane present in virtually all mammalian tissue cells. The effects are exerted locally in the tissues where they were produced. The release of arachidonic acid occurs in response to widely divergent physical, chemical and hormonal stimuli. Pathways of formation of prostaglandins and leukotrienes from arachidonic acid are summarized in Fig. 30.1. Possible eicosanoid functions in physiological and pathophysiological processes include: regulation of neurochemical transmitter release, vascular tone and reproduction, contraction or relaxation of smooth muscle, stimulation or inhibition of platelet function, activation of leukocytes, control of growth and/or metastasis of malignant cells, and mediation or modulation of inflammatory and immune responses. A number of mediators appear to participate in the generation of inflammatory reactions. Vane (1971) and Smith and Willis (1971) demonstrated that non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), aspirin and similar drugs inhibit the biosynthesis of prostaglandins. The correlation between the activity of NSAIDs as cyclooxygenase inhibitors and their anti-inflammatory activity is very convincing. Thus, it has been believed that the anti-inflammatory effects of
NSAIDs are mediated via inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis (for review: Vane, 1987).

### 30.2 ARACHIDONIC ACID METABOLISM IN DENTAL PULP

### TISSUES

The presence of the cyclooxygenase system in dental pulp tissue has been suggested by reports that PG-like materials were released from incubated rat dental pulp (Tiirker and Turker, 1974), that PGI2, TxA2'
PGF2c; and PGE2 were released from incubated rat dental pulp (Hirafuji et al., 1980, 1982; Hirafuji and Ogura, 1983, 1987), and that malondialdehyde, an indicator of prostaglandin formation, was produced by incubating the homogenate of rabbit dental pulp (Antila and Pohto,
1984). These studies above demonstrated that the release was blocked by indomethacin. The ability of dental pulp to synthesize prostaglandin is further confirmed by studies which demonstrated the conversion of
[1-14C]arachidonic acid to [1-14C]prostaglandins by homogenates of dental pulp of dogs and rats (Lessard et al., 1986; Okiji et al., 1987) and by microsomes of bovine dental pulp (Anamura et al., 1988). The major cyclooxygenase metabolites of arachidonic acid in the dental pulp in these studies were 6-keto-PGF1c;' followed by TxB2' PGF2c;' and PGE2.

It is of interest that biosynthetic activity of prostaglandin, especially PGE2, increased in experimentally inflamed dental pulp (Lessard et al., 1986; Okiji et al., 1987). More details regarding prostaglandin formation by dental pulp have been described in other chapters.

### 30.3 INHIBITION OF CYCLOOXYGENASE BY PHENOLIC

### COMPOUNDS

The inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis by anti-inflammatory drugs has been demonstrated in a wide variety of cell types and tissues.
Hamberg and Samuels son (1974) isolated two endoperoxides - PGG2 and PGH2 - formed in the transformation of arachidonic acid to prostaglandins, thus proving that prostaglandin synthesis involves two stages: cyclooxygenation followed by the subsequent action of selective synthase. The cyclooxygenation step is specifically inhibited by NSAIDs (Fig. 1). A good correlation has been demonstrated bet~een the capacity of a variety of structures to inhibit prostaglandin synthesis and produce anti-inflammatory effects as evaluated with pharmacokinetic considerations in the rat paw oedema assay. Thus it is believed that the inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis is the key mechanism in preventing inflammation (for review: Vane, 1987).
It has been shown that phenolic compounds such as eugenol, cresol, thymol and guaiacol inhibited O2 consumption by microsomal preparations of sheep seminal vesicle in the presence of arachidonic acid (Lands et al., 1973; Vanderhoek and Lands, 1973; Dewhirst and Goodson, 1974).
Various essential oils which contain phenolic composites such as eugenol are used in the treatment of diarrhoea and of rheumatism and inflammation. Inhibition of prostaglandin biosynthesis by these phenolic composites has also been reported (Bennett et al., 1974; Burstein et al., 1975; Misra et al., 1978; Rasheed et al., 1984; Wagner et al., 1986).
Structure-activity studies on the inhibition by phenolic compounds of prostaglandin cyclooxygenase demonstrated the requirement of a hydroxyl, and electron-donating or hydrophobic ring substituents play an important role in enhancing the action (Baumann et al., 1979;
Dewhirst, 1980; Habicht and Brune, 1983). It has been revealed by these studies that eugenol, thymol and guaiacol are the potent inhibitors of the phenolic medicaments.

However, the order of potency of phenolic compounds in inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis does not correlate with the phenolic coefficiency. For example, guaiacol was a much more potent inhibitor than phenol (Table 30.1), although their phenolic coefficients are similar, suggesting that the mechanism that inhibits prostaglandin synthesis is different from the antibacterial mechanism. Inhibitions by p-chlorophenol and guaiacol were in fact reversed by washing the microsomes or by diluting the concentration of the agents (Anamura et al., 1988). The reversibility of the effects of 2,6-xyenol and tricresol on prostaglandin synthesis in
3T3 fibroblast cultures has also been reported (Lindgren et al., 1977). The specific interaction of phenolic medicaments with cyclooxygenase was suggested by the observation that the concentrations of phenolic compounds required to affect the activities of other enzymes located in both membranes and the cytosol, such as glucose-6-phosphatase, total
ATPase and lactate dehydrogenase, were much higher than those for inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis (Anamura et al., 1988). The results may suggest that the phenolic antiseptics specifically inhibit prostaglandin biosynthesis.
Referring to the mechanism of action of NSAIDs, Lands (1981) in his review divided these drugs into three groups according to their inhibitory action on cyclooxygenase: (a) rapid, reversible, competitive inhibition (drugs with carboxylic acid substituents such as arylpropionates, ibuprofen); (b) irreversible, slowly developing inactivation, which continues until a new enzyme protein is synthesized (acetylsalicylic acid); and (c) rapid, reversible, non-competitive action based on scavenging of free radicals or antioxidation (paracetamol). The first step in the biosynthesis of prostaglandin is the oxygenation of arachidonic acid to the hydroperoxy endoperoxide PGG2• This reaction is catalysed by a haem-containing oxygenase, namely cyclooxygenase. The hydroperoxy group of PGG2 is reduced to a hydroxy group of PGH2 by hydroperoxidase. PGH synthase has both oxygenase and peroxidase activities (Ohki et al., 1979). Lands and his co-workers showed that a small amount of hydroperoxide is required to initiate and maintain the free-radical cyclooxygenase reaction, although large amounts of peroxide inactivate the enzyme (Hemler and Lands, 1980; Marshall et al.,
1987). They suggested that the free-radical chain reaction is initiated by the interaction of peroxide with the haem prosthetic group on the cyclooxygenase and propagated by subsequent radical-rearrangement reactions. ### 30.6 CONCLUSION

Besides as antiseptics, phenolic dental medicaments are used for treatment of pulpal pain and of dentine hyperalgesia. This clinical use is due to the analgesic effect of the medicaments. However, the analgesic and anti-inflammatory mechanism of phenolic compounds have been entirely obscure until quite recently.
This review has revealed the profile of pharmacological action of phenolic dental medicaments as anti-inflammatory agents: (a) these compounds inhibit the activities of cyclooxygenase and lip oxygenase in various preparations; (b) the inhibition is not due to non-specific denaturant action of protein but is specific for the oxygenases; (c) topical application of these compounds produces the anti-inflammatory effect by mouse ear oedema assay. Thus phenolic dental medicaments may produce the analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the metabolism of arachidonic acid. A dual inhibitor of cyclooxygenase and lip oxygenase such as eugenol is assumed to be especially valuable for clinical use as an analgesic.
