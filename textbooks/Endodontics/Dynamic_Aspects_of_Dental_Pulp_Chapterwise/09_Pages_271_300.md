### 16.4 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

### 16.4.1 Uronic acid

Pulps from human premolars contained amounts of glycosaminoglycans, measured as uronic acid, which were somewhat lower than the values given by Linde (1973a) and van Amerongen et al. (1983). These differences were most probably due to the preceding water washings which removed about 20% of the total uronic acid. The water washings in the present experiments were performed to remove blood, which contains high amounts of glycoproteins (Schultze and Heremans, 1966).
It is known that these glycoproteins disturb the tetraborate carbazole reaction (Munemoto et al., 1970). Therefore we could only approximate the amount of uronic acid in the water extract. Further, it was not possible to establish whether these glycosaminoglycans originated purely from blood or whether they originated partially from the pulpal tissue. Therefore the values as given by us for the amounts and the composition (Tables 16.1 and 16.2) are applicable only for those glycosaminoglycans in pulp tissue that do not dissolve during the short water washing procedure.
Sakomoto et al. (1979) found amounts of uronic acid in bovine first molar pulps which were about 100% higher than the values as demonstrated by us for bovine incisor pulps. This difference cannot be ascribed only to a loss of glycosaminoglycans by washing with water. Probably, the kind of teeth investigated playa role in this case. The lower uronic acid content we observed in older teeth (Table 16.1) is in agreement with the findings of Sakomoto et al. (1979) and also with the conclusion of
Linde (1973b) that the decrease of dentinogenic activity is accompanied by a large decrease in the total glycosaminoglycan content in the dental pulp.

### 16.4.2 Evaluation of literature data on pulpal

### glycosaminoglycans

Sakomoto et al. (1979) detected heparan sulphate in bovine, human and rabbit pulps, while Embery (1976) indicated the presence of chondroitin
6-sulphate. At variance with these last data we have to conclude, in agreement with Sakomoto et al. (1979) and NlUrakawa (1974), that in pulp chondroitin 4-sulphate is present and not chondroitin 6-sulphate, because its mobility in electrophoresis and thin-layer chromatography corresponded with standard chondroitin 4-sulphate (Fig. 16.1). On the other hand, we could not find an indication for the presence of heparan sulphate, which is consistent with the results of Embery (1976). Neither

1983). Using this method the constituent monosaccharides, present in small tissue samples with a relatively low glycosaminoglycan content, can be determined simultaneously. However, when using this method it is not possible to distinguish hybrid forms in these samples, e.g.
between chondroitin sulphate and dermatan sulphate.
In accordance with Sakomoto et al. (1979) we found a lower content of chondroitin 4-sulphate in older bovine teeth (Table 2), which supports the argument that this glycosaminoglycan could play an important role in maintaining a high concentration of calcium ions in calcifying tissues (Woodward and Davidson, 1968). When we compare the composition of glycosaminoglycans as studied by several investigators some differences become evident. A conspicuous discrepancy consists in the high percentage of hyaluronic acid (60%) in human premolar pulps quoted by Linde (1973a), who based his data on densitometric recordings of alcian blue stained glycosaminoglycans separated by electrophorsis on cellulose acetate, and the low percentage found by Embery (1976) and by us. This diversity may reflect the non-specificity of the alcian blue staining as already mentioned by Embery (1976).

Another fundamental discussion refers to the presence of keratan sulphate. Linde (1973a) concluded to low amounts in rabbit and rat pulps. Sakomoto et al. (1979) could not detect it with electrophoresis,

Embery (1976) presents evidence that with CPC a fraction can be isolated corresponding to our CPC fraction I, containing hexosamines and no uronic acid. He considered this fraction to be keratan sulphate or acidic glycopeptides. Shuttleworth et al. (1982) suggest as a result of preliminary investigations that the fraction not retained by the CPC-cellulose columns was glycoprotein in character but not keratan sulphate. In other tissues, e.g. skin and uterus, no keratan sulphate could be demonstrated (Kennedy, 1979). In sharp contrast to these observations, we found a relatively high amount of keratan sulphate in pulps (Table
16.2), while preliminary experiments showed that, using detection techniques as described in this study, other tissues like skin and uterus contained this glycosaminoglycan in substantial amounts as well (respectively about 20 and 30%). The IH-NMR assignments, as given in
Fig. 16.4, are based on the data reported for a consistent series of welldefined, N-glycosidic glycopeptides (Vliegenthart et al., 1983). For CPC fraction I of bovine periodontal ligament, IH-NMR results most likely have to be interpreted in terms of the structure as given in Fig. 16.5.
The linkage region in the proposed structure shows close resemblance with those given for proteoglycans isolated from monkey cornea (Nilsson et al., 1983) and rat chondrosarcoma (Nilsson et al., 1982). In principle the NMR method also can be used to identify the type of branching as di-, tri- or tetra-antennary, as well as to determine the number of ~(1~3)-repeating N-acetyllactosamine units (Vliegenthart et al., 1983). These structural features will be further investigated in a collaboration between several laboratories. The presence of glucose most probably points to a contamination introduced by the isolation procedure, because glucose is neither a structural part of glycosaminoglycans nor glycoproteins. We found 16% protein remaining in our keratan sulphate-like material which could not be removed despite protease and alkali treatment. Amino acid analysis showed that high amounts of aspartic acid were present.
This supports the theory of Stein et al. (1982) and also Nakazawa et al. (1983) that the linkage between keratan sulphate chains and the core protein consists of an N-glycosidic bond between N-acetylglucosamine and asparagine (Fig. 16.5), which is stable to alkali (Muir and
Hardingham, 1975). From all these data we conclude that the fraction I which was isolated from bovine periodontal ligament can be qualified as keratan (sulphate)-like material including the linkage region.
From the similarity of data, obtained for pulp and bovine periodontal ligament by colorimetry, electrophoresis, thin-layer and gas-liquid chromatography, we tentatively conclude that also in pulp keratan sulphatelike material is present in relatively large amounts.

### 16.4.4 General conclusions, summary and questions

Gas chromatography proved to be a good technique for the simultaneous determination of glycosaminoglycans and, especially in combination with NMR, useful for further characterization.
Summarizing: the major glycosaminoglycans in dental pulp are chondroitin 4-sulphate, dermatan sulphate and keratan sulphate, next to a minor amount of hyaluronic acid. In view of the relatively high content of keratan sulphate in soft connective tissues our data imply that the amount of uronic acid currently given as a criterion for the total amount of glycosaminoglycans can better be replaced by uronic acid plus galactose.
The possible functions of glycosaminoglycans in the dental pulp have been frequently discussed. Many authors are convinced of the crucial role they play during, for example, dentinogenesis, in which chondroitin sulphate especially seems to be involved by its capability for high calcium binding (Linde, 1973b). Dermatan sulphate is thought to play an important role in collagen fibrillogenesis (Obrink, 1973). Hyaluronic acid, chondroitin sulphate and dermatan sulphate are polyanionic components which can retain water in the tissue. Thus in combination with collagen they form a protection against compression of the tissue (Fessler, 1960). As we found rather large amounts of keratan sulphatelike material to be present not only in pulp, but also in other soft connective tissues, the question arises about its possible role in these tissues. The speculation that the isolated keratan sulphate-like material has pronounced and specific functions seems justified, because of the relative weight and size of the linkage region of this proteoglycan.
In general, the individual functions of glycosaminoglycans are not at all clear, especially in soft connective tissues. Therefore further investigations are required, not only of glycosaminoglycans but also of their proteoglycans.

## Concluding remarks

S. YAMADA

Traditionally biochemistry is a field of life sciences that deals with the physicochemical properties and functions of biological molecules. It also contains vast amount of already established and currently developing techniques of analysis at the molecular level. A central problem of present-day biochemistry is, however, rather to clarify the molecular mechanisms of function, development and differentiation of cells, tissues, organs and multicellular organisms. On the other haJld, scientists working in other fields of life sciences such as developmental biology, immunology, neurobiology and genetics are also trying to explain the vital phenomena in terms of biochemistry or molecular biology. Thus, various fields in the life sciences are now Qverlapping, with biochemistry being the common ground.
Part Four of this book is concerned with several t())pics on the biochemistry of dental pulp; these include enzymes of the glycolytic pathway, collagenase synthesis, collagens and glycoproteirns, and glycosaminoglycans, respectively.
In chapter 13, three important enzymes in the glycoWtic pathway - phosphofructokinase, pyruvate kinase and lactate dehydrogenase - are discussed. Molecular properties of these enzymes from dental pulp are compared with those from other sources, especially with respect to isozyme patterns. Characteristics of carbohydrate met,abolism in dental pulp during its development and differentiation are yet to be elucidated.
Several enzymes of the glycolytic pathway, such as lactate dehydrogenase, could be a good marker to check the effect on cells in dental pulp of various drugs used for clinical treatment in dentistry.
In chapter 14, studies on collagenase synthesized by odontoblast-like cells are introduced. The destruction of collagenous tissue is a main pathological feature of peri<pdontal disease; and tissue but not bacterial collagenase is involved in ~he destruction. This appears also to be true in dental pulp: collagenolysis by collagenase, which might be initiated by dental caries and laterally induced infections, may be implicated in

the pathology of the tissue. What then is the source of dental pulp collagenase? Production of substantial amounts of collagenase by odontoblast-like cells appears somewhat of a paradox because odontoblasts are the cells responsible for primary or secondary dentinogenesis.
On the other hand, it has been shown that the primary cells responsible for collagenase synthesis during bone resorption are not osteoclasts but osteoblasts. Thus it would not be so surprising if odontoblasts did play a role in the degradation of collagen in physiological and pathological states of dental pulp.
Chapters 15 and 16 deal with structural substances in dental pulp matrix; i.e. collagens and glycoproteins, and glycosaminoglycans, respectively. Collagen fibres, for example, are traditionally thought to impart tensile strength to the tissue. It is, however, now recognized that collagens, proteoglycans and other matrix components playa major role in the control of integration of cell behaviour, and can influence migration and morphogenesis. Type I and III collagens represent the bulk of dental pulp collagen. Also in isolated dental pulp cells these two types are secreted as major components. However, fibroblastic cells subcultured from tooth bud explants failed to synthesize type III collagen, suggesting type III expression is a reflection of the odontoblastic potential.
The glycoprotein, fibronectin, is abundant in many developing tissues and its role in cell adhesion is well documented. A role for fibronectin of dental pulp in odontoblast differentiation is implicated by its loss from the dental mesenchyme during odontoblast differentiation. The high level of type III collagen in dental pulp and its interaction with fibronectin also imply its importance, but little information is yet available on its role in dental pulp structure and function.
Proteoglycans, which consist of protein molecules to which glycosaminoglycan chains are linked, are also important constituents of the extracellular matrix. The major glycosaminoglycans in dental pulp are chondroitin 4-sulphate, dermatan sulphate and keratan sulphate, and there is also a minor amount of hyaluronic acid. In dental pulp, glycosaminoglycans are thought to play a role during dentinogenesis:
chondroitin sulphate seems to be involved in terms of its capacity to bind calcium strongly, and dermatan sulphate is thought to be important in collagen fibrinogenesis. A decrease in dentinogenic activity is accompanied by a large decrease in the total glycosaminoglycan content in the dental pulp. Individual functions of glycosaminoglycans remain to be elucidated.

The Monroe-Kelly doctrine states that a rise in intra-organ tissue pressure, as a result of vasodilation, will be transmitted equally throughout the cavity. Like the brain, the pulp is enclosed by rigid structures. Thus, the dilation of arterioles due to the activation of j3-receptors by ISO would raise the intrapulpal pressure, which, when exceeding the venular pressure, results in their passive compression and an attendant reduction in PBF.
Additional research, especially designed to provide continuous PBF data, has provided new evidence that pulpal vessels are equipped with j3-receptors and that in the pulp their activation causes a sharp but transient PBF increase followed by a prolonged reduction. This biphasic flow response to the j3-agonist ISO has since also been observed in response to other vasodilators such as substance P (Mountcastle, 1974).

### 18.7 THE ROLE OF ADRENERGIC RECEPTORS IN CLINICAL

### DENTISTRY

When a dentist injects a local anaesthetic in preparation for a dental procedure, he in effect manipulates the adrenergic system by blocking the adrenergic receptors with an a-agonist. The most widely used local anaesthetic in dentistry is 2% lidocaine with epinephrine 1 : 100 000.
Epinephrine is a potent a-agonist, which potentiates and prolongs the anaesthetic effect by powerful vasoconstriction, thereby preventing the anaesthetic from being 'washed out' quickly. Modern dentistry is unthinkable without the benefits of local anaesthesia; however, it has been shown through many studies that the addition of epinephrine can also be detrimental for the survival of the dental pulp. When we consider that the primary function of the microcirculation is to transport nutrients to and remove metabolic wastes from the tissues, we realize that a significant reduction in volume and speed of blood flow results in an accumulation of metabolic wastes. Hellner recognized this as early as 1927, when he suggested that a marked reduction in pulpal blood flow resulting from dental anaesthesia could result in pulpal tissue injury. Olgart and Gazelius (1977) reported that supraperiosteal injection of lidocaine containing epinephrine in the apical area of the tooth caused almost complete cessation of blood flow in the pulp, and recently we have shown that anaesthetics with epinephrine as a vasoconstrictor caused varying degrees of pulpal blood flow reduction with every injection technique (Kim et al., 1984b). The activation of adrenergic receptors is thus not only of benefit in dentistry, but can also contribute to damage of the tissue. 18.8 CONCLUSIONS

The sympathetic nervous system is activated when an individual is exposed to physical or mental stress, such as haemorrhage, trauma, or perhaps even dental chair anxiety. These stimuli act through afferent nerves, or directly on the central representation of the sympathetic

system to bring about a discharge of the preganglionic and postganglionic neurons. The sympathetic impulses then modify the activities of the innervated effectors either by excitation or inhibition. Sympathetic activities generally serve to mobilize the energy stores of the body, to increase the blood flow through vital regions of the body at the expense of other, primarily peripheral, regions. Reduction of PBP in response to excitation of the sympathetic nervous system via haemorrhage suggests that survival of the pulp is secondary to the preservation of lifesustaining organs. Common sense would support this arrangement, fo,!
to have or not to have teeth is in the end a question of quality of life, not of life itself.
Several studies have shown that the adrenergic system also plays an important role in maintaining homeostasis in the pulp. Specifically, it has been shown that exogenous neurotransmitters, such as epinephrine contained in local anaesthetics, will bind to a-receptor sites in the pulpal vasculature to facilitate vasoconstriction.
Studies of microcirculatory and adrenergic responses and their relationship to clinical dentistry are still in their infancy. We have come to understand some of the basic principles of pulp circulation and hope to gain more insight as methodologies and technologies become available to study this complex encapsulated organ in future years.

### 19.1 DEVELOPMENT OF THE CHOLINERGIC SYSTEM

The role of cholinesterase (ChE) in acetylcholine-mediated neural transmission is well known. After release of the transmitter acetylcholinesterase at the myoneural or intraneural junctions, ChE stops excitation by splitting acetylcholine into its inactive compounds. Although ChE is widespread in neural tissue it appears in many tissues and cells not related to neural function and little is known of its role at these locations.
A discussion of the origin of the cholinergic system seems pertinent to this consideration.

### 19.1.1 Neural crest and cholinesterase

There is considerable information in the literature regarding the development of the cholinergic system in the embryo. The mesencephalic neural crest (NC) in the embryo, for example, is known to synthesize acetylcholine at the time of early cell differentiation and the early stages of their migration. Acetylcholinesterase (AChE), the enzyme responsible for the degradation of ACh, has been found in chick (Drews et al., 1967; Kussather et al., 1968; Usa del et al., 1967; Drews, 1975;
Markov, 1979; Miki, 1981). It has also been found more recently in the mouse and rat neural crest cells, using both cytochemical and biochemical methods of identification. The latter involves study of the characteristics of the sedimentation pattern of the homogenates of NC cells.
Cholinesterase activity, identified as true AChE, was found at all levels in presumptive cells in the margins of the neural folds, prior to and soon after the closure of the tube. This concentration of ChE was maintained in these cells not only during their differentiation but also during their migration in the cephalic and trunk regions (Cochard and Coltey, 1983).
