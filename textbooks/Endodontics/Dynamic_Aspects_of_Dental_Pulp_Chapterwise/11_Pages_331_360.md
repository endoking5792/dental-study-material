## Concluding remarks

J.K. AVERY

The transmission and modulation of pain from the dental pulp is a highly complex and multidimensional phenomenon which involves processing of input derived from primary nociceptors located in the dental pulp. Presently, there are a few studies which have characterized the contents of the vesicles located within nerve terminals in the predentine, odontoblastic zone and the pulp proper. These studies have been able to demonstrate whether the material in the vesicles is a catecholamine or related to acetylcholine. This was a giant step forward since prior classification had been by size of the vesicles, which varies according to the activity in the ending, or the fixative employed. A fixative such as permanganate, for example, forms a precipitate of manganese oxide at endings when noradrenaline is present. This has allowed identification of adrenergic endings with dense vesicles from cholinergic which are more electron-lucent. Recent technical advances now allow the use of monoclonal and polyclonal antibodies, made against various neurotransmitters, to be used with fixed and demineralized tissues. With these probes, the presence and location of the various types of neuronal terminals located in the dental pulp should be elucidated within the next few years. This evidence, in conjunction with the morphological and immunohistochemical evidence already available of the various neurotransmitters located in the dental pulp, i.e. VIP, substance P, calcitonin gene-related peptide, somatostatin, acetylcholine, norepinepherine, neuropeptide Y and serotonin, should enable a dual attack by anatomists/pulp biologists and physiologists to discriminate whether these neurotransmitters are excitatory or inhibitory.
This is only part of the picture, however, since there is a high probability that more than one neurotransmitter is contained within a nerve terminal located in the dental pulp and can be selectively released by an appropriate stimulus. Avery and Chiego noted the presence of terminals with large, dense-core vesicles, as well as some smaller, lessdense ones. These terminals may have several colocalized transmitters

Once the nociceptor located in the dental pulp has generated an action potential and propagated it to the central nervous system, the questions become more complex relative to the types of neurotransmitters associated with the second-order neurons. This is a very exciting and challenging area for future investigations.
A historical perspective of neurohumoral transmission is presented by Jutta Dorscher-Kim and Synguck Kim. They noted that acetylcholine and norepinephrine are the two major chemical mediators for synaptic transmission in the autonomic nervous system. Adrenergic receptors have been further classified into CI. and f3 and subclassified into Cl.l and

Cl.2 based on specific agonist sensitivities.
There have been few systematic and quantitative functional studies on the role of the adrenergic nerves in the dental pulp, owing mostly to the need of invasive methods which have had to be utilized. Recently the 133Xe washout technique and microsphere injection methods have been developed which preserve the integrity of the pulpal tissues. Using these techniques, the presence of Cl.l and possibly Cl.2 adrenergic receptors in the dental pulp have been positively confirmed on arterioles and venules. The presence of f3-adrenergic receptors is uncertain at this time since the literature is contradictory on this point.
The changes in vessel diameter with the change in RBC velocity and volumetric flow rate have been accurately measured by Jutta DorscherKim and Synguck Kim. In response to noradrenaline administration, a resulting vasoconstriction, reduction in RBC velocity and volumetric flow rate took place in both the feeding arterioles and collecting venules, with a small percentage change in the capillaries. This is evidence of the presence of adrenergic receptors in the pulpal vessels.
The sympathetic nervous system' via adrenergic receptors on the effector smooth muscles thus plays an important role in the regulation of pulpal haemodynamics and in homeostasis.
Studies of microcirculatory and adrenergic responses have a profound relationship to clinical dentistry and will in the future be more fully examined in the hope of gaining a better insight into a pulpal haemodynamics in response to various clinical therapies.

### 21.1 INTRODUCTION

The dental pulp is a very unique tissue compared with other tissues. It is enclosed in a rigid dentine chamber and has a special character in that all external mechanical, chemical and thermal stimuli are recognized as pain. Numerous papers have been published in the past on morphological, physiological and pharmacological characteristics of the nerves in the dental pulp. According to the classical classification, the peripheral nerves are divided into sensory nerves and autonomic ones; the latter are also subdivided into sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves.
Morphological analysis has revealed the distribution and morphology of nerves in the dental pulp of various mammals (Bradlow, 1939; Arwill,
1968; Corpron and Avery, 1973; Byers and Matthews, 1981; Gunji, 1982).
However, these morphological studies could not clarify what kind of neurotransmitters or neuroregulators are involved in the intrapulpal nerves. The details of sympathetic and parasympathetic innervation in the dental pulp have been reported by several investigators, and are discussed elsewhere in this book.
Recent advances in immunohistochemistry have revealed the existence of various neuropeptides in both central and peripheral nervous systems. These neuropeptides are located not only in sensory nerves but also in autonomic nerves. The dental pulp is no exception, and many investigators have reported the distribution of various neuropeptides in the dental pulp (Olgart et al., 1977; Uddman ei al., 1980, 1984,
1986, Wakisaka et al. 1984, 1985a,b, 1986, 1987a,b, 1988; Hoshino et al.,
1987; for a review, see Akai and Wakisaka, 1986). In this chapter, we describe the distribution and origins of nerve fibres containing substance P (SP), calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), neurokinin A (NKA), vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP) and neuropeptide Y

### 21.2 EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE

The results discussed here are mainly based on those obtained by an indirect immunofluorescence method of Coons (1958) using the feline dental pulp as an experimental model. In some cases, the dental pulps of rats or human molars and the trigeminal ganglion of the cat' were also examined. The experimental procedures have been reported previously (Wakisaka et al., 1984, 1985a,b, 1986, 1987a,b, 1988). For detection of origins of neuropeptides, sensory neurectomy and sympathectomy were also performed (Wakisaka et al., 1986, 1987a,b, 1988).
The nerve fibres showing immunoreactivity, which should strictly be described as, for instance, 'those showing SP-like immunoreactivity (SPUr, are simply termed 'SP fibres' or 'SP immunoreactive (SP-IR) fibres' here.

### 21.3.4 Functional role of neuropeptides in the dental pulp

Our present studies show the distribution and origins of SP, CGRP,

Thus it must be emphasized that negative immunohistochemical results should be interpreted with caution, since the concentration of neuropeptides in the neuronal elements may be too low to be detected with this technique.
The exact functional roles of neuropeptides in the dental pulp remain unknown. Recent pharmacological and physiological studies showed that SP, CGRP and VIP have vasodilator functions (Burcher et al., 1977;
Brain et al., 1985; Fahrenkrug, 1982). Gazelius et al. (1977) have suggested that SP may be involved in the regulation of blood flow in the dental pulp. They also reported that CGRP has vasodilatory effects in the dental pulp (Gazelius et al., 1987). Moreover, the origins of tachykinin fibres and CGRP fibres are also different from that of VIP fibres. Thus
SPINKA/CGRP and VIP represent different vasodilator mechanisms in the dental pulp. Edwall et al. (1985) reported that NPY may have some sympathetic function co-acting with catecholamines in the dental pulp.

### 21.4 CONCLUSION

Our studies show that there are several neuropeptides in the dental pulp. These neuropeptide-containing nerve fibres are mainly distributed in association with blood vessels; some are apart from the blood vessels.
Substance P fibres, CGRP fibres and NKA fibres are also localized at the subodontoblast layer, running towards the predentine and penetrating into the predentine and dentine. These three neuropeptides may be contained in the same nerve fibres, originating from the trigeminal ganglion. Vasoactive intestinal polypeptide fibres are localized mainly around the blood vessels, and may originate from parasympathetic ganglia. All NPY fibres are localized around blood vessels, and have their origin in the superior cervical ganglion. The exact functional role of these neuropeptides is still obscure, but the present immunohistochemical evidence suggests that neuropeptides around the blood vessel may be involved in the regulation of blood flow in the dental pulp.
Moreover, SP, CGRP and NKA in the pre dentine may also be involved in pain transmission mechanisms in the teeth.

### 22.1 INTRODUCTION

During the two last decades an increasing number of biologically active peptides have been encountered in autonomic and sensory nerves in peripheral tissues. The dental pulp is no exception to this, as reviewed in the previous chapter. The knowledge about the functional roles of neuropeptides is, however, still incomplete. The fact that in many tissues several of the substances are found in nerve terminals in close association to vessels and the finding that they have vasoactive properties has been taken to indicate that they are involved in vasomotor regulation. However, some of the pep tides may have other roles, such as in endocrine and exocrine secretion, growth, bone remodelling, nerve regeneration and influences on immune responses, to mention a few.
In analogy with the variety of actions that classical neurotransmitters have in different organs, it is not surprising that neuropeptides are associated with many different effects in different target organs. This chapter will focus on certain neuropeptides in the dental pulp and their possible roles in haemodynamic regulation.

Vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP) consists of 28 amino acids and is present in cholinergic parasympathetic nerves in many tissues. In the nasal mucosa and salivary glands of the cat, fibres containing VIP are found around blood vessels (Uddman et al., 1978; Lundberg et al.,
1981a). In these tissues VIP and acetylcholine (ACh) have been shown to co-exist in the postganglionic parasympathetic nerves (see Lundberg et al., 1981a). A simultaneous release of VIP and ACh has been shown in the submandibular gland in cats in response to stimulation of parasympathetic nerves. VIP is known as a potent vasodilator and, in addition, it enhances the ACh-induced vasodilation in cat submandibular gland (Lundberg et al., 1981b). Based on these findings it seems obvious that, at least in certain tissues, both VIP and ACh represent important mediators of parasympathetic vasodilation.
The question then arises whether such conclusions arrived at in other tissues can be applied to the dental pulp. Do the perivascular VIPcontaining fibres in the pulp represent a cholinergic or a non-cholinergic parasympathetic supply to the pulp? Despite much effort to elucidate this question in animal experiments, there is only indirect evidence supporting the existence of a cholinergic influence on pulpal vessels as reviewed in a previous chapter.
