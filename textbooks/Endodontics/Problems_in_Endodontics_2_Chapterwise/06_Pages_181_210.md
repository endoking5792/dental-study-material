## Background

Michael Arnold

Besides thorough examination and exact diagnosis, one of the most important requiremenk for successful root canai treatment is to visualize the root canal system as completeiy as possible. Root canal preparaon, which includes much more than merely accessing the pulp cavity. facilitates optimum access to the branches and ramifications of the root canals with all their variations. Any vital parts of the root pulp that have not been located and removed can be responsible for heat sensitivity and pain for many years, even after root canai treatment has been completed. If infected pulp necrosis develops, a failure to locate larger sections ofthe root canai system, orto clean and disinfect them completely, increases the risk of continuing infection within the canal.'

## Nature of the problem

At 40-50 years of age, the resolution of the human eye at a working distance of 25 cm is restricted to about 70 pm.Therefore, the cltnician will not be able to see the workingfield in sufficient detail without magnifying aid9 This appi~es even more so to the diagnosis and treatment of the complex endodontic system, with its many therapeutically relevant fine structures where the view is poor and illumination difficult.

## Variability in number and position of root canals

Difficulties In locating root canals can occur particularly in crowned teeth, which have undergone alterations to shape, size and axis (see Chapter 8). A further complication b that the structures that need to be assessed are very small and cannot be illuminated properly, making controlled preparation under adequate visualization impossible. The variability In the shape and position of the root canals means that there can be no exact match with the diagrams found in textbooks.

### Pros and cons: loupe or microscope in endodontics

Declding whether to use a loupe or a microscope depends on a variety of factors.
w The commonly encountered view, which states that microscopes are for specialists while loupes are for general~sts, takes no account of actual selection criteria. The ex-
- tent to which the treatment range can be extended by using optical magn~fication

isgenerally underestimated, and therefore gets little consideration in treatment plan1 ning and decision making. in general, the options for minimally invasive treatment will be e~tended.l~-'~The rule that applies to endodontics Is that minerallzed and highly constricted toot canals can be more reliably located and exposed with the aid of an operating microscope. Artificial ledges produced during preparatlon can be remedied and fractured instruments retrieved; perforations can be dlagnosed more reliably and accurately sealed."@ in most cases, the limitations of the loupe with regard to magnification and illumination cannot be overcome by a substitute

of equivalent cost.
The microscope's greatertechnical complexity and improved user comfort make it a much more expensive option than the loupe.The space required to site it must also be taken into account; furthermore, it has to be fixed to a steady surface free of vibration. A loupe would appear to be a more straightforward option for the firsttime user of magn~fication aids, as it is more closely linked to the tooth being treated, Its fixed position and larger field of view provide greater flexibility for the user.
allowingquick changes in working position. Disadvantages include the non-variable magnification, the absence of an integrated treatment-recording facility and the cables needed for the lighting system, and thus the high overall weight of the device.
Both types of magnification aid require adequate training if they are to be integrated into the practice quickly, while takingfull account of health and safety issues.
