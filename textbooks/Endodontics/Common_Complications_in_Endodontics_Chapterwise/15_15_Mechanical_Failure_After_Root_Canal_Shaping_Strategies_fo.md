## Mechanical Failure After Root Canal

## Shaping: Strategies for Prevention

Hyeon-Cheol Kim

### 15.1

### Introduction

The purpose of root canal treatment (RCT) is to remove bacteria and infected tissue from the root canal system. RCT resolves the periapical lesions, prevents the recurrence of periapical pathology, restores the function of the tooth, and ensures its long-term prognosis [1].

In contemporary endodontics and dentistry, the success rate of RCT is quite high
[2–4]. With advancements in microscopic endodontics, missing canals have been minimized, and the use of nickel-titanium (NiTi) files for RCT has reduced procedural errors with minimal aberrations and facilitated efficient canal shaping with efficient removal of infectious debris coronally [1, 5].

However, there are clinical failures due to either biological reasons or mechanical origins. Biological failures stem from various clinical scenarios and procedures such as a missed canal, incorrect determination of the working length, incomplete cleaning, inadequate shaping, poor or leaking obturation, among others [2–4]. On the other hand, apical cracks and vertical root fractures (VRF) are types of mechanical failures, differing from biological failures [6, 7]. While biological failures can often be addressed through nonsurgical retreatment or surgical intervention, teeth with mechanical failures are exceedingly difficult to salvage and retain (Fig. 15.1).
Furthermore, the fracture of a NiTi file poses a significant risk factor that can compromise the success of nonsurgical root canal treatment (Fig. 15.2).

This chapter outlines the possible causes of mechanical failure during instrumentation, along with strategies to prevent file fractures and minimize damage to the root dentin.

Even before the widespread use of NiTi instrumentation, a report from 1999 identified VRF as a leading cause (50%) of failures in ETT [13]. Interestingly, the average time from endodontic treatment until failure due to VRF was found to be
20.2 months. This could be attributed to microcracks potentially initiated around the apical foramen during the instrumentation process, which may progress to apical cracks and eventually lead to VRF under functional loading over time.

Adorno et al. [14] reported that the length of instrumentation significantly influenced the development of apical cracks, rather than the technique of instrumentation.
This suggests that over-instrumentation could lead to the creation of apical cracks around the apical foramen, which may progress to VRFs. Therefore, clinicians need to be cautious about maintaining the correct working length and ensure that the motordriven instrumentation remains within the apical root canal while in active motion.

Therefore, maintaining the working length is a crucial aspect during the instrumentation procedure. Given the characteristics and mechanical properties of NiTi files, certain files (especially with stiffer and/or active characteristics) may exert higher screw-in forces during their kinetic motions, increasing the risk of over-instrumentation.

### 15.2.2	 Forces Generated by Motor-Driven

### Nickel-Titanium Instruments

Engine-driven instruments make the torsional force along with reaction force, and these are the basic forces used to cut the root denting for shaping. The forces generated during the operation differ based on the geometries of the file and properties of the NiTi alloy [15].

Files cut and shape the root dentin and canal wall using cutting flutes, whose designs vary with pitch and helical angle. The pitch plays a crucial role in the performance and mechanical actions of the files. Several finite element analysis (FEA) studies have simulated stress generation, stiffness, and reaction forces induced by file movement according to the various geometries [15].

Baek et al. [16] examined the theoretical impact of pitch and cross-sectional geometry on the torsional stiffness of NiTi instruments. They concluded that reducing the pitch (increasing the number of threads) and increasing the cross-sectional areas, rather than the center-core area, could potentially reduce torsional deformation and/or fracture of NiTi rotary files. Similarly, Versluis et al. [17] investigated the influence of pitch and cross-sectional geometry on flexural stiffness. They found that flexural stiffness correlated with the center-core area when tip deflection was kept constant, simulating canal curvature. Their analysis suggested that a slender-­ rectangular cross-section (blue lines) and a short pitch (15 threads) resulted in lower stiffness and reduced stress generation (Fig. 15.4).

### 15.2.3	 Minimization of Screw-in Forces of Motor-Driven

### Nickel-Titanium Instruments

A study investigated how the geometric features of rotary instruments affect the creation of ‘screw-in’ forces and stresses on root dentin using 3D FEA with standardized models under controlled conditions [18]. It was found that the likelihood of ‘screw-in’ depends on both the instrument’s geometry and the curvature of the canal. Consistent with previous research by Versluis et al. [17], instruments with a slender-rectangular (SRec) cross-section and a short pitch (15 threads) showed lower ‘screw-in’ forces and reduced generation of reaction forces (Fig. 15.5).

The off-centered cross-section aims to enhance the efficiency of the file in coronally removing cut debris. This design feature is utilized in instruments such as
ProTaper Next (PTN; Dentsply Sirona) and Revo-S (Micro-Mega). Manufacturers assert that compared to conventional concentric instrument designs, the off-­centered cross-section induces a snake-like, swaggering movement of the instrument, reducing stress generation during rotation and screw-in forces by minimizing the instrument’s contact with the canal wall while still facilitating debris removal [19, 20].
The effects of an off-centered cross-sectional design on the stiffness and stress distribution of NiTi rotary instruments were assessed using FEA [21]. This study indicated that, among various design options, the PTN-based off-center design demonstrated the most optimized flexural responses, deemed clinically desirable.

The kinetics of engine-driven files can significantly impact screw-in forces.
Among various kinematic motions, reciprocating motion offers the advantage of mitigating screw-in movement. Reciprocating file systems have been shown to enhance the longevity of file instruments under cyclic fatigue and torsional conditions [22, 23]. Moreover, the periodic alteration of rotation direction inherent in reciprocating kinetics may decrease the apical driving force compared to the continuous rotary motion of conventional NiTi files.

This awareness necessitates operators to maintain control of the handpiece or employ a brushing action to prevent instruments from being inadvertently pulled into the canal.

### 15.2.4	 Minimization of Stress Generation from the Motor-Driven

### Nickel-Titanium Instruments

After the introduction of NiTi files in the market, manufacturers have been trying various techniques to make files for better clinical performance, and mechanical properties with lower fracture susceptibility. Nevertheless, the NiTi files may induce some vulnerable defects or damage to the root canal dentin during the mechanical shaping procedure.

The incidence of dentinal defects (including fractures and craze lines) following canal preparation is related to the use of various NiTi rotary files [26, 27]. Many in-vitro studies and computational studies suggested that certain endodontic preparation methods could potentially damage the root and lead to dentinal defects
[26–28]. Notably, dentinal defects were absent in unprepared roots and those prepared using hand files [26]. However, preparations with ProTaper, ProFile, and GT resulted in dentinal defects in 16%, 8%, and 4% of teeth, respectively. Thus, it is crucial to be aware that certain endodontic preparation techniques may pose a risk of inducing dentinal defects.

Stress levels during shaping and the susceptibility to apical root cracks after shaping vary with instrument design [27]. The stiffer file designs generate higher stress concentrations in the apical root dentin during shaping, especially in a curved canal, and the stresses may raise the risk of dentinal defects and apical root cracks (Fig. 15.6).

The Self-Adjusting File (SAF: ReDent Nova, Berlin, Germany) features unique designs in both cross-sectional and longitudinal aspects. In an FEA study comparing the SAF with representative conventional NiTi files such as ProFile and ProTaper
Universal, the SAF demonstrated the lowest von Mises stress concentration and tensile principal stress in root dentin [29]. Specifically, stress values calculated for
ProTaper Universal (size F1) and ProFile (size #20/.06) were approximately 8–10 times greater than those observed for the SAF (Fig. 15.7).

Moreover, the technique employed by the operator can also influence stress generation on both the file itself and the root dentin through reaction forces.

### 15.3

### Conclusion

In summary, advancements in endodontic instrumentation have led to the development of various file designs and techniques aimed at minimizing stress concentrations in root dentin and reducing the incidence of adverse events of mechanical failures. Clinicians should carefully evaluate the mechanical properties of different files and adopt appropriate strategies to mitigate the risk of root fractures and other complications during root canal procedures.
