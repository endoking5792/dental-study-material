### 14.1

### Introduction

The importance of patient safety has been recognized since the nineteenth century.
The duties and responsibilities of medical and dental professionals have always been regarded as noble and benevolent. However, with an increase in medical malpractice and negligence in the recent times, the profession is viewed with an impression of doubt and suspicion.

A medical professional who violates established rules and guidelines and causes harm to a patient is guilty of medical malpractice. In contrast, “medical negligence” refers to a careless act by a medical professional that compromises the necessary standards of healthcare [1]. Dental malpractice is extremely similar to medical malpractice in that it occurs when a dental professional fails to adhere to the required standards and laws governing dentistry, causing harm to the patient [2].

Without a doubt, general dentists perform extremely technique-sensitive procedures that require training, experience, and both cognitive and psychomotor abilities
[3]. Owing to the increasing density of healthcare requirements and increase in patient awareness, it is important to familiarize oneself with the legal outcomes/ consequences of any adverse effects, failed restorations, or other complications related to endodontic procedures and techniques.

Among the various disciplines of dentistry, endodontics sees the highest number of malpractice claims [4]. For example, endodontic malpractice claims have been reported at rates of 14% in Sweden [5] and 17% in the USA [6]. Endodontic procedures can be technically challenging for general dental practitioners and uncomfortable for patients. Issues stemming from endodontic treatment are more likely to result in complaints than those from other dental disciplines. This can be attributed to several factors, including the invasive and microscopic nature of the clinical environment, the reliance on the tactile sensation of the clinician, and the lack of direct visualization during the procedure. To build a trusting and safe relationship with patients, it is crucial to follow standard national/international ethical dental principles and practices, using them as a guide in clinical situations/procedural mishaps such as instrument separation.
Providing thorough and detailed explanations before treatment, through appropriate informed consent, can prevent misunderstandings and dissatisfaction for both the patient and the dentist. The main areas for complaints are non-communication with patients about the procedure, obtaining their consent for treatment, and inadequate or incomplete record-keeping.

In the debate between malpractice and procedural accidents, medical malpractice is defined as negligence arising from the doctor-patient relationship, while negligence is an unreasonable act or omission by a provider resulting in patient harm
[8]. Failing to strictly adhere to standards and treatment protocols can compromise treatment quality and outcomes and lead to malpractice claims. However, common complications such as infection or persistent pain without obvious pathology are not considered malpractice [9]. Endodontics is most frequently involved in malpractice cases (28%), highlighting the need for knowledge about legal implications and attention in this field [10].

This chapter explores the reasons behind patient complaints and offers strategies for dentists to enhance communication and manage patient expectations. It also identifies common problem areas where dentists are susceptible to complaints.
With this information, dentists can reflect on their own practices and reduce the likelihood of facing legal action or complaints from patients.

The standards refer to the level of expected outcomes for professional interventions as defined by professional or regulatory organizations. They are grounded in evidence and further shaped by current scientific knowledge and expertise [11].

Although the legal concept of “standards of care” is well-established, it is often not specifically defined in medical contexts. It comprehensively includes consultation, maintaining confidentiality, diagnosis, informing the patient about decided treatment with its adverse effects, providing information on the perceived risks and benefits of the treatment options, treatment procedure and referral to the specialist when required.

According to tort law (civil wrongs), it refers to “the caution and prudence expected of a reasonable person fulfilling a duty of care in similar circumstances when providing patient care” [12]. The consensus among endodontists and legal experts regarding the definition of the level of endodontic care refers to any clinical procedure performed by a competent and properly trained endodontist that is in the patient’s best interests.

The treatment goal of an endodontic procedure is to eliminate pain, manage infection, disinfect, and fill the root canal system, as well as provide a final restoration. These principles help guide a clinician, whether managing an emergency case, providing palliative management, or as part of an overall treatment plan. All health professionals, including endodontists, must adhere to the established standard of care and guidelines while providing treatment, as established by a body of similar practitioners. Deviating from this standard without justifiable reason can lead to claims of malpractice.

Policy or position statements from professional organizations, prepared by experts in the field of various disciplines of dentistry, can act as benchmarks for clinicians to ensure they are practicing within an expected standard of care. The
American Association of Endodontists (AAE) has published a number of clinical guidelines and position statements regarding many aspects of endodontic care, which could be used as a surrogate guide for determining standards of treatment
[13]. The Australian Dental Association (ADA Inc.) has specific policy statements regarding core aspects of endodontic treatment, such as “Dental Dam in Endodontics” and “Treatment Standards in Non-surgical Endodontics.” The European Society of
Endodontology (ESE) quality guidelines provide a statement of good practice for dentists undertaking endodontic treatment and for patients to understand the treatment procedure [14]. The guidelines include recommendations on diagnosis and treatment planning, record-keeping, infection control, maintenance of pulp vitality, root canal treatment, endodontic surgery, assessment of endodontic treatment, and management of traumatic injuries.

The experience level of a health practitioner does not modify the duty of care they provide. Dentists must work within their training and experience. Those who wish to undertake more complex procedures usually performed in specialist settings, such as periapical surgery, might be held to a specialist standard of care should a complication or complaint arise.

### 14.2.1	 Rubber Dam

A dental dam is crucial in endodontics, especially in non-surgical treatments like emergency pulp extirpation. It minimizes bacterial contamination of the root canal system and safeguards the patient’s airway. It is imperative that instruments are not introduced into the root canal system without a dental dam. Failure to use one can result in negative outcomes and potentially serious medical complications, especially if instruments are aspirated or ingested, posing legal risks for the clinician [15, 16].

The dental dam effectively reduces contaminated aerosols in the dental office setting [17]. While there is limited evidence directly linking dental aerosols to disease transmission [18], it is well-established that these aerosols contain microorganisms [19]. COVID-19 can be found in the saliva of infected individuals [20], and virus particles have been detected in the air at dental surgeries following the treatment of COVID-19 patients [21]. Research indicates that aerosolized COVID-19 can remain viable for up to three hours [22]. This raise concerns that vulnerable individuals, such as those with medical conditions or dental staff, could inhale contaminated aerosols and potentially contract a disease [23].

Majorly, dental clinical practice guidelines during the pandemic recommended the use of dental dam for restorative procedures, all with the intention of reducing aerosols in the dental operatory worldwide [24, 25]. The AAE position on Dental
Dam states that “tooth isolation using the dental dam is the standard of care; it is integral and essential for any non-surgical endodontic treatment [26].

Many general dentists may unnecessarily expose themselves to risk by not using a rubber dam during endodontic procedures [27]. Although the use of rotary instrumentation has increased over the years, but hand files are still required in certain protocols for initial instrumentation which may pose a danger of being swallowed or inhaled during canal instrumentation or exposure of radiographs unless rubber dam is used.

Failure to use a rubber dam may suggest that the clinician lacks an understanding of the microbial nature of the disease process and the significance of using a rubber dam and the skills to use it. In the United States, using a rubber dam is considered the standard of care; therefore, expert testimony is not required in cases where patients swallow or aspirate instruments or materials due to the absence of rubber dam isolation. Juries are considered competent to determine negligence.

Patients may sometimes also refuse to use a dental dam due to medical concerns like asthma or mouth breathing, or discomfort factors such as gagging or claustrophobia. For patients with known allergies to latex, latex free rubber dam sheets can be used. However, a patient’s refusal of a dental dam is not an excuse or a reason to proceed with treatment without it. It is the dentist’s responsibility to explain to the patient why the dental dam is necessary. If the patient still refuses the application of the rubber dam, the dentist can decline to proceed with the treatment. Ensuring patient safety is of utmost importance. In cases, where the placement of a dental dam is not possible, the patient should be referred to an endodontist for management, a prosthodontist for restorative treatment to enable dam placement, or alternative options may be considered.

### 14.3

### Risk Management

Legally the tort (civil wrong) of negligence can be established against a dental practitioner only if the any of the following elements can be proved [28]:

1.	 A violation from the clinician’s duty of care.
2.	 An injury to the patient occurred.
3.	 A proximate relationship between the violation and the injury can be established.

Historically, the Bolam test has been used to establish the standard of a “reasonable doctor,” meaning that a clinician is not considered negligent if their actions align with practices accepted by a competent body of medical professionals in that specific field [29].

In practice, the Bolam test implies that a doctor will not be found negligent if their actions are consistent with a responsible medical opinion. This test has been consistently used in courts and is firmly embedded in law. However, the main criticism of the Bolam test is that it does not differentiate between “what is done” and
“what should be done” [30].

As dental practitioners increasingly perform endodontic procedures in their offices, the risk of malpractice litigation rises substantially. Simply possessing strong skills and techniques is not sufficient; dentists must actively and meticulously follow risk-reduction strategies to minimize or avoid lawsuits in the future.
Proper documentation of patient history, treatment plans, and obtaining informed consent are essential practices to prevent legal complications.

Clinical risk management is the process by which a framework of protocols is in place in order to prevent complaints or at least help minimize their impact (Table 14.1).

The UK General Dental Council (GDC) has established guidelines regarding the standards of dental treatment and principles of patient consent [31]. These guidelines are just as relevant to endodontic practice as they are to other branches of dentistry. Understanding patient expectations before beginning treatment can guide the discussion to suit the situation, helping you to give the patient a realistic outlook on potential difficulties and how they might be addressed.

If the dental clinician feels that the patient is unsure in their understanding of the potential difficulties with their treatment, referral to a specialist should be discussed, including the likelihood of acceptance by the specialist, their potential waiting lists, and the cost of a private referral. Carefully document these discussions.

No treatment can guarantee 100% success in health sciences including endodontics. When stating that endodontic treatment can never be 100% successful, it is advised to avoid giving a specific success rate. Instead, it is better to say that there is a very good chance of a desired outcome of the treatment provided the tooth is not periodontally compromised and is restorable. Functionality can be an appropriate term to measure the success of root-treated teeth [35].

Informed consent, referral and recall can be used to better understand the concept of risk management.

### 14.3.2	 Referral

Referral is a fundamental aspect of providing quality healthcare. While a dental qualification equips a dentist with skills across all disciplines of dentistry, it does not imply that every dentist can perform every procedure. In an Australian dental malpractice case, a general dentist attempted orthodontic treatment without adequate knowledge of orthodontic diagnosis, planning, or treatment, leading to a ruling in favor of the patient. The court determined that the dentist should have referred the patient to a specialist [39, 40].

Referrals can be as straightforward as directing a patient to a more experienced colleague within the same practice or as formal as referring to an external specialist.
An Australian study revealed that 62% of general practitioners rarely or never refer patients to endodontists [41].

One challenge for general dental practitioners is determining when and how to refer (Fig. 14.3). American Association of Endodontists (AAE) case difficulty form can be utilized in such cases to assess the level of difficulty (refer to Fig. 14.1).

### 14.3.3	 Maintaining Records and Recall

Patients generally struggle to remember information given to them, regardless of the dental procedure involved. One way to improve recall in patients can be by providing written information along with verbal explanations. Despite these efforts, some patients may still not remember any information after a consultation. However, the use of computerized notes and diagnostic history detailing patient’s symptoms and examinations undertaken eliminates the administrative burden of written information (risk of missing anything) and documenting that it was given. A variety of patient management software specific to endodontics are available on the market (Fig. 14.4).

As mentioned before, information about the procedure and any potential risks must be presented in an understandable way, using layman’s terms and analogies to explain clinical concepts. Providing written information aids in patient retention and supports record-keeping, particularly if a complaint arises. While a signed consent form alone does not prove that consent was obtained, it supports the claim that a discussion with the patient took place. Keeping scanned copies of any written information given to the patient in the records can also help at a later date in case of a claim filed.

### 14.4.1	 Endodontic Errors

Various endodontic errors and malpractices can occur during routine procedures like perforations and broken instruments [7, 42]. According to the Danish Dental
Complaint Boards (DCBs), malpractice is considered when patients are not informed about potential consequences and there is persistent periapical pathology after completion of the endodontic treatment. Additional reasons for endodontic malpractice identified by the DCB include defective or poor-quality apical seals, short root fillings, overfilled, over-instrumented, and unfilled root canals in simple canal anatomies [9, 42]. However, in Finland, operator-related factors are not considered or have any influence on endodontic malpractice claims [43].

Errors can occur at any stage of an endodontic procedure, leading to complications. This raises several questions such as should these be considered mishaps, procedural errors, or negligence, what are the legal perspectives and provisions if negligence is found, and how can dentists prevent such mishaps and protect themselves? Certain clinical situations in endodontics are viewed as neglect or deficiency in service or standard of care, which may lead to legal action.

### 14.4.2	 Misdiagnosis

One of the main challenges in endodontic diagnosis is the failure to identify the correct source of pain, leading to the treatment of the wrong tooth and misdiagnosis. A systematic approach is essential for accurate diagnosis.

Misdiagnosis or failure to diagnose endodontic disease is a frequent cause of complaints against dentists, occurring even after thorough examinations. More often than not, patients present with vague symptom histories and signs. In such cases, invasive dental procedures should be postponed until a definitive diagnosis is established. Persistent pain after treatment, which may not be related to the initial symptoms, is common, as patients can have non-odontogenic or atypical pain following endodontic procedures. Conditions such as dentine hypersensitivity, sinusitis, cardiac ischemia, or orofacial pain may mimic tooth pain and should be considered in differential diagnosis.

Treatment should never be initiated based solely on patient symptoms. Radiographs are essential during the initial examination to maintain a baseline history of present illness but should only be taken after completing all tests and clinical examinations.

### 14.4.3	 Radiographs

A preoperative intra-oral radiograph is mandatory before starting any endodontic procedure to diagnose the status of the tooth and surrounding structures. Bisecting angle technique, which can distort the length and curvature of the roots and inadequately display endodontic pathology, should be avoided. Use of beam aiming devices is recommended to ensure a reproducible technique. In case of a manual film processing, if a radiograph is poorly developed, it would fail to provide the correct information and be difficult to interpret. Thus, it would be unwise to diagnose a case based on such radiographs (Fig. 14.6).

Although not related to the field to endodontics, but there is an interesting case on the importance of preoperative radiography in dentistry. A dental legal claim was filed in Australia against a dentist who surgically extracted a fragment of a retained root based solely on his clinical assessment and opinion. The extraction was done without confirming with a preoperative radiograph. The extracted fragment was in fact an unerupted and impacted third molar [40, 45]. The result was permanent facial nerve damage. The court ruled that the dentist breached their duty of care by not performing preoperative imaging prior to the procedure. This can be used as an

Consent should be obtained for any radiography, detailing the nature of the radiation, dose, and the risks and benefits of the scan [46]. Maintaining records of all intra-oral periapical (IOPA) radiographs can protect against any potential future litigations from patients.

CBCTs can also be used when accurate information cannot be obtained with intra-oral radiography, such as in cases of vertical root fractures. In such cases, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is an appropriate adjunct to assist with diagnosis. However, CBCT should not be viewed as a substitute for intra-oral radiography. CBCT should be used judiciously, particularly in children as its radiation exposure is cumulative [47] despite CBCT’s lower radiation exposure when compared to medical CTs.

Although dental students are trained to interpret intra-oral radiographs, but the training does not typically cover detailed interpretations of CBCTs. The AAE position statement and Australian Dental Association (ADA Inc.) Policy Statements both agree that the entire volume of a CBCT must be interpreted, even if it was taken to assess just one tooth [48, 49]. The clinician who performs the scan is responsible for either interpreting it or arranging for it to be interpreted. A dentist cannot elect to read and interpret only part of a bitewing, lateral cephalogram, or
OPG [50].

### 14.4.4	 Fractured Teeth

Most teeth needing root treatment already have large restorations. To prevent a root-­ filled tooth from fracturing, it should be properly restored after the procedure, either with a post (if necessary) or adequate cuspal coverage. Patients should be informed, before initiating, endodontic treatment, about the risk of fracture of the treated tooth post-procedure and the importance and necessity of proper restoration. This information must be documented in the patient record, along with confirmation that the patient has understood the associated risks if the tooth is not adequately restored.

### 14.4.5	 Broken (Separated) Instruments

Root canal instruments mainly separate either due to flexural fatigue, which is primarily due to instrument overuse, or torsional fatigue, which results from rotational forces locking and fracturing the instrument. Hence, the more an instrument is used, the more likely it is to fracture. Rotary instruments should be used with an appropriate endodontic motor, ideally with torque control. Most rotary nickel titanium instruments operate at 250–350 rpm and require a reducing handpiece with a 16:1 or 20:1 ratio. It is not advisable to use a rotary instrument with a 1:1 handpiece, as any resulting instrument separation would be difficulty to defend in a negligence claim, in such a case.

For general practitioners to use these instruments successfully, they should follow manufacturers’ guidelines and enlarge the canals with hand instruments before introducing rotary nickel titanium. Hand or rotary endodontic files can separate during canal shaping due to unusual canal anatomy, severe curvature, calcifications, improper working length determination, forceful instrumentation, and overzealous use. Although such an instrument fracture is usually considered a mishap rather than negligence, it can still lead to a negligence claim particularly if the patient develops symptoms.

There is enough debate in the literature about single use of endodontic files and reamers. The guidelines on these are subject to interpretation. The single use of endodontic instrument guideline originated in the United Kingdom due to concerns about the potential spread of prion (Creutzfeldt-Jakob) disease [51], stemming from the difficulty in adequately cleaning and sterilizing endodontic files and reamers
[52]. However, this perspective is not universally adapted [53]. Although benefits to a single-use approach exist, such as reduced risk of file separation [54] and no risk of cross-contamination [51, 55], the increased costs associated with these protocols make it difficult to follow these guidelines.

Dentists must regularly review their decontamination and sterilization procedures. It has been suggested that endodontic files and reamers can be reused on the same patient [53] provided the risk is minimal.

### 14.4.6	 Hypochlorite Accidents

Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) is the most commonly used irrigants in endodontics due to its effectiveness at different concentrations. However, its ill effects are also well documented in literature. NaOCl can be harmful to clothing, eyes, skin, and oral mucosa and should be used with caution. Accidental extrusion of NaOCl through the apical foramen can cause severe reactions, which may occur if the solution is injected under pressure with the needle locked into the canal.

To minimize this risk, endodontic irrigating syringes with luer-lock attachments and side vent needles are available and should be used to ensure the irrigant is injected laterally under minimal pressure rather than apically so that the irrigant solution remains in the canal (Fig. 14.8) [58]. The needle should be fine enough to reach the apical third but should remain loose in the canal during irrigation.
Additionally, the needle should always be kept in motion while irrigating and should be at least 2 mm short of working length during use [59].

In the event of an accident, the reaction can be rapid and severe, with intense pain despite local anesthesia, instant swelling, and possible hemorrhagic exudate from the apical tissues. Immediate steps should include reassuring the patient, providing additional local anesthesia for pain relief (including block anesthesia if necessary), thoroughly washing the canal with saline, drying with paper points, and closing the canal without medication. The patient may experience severe swelling and bruising and may need prescriptions for analgesics and anti-inflammatory medications.

### 14.4.7	 Obturation Related Concerns

Malpractice claims related to obturation can include overfilling, overextension, underfilling, and poorly condensed root fillings. Silver points are no longer acceptable, as stated by the American Association of Endodontists in their position statement [62]. Gutta-percha (GP) is an acceptable root canal material. Extrusion with symptoms and no radiograph would be a difficult case to defend in the court of law.
However, if proper length control is demonstrated with a satisfactory working length radiograph with a file and/or GP is obtained, extrusion would be easier to defend against any potential negligent claims.

Modern techniques of patency and preparation to the apical foramen, combined with filling techniques using heated gutta-percha, can sometimes result in excess material in the periapical tissues. While this may raise doubts on the success of the treatment when viewing on a radiograph, it is not necessarily a cause of failure. The
American Dental Association considers the extrusion of material more than 2 mm beyond the apex an error, which could give a patient ground to file a case against the dentist.

Despite best efforts, root canals can still be under-obturated. In such cases, the difficulty arises when a restoration is placed on such a tooth immediately after, especially if swelling and pain occur after placing the crown. Communication is vital and the patient must be informed of all issues related to the final outcome. If the final endodontic result does not justify an expensive crown, it is advisable to not provide one. However, if the tooth is restored with a crown, any potential risks or problems should be explained in advance.

Offering to consult with a specialist for advice or treatment in cases with imperfect clinical results is advisable, although the patient retains the right to refuse both consultation and treatment with a specialist. This fact should be documented, along with all discussions and communications with the patient, to support one’s defense if needed in the future.
