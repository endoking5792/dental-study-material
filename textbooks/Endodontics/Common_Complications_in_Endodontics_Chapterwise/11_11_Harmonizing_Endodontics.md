### 11.1

### Introduction

Dentistry has evolved over the years with great progress and advancement in each specialty. The key to successful dental management relies on integration and collaboration between various dental specialties and ensuring an interdisciplinary approach during planning and execution of planned treatment procedures.
Endodontic and orthodontic procedures complement each other in attaining satisfactory patient outcomes. The harmonization of these procedures not only helps in achieving short-­term clinical goals but also ensures long-term stability of the final results achieved for both orthodontic and endodontic cases.

The specialties of orthodontics and endodontics often have synergistic effects on the overall management of dental patients. Prior to the commencement of orthodontic treatment, restorative and endodontic procedures may be required to prepare the patient for long-term fixed therapy. This is becoming increasingly important as a greater number of adult patients are now seeking orthodontic treatment. Additionally, orthodontic interventions can occasionally lead to root resorption in certain teeth. In cases with severe root resorption, endodontic therapy may be required upon completion of orthodontic treatment to resolve such complications.

Endodontic and restorative procedures may be required to enhance post-­ orthodontic esthetics by recontouring or build-up of teeth for the management of residual spaces that cannot be closed by orthodontics. On the other hand, orthodontic procedures complement endodontic/restorative procedures by redistributing space to facilitate oral rehabilitation in certain cases. Orthodontic treatment may also be employed in endodontic management of teeth with trauma or insufficient crown lengths, more commonly to enhance crown length, aiming for improved endodontic and restorative results.

Orthodontic tooth movement (OTM) occurs in response to an applied force in three phases: the initial, lag, and postlag phases [1]. The initial phase is rapid, spanning 1–2 days wherein the tooth is displaced within the bony socket due to compression of periodontal fibers on the side of force application and stretching on the other.
This is followed by a phase of no movement or a lag phase in which tooth movement cannot occur due to hyalinization of the compressed periodontal ligaments and may last between 20 and 30 days. This hyalinized or necrosed tissue is then removed by the cells to allow tooth movement in the next or postlag phase, which involves rapid tooth movement. Thus, orthodontic tooth movement primarily involves the remodeling of periodontal tissues. However, since the pulpal complex is a continuum of vascular and nervous tissues traversing from the periodontal zone to the endodontic zone, the effects of such applied forces also impact the pulpal tissues. Figure 11.1 shows the various phases of OTM.

A sound understanding of the orthodontic-endodontic interface is therefore essential to ensure harmony in the implementation of the most suitable treatment plan with predictable results esthetically, functionally, and financially. Mustaffa et al. conducted a literature review exploring the interdependence of ortho-endo procedures and noted the lack of uniformity in the reported study protocols in the literature [2]. Knowledge of the fundamentals of endo-ortho interactions would help to minimize complications and ensure successful comprehensive treatment for patients [3].

### 11.2

### The Ortho-Endo Confluence

### 11.2.1	 Biological Harmony

OTM is caused by forces applied to the teeth that create stresses in the periodontium, activating various chemical mediators and neurotransmitters and employing different cell types. These applied forces, however, may also affect the pulpal tissues in two ways: a direct response to heavy pressure applied to the crown [3] and as pressure to the root surface, especially at the apex, from where the neurovascular bundle enters the pulp chamber from the surrounding periodontal zone. The latest hypothesis proposes that mechanical or physical stimulation of sensory nerves by a force may induce neurogenic inflammation of the pulp, resulting in alterations in pulpal blood flow [4]. However, such transient changes are reported to be of minimal grade [5]. The initial discomfort experienced during orthodontic activations may be related, though minimally, to this modest and transient inflammatory response within the pulp in addition to the periodontal effects of the applied pressure.

Only in cases of previous trauma or excessive pressure, permanent damage to the tooth pulp may occur, resulting in loss of tooth vitality. As the pulpal tissues are in a continuum with the periodontal tissues, an unphysiological force at the apex of the tooth root may result in the severing of blood vessels entering the pulp with a consequent loss of vitality. A heavy continuous force causes an undermining resorption due to abrupt movements resulting from large increments of change that can even sever the blood vessels as they enter the pulp through apical opening [4].

### 11.2.2	 Physiological Harmony

The effect of OTM on pulp has been reported to range from mild transient inflammation to loss of tooth vitality. The application of forces triggers a biophysiochemical cascade in the pulp.

Bucheli et al. studied the reactions of human pulp to occlusal trauma and orthodontic forces. He reported that the greatest increase in expression of substance P (SP), calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) was seen in the occlusal trauma plus orthodontic forces group, followed in order by the moderate orthodontic forces, occlusal trauma and control groups, respectively [6]. SP, CGRP, and VEGF play vital roles in activating angiogenesis and neurogenic inflammation, which are the defense mechanisms of pulp tissue against harmful stimuli. This is followed by formation of mineralized tissue to repair the damaged tissues.

In addition to blood flow, alterations in the respiration response of the pulp to various amounts and durations of orthodontic forces have also been studied. In their research, Unsterseher et  al. evaluated the respiration response of the pulp upon application of 72-h orthodontic force, followed by a rest period of 1 week. They concluded that pulpal respiration decreases by 27–33% in response to the applied force and returns to normal during the rest of the period. The time required for

### 11.3

### Orthodontic Treatment and Endodontic Implications

The orthodontic forces are generally light and dissipate over a period of time between regular activations. These forces directly affect the periodontium via compression of the neurovascular bundle, as explained previously. They also induce hypoxia due to metabolic cellular stress in addition to mechanical stress from cytoskeletal deformation. To relieve stress, cytokines and other inflammatory mediators are released that promote bone resorption to provide space for stressed cells [8].
Hence, under physiological conditions, inflammatory mediators cause resorption of the surrounding bone. The following paragraph examines the factors that can trigger an inflammatory response during orthodontic treatment that can lead to resorption of the root in addition to the resorption of bone.

### 11.4

### Root Resorption Due to Orthodontic Treatment

The resorption of roots during orthodontic treatment has been reported to range from 4 to 99% [9–12]. The upper incisors are the most susceptible teeth, especially in class II div 1 malocclusions, where a greater amount of retraction is carried out
[13]. However, the incidence of severe root resorption is relatively low, although root shortening by apical blunting has been reported to occur in 91% of teeth [14].
Figure 11.2 shows radiographs demonstrating post-orthodontic root resorption in two different patients.

### 11.5

### Factors Affecting External Apical Root Resorption

### (EARR) Due to Orthodontic Treatment

The resorption of roots may occur due to a variety of reasons, such as trauma, infectious inflammation of periapical tissues and periodontal diseases, but orthodontic treatment is the most common cause of root blunting [15]. Although root resorption is a physiological process in deciduous teeth, in permanent teeth, the orthodontic treatment may act as a triggering factor for a sterile inflammatory process that can lead to resorption of roots besides other contributing factors [16]. The problem of root resorption as a sequalae of orthodontic tooth movement was first reported by
Ottolengui in 1914 [17]. Brezniak and Wasserstein reviewed in detail the factors involved in root resorption during orthodontic treatment and suggested the use of the term orthodontically induced inflammatory root resorption (OIIRR) to describe the resorption process resulting from orthodontic force [18, 19]. ### 11.5.2	 Exogenic Factors

1.	 Magnitude and type of orthodontic force: In the 1930s, Schwarz opined that

the “optimal” force for tooth movement should be within the levels of capillary pressure to ensure maximal cellular response and maintain vitality of the tissues.
Heavy orthodontic forces cause undermining resorption that permits a large increase in positional change, which can result in the severing of blood vessels at the entrance to the pulp canal with a consequent loss of tooth vitality [4]. The cellular cementum covering the apical region depends on active cells and supporting vasculature. The loss of blood supply may result in degeneration of the outer protective mineralized layer of roots, i.e., precementum and cementoblasts, allowing the attachment of clastic cells to the denuded root surface [34]. Thus, the root apex shows greater vulnerability to resorption and is usually the first region to demonstrate the resorptive process radiographically. The other factors that contribute to the increased risk of resorption at the apical and middle thirds include a gradual decrease in the hardness and elastic modulus of the cementum at the apex from the cervical region. ### 11.6

### Orthodontics as an Adjunct to Endodontics

Orthodontic treatment plays a crucial role in various clinical scenarios by enabling endodontic and prosthetic restoration. When space is compromised due to tooth position or crowding, it can obstruct access for restorative, endodontic, or prosthetic procedures. Orthodontic interventions aimed at specific tooth movements like, uprighting of tipped teeth, decrowding, extrusion, intrusion can help achieve favorable outcomes in such cases. By strategically redistributing space through orthodontic therapy, it becomes possible to support restorative and rehabilitative treatments, particularly in situations involving microdontia and hypodontia.
Figure 11.5 shows a case where orthodontic treatment facilitated the rehabilitation of missing teeth.

In cases of dental trauma, the incidence of crown-root fracture is reported to be
0.3–5% [45]. In situations where a fracture line extends sub-gingivally resulting in the loss of the upper part of the tooth, a multi-disciplinary and comprehensive approach involving endodontist, orthodontist, periodontist, and prosthodontist becomes essential to manage such complex cases effectively [46]. The primary challenge in this situation is inadequate supragingival tooth structure to support the crown prosthesis, which necessitates the exposure of the root structure to support the prosthesis. Figure 11.6 shows a case where a tooth with a subgingival fracture line was extruded by applying orthodontic force through an elastomeric chain anchored to a wire bonded to adjacent teeth to increase the supragingival crown height. However, the prerequisite for increasing the length of the supragingival tooth structure is that the remaining root within the bone must be of sufficient length to be adequately supported by bone. This approach ensures a favorable crown-root ratio (postrestoration) of 1:1 even after extrusion [39]. ### 11.7

### Endodontics as an Adjunct to Orthodontics

1.	 Before the start of orthodontic treatment: The placement of orthodontic

attachments alters the oral environment through both qualitative and quantitative changes in the oral microflora by increasing the number of plaque-accumulating surfaces [50]. This promotes periodontal disease as well as demineralization of enamel, resulting in dental caries [51]. Hence, it is essential to remove any existing plaque and restore any carious tooth prior to the start of orthodontic treatment. Any pulpal involvement or trauma to a tooth should also be treated endodontically before orthodontic therapy commences to avoid the aggravation of inflammatory processes that can result in root resorption of the affected tooth
[4, 52]. The resolution of any persisting carious lesion or pulpal damage before the placement of any orthodontic attachment or appliance minimizes the risk of any endodontic complication during or at the end of orthodontic treatment.
2.	 Endodontic procedures during orthodontic treatment: The increased risk of

plaque accumulation along with difficulty in adequate plaque removal predisposes patients to compromised gingival/therapeutic health as well as the development of carious lesions. However, regular follow-up appointments during orthodontic therapy provide an opportunity for monitoring oral hygiene

### 11.8

### Future Trends

### 11.8.1	 Aligners and Endodontics

Aligners are the latest addition to the orthodontic armamentarium and offer the advantage of being removable to allow for the possibility of thorough oral hygiene maintenance. These are sets of preprogrammed transparent trays that are replaced every 7–14 days to bring about intended tooth movement in a stepwise way. Since these appliances are removed while eating, the risks of food retention in the oral cavity during orthodontic treatment are minimized. The ease of biofilm removal, as the appliances are not permanently fixed, facilitates better gingival and periodontal health and reduces the risk of white spot lesions [38, 59–61]. The forces applied through aligners are intermittent, as the appliance can be removed in contrast to the continuous forces applied through fixed appliances. Recent evidence regarding the risk potential of aligners compared to that of conventional fixed mechanotherapy for causing root resorption is reportedly inadequate to draw definite conclusions but seems to favor aligners in terms of a lower incidence and severity of root resorption
[62, 63].

### 11.8.2	 Role of Biomarkers in the Prediction of Root Resorption

There is growing evidence regarding the utility of various biomarkers in body fluids, such as gingival crevicular fluid (GCF), saliva, and blood, indicating external root resorption. Dentin phosphoproteins (DPPs) are released in the GCF during resorption and are considered useful markers. With the help of ELISA, highest levels of these DPPs have been found in patients with resorbed deciduous molars, followed by those with mild OIIRR. Out of the extracellular matrix proteins, dentin matrix acid phosphoprotein 1 (DMP-1), dentin phosphoprotein, and dentin sialoprotein (DSP) the concentrations of DMP-1 and DPP were greater in the GCF of orthodontic patients with evidence of OIIRR than in those of controls. The levels of DSP have been found to correlate with the severity of resorption [64].

Dentine phosphoprotein (DPP) has been found to be a relatively useful marker for root resorption in orthodontic treatment [65–67]. Other biological molecules, such as dentin sialoprotein (DSP), and inflammatory cytokines (pro- and antiresorption), such as osteopontin (OPN), osteoprotegerin (OPG), RANKL and alkaline phosphatase (ALP), play roles as markers to explain the biological mechanisms involved in orthodontic movement but have not been proven to be definite biomarkers for predicting root resorption and its extent [66]. Such predictors for root resorption may greatly help in screening patients for the possibility of OIRR before the start of orthodontic treatment to design safer treatment strategies.
