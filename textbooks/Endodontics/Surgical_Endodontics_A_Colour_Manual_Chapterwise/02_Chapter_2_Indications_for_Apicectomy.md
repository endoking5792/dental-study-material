## Indications for Apicectomy

The principal indications for apicectomy are listed below, subdivided according to the operative technique that is usually best used.

Fully Filled by a Coronal Approach following
Removal of the Apical Segment (Orthograde or
Orthograde + Retrograde)

(1) Failed root fillings that can be removed from the canal:

Apicectomy is sometimes indicated in the case of overextended root fillings because the apical constriction has been destroyed by instrumentation and a good seal cannot thereafter be achieved by conventional methods. A failure resulting from under filling should be corrected in the first instance by refilling to the correct length and not by surgical means.

When a Full-length Conventional Root Filling can be

Retrograde)

(1) Curved roots with wide canals may be cleansed with fine flexible files but there is no way of ensuring that the canals have been instrumented to a circular cross-section. Consequently, it is unlikely that accurate obturation will be achieved by the use of a matched or master point.

(2) Where a tooth has died before root formation has been completed. In such cases it is rarely possible to properly seal the funnel-shaped canal with a conventional root filling. Unfortunately, placement of a retrograde amalgam seal also poses problems because the thin collar of dentine at the end of the root is extremely friable and, even if a seal can be achieved, large areas of amalgam are placed in contact with the tissues (Figure 2.5a and b). Apicectomy is a poor option in such cases and attempts should be made either to stimulate the completion of root formation or to close the apex by filling the canal with calcium hydroxide, this being followed by conventional root canal treatment. A surgical solution should be attempted as a last resort only if conservative methods fail.

Medical Reasons

It is my opinion that patients at risk of subacute bacterial endocarditis (SBE), and who require a tooth to be root filled, should have this done as an apicectomy procedure. Such an approach offers three advantages.

(1) No reliance need be placed on empirical and often inexact radiographic assessments of root canal length.

(2) The integrity of the apical seal can be checked visually.

(3) The work is done in one visit at the time when the antibiotic cover is at its most effective. The problem of determining a proper antibiotic regimen that will account for multiple appointments is avoided.

A hard-line approach such as this does mean that teeth which cannot satisfactorily be apicected cannot be saved. However, this misfortune is associated with a lower morbidity than is SBE.
It must be said that the opinion expressed above is at variance with some reports which state there is a minimal risk from conventional endodontic therapy provided that the apical constriction is not broached.

Expediency

Occasionally one or a number of teeth require to be root filled in one visit or within a short period of time. In such cases expediency may outweigh the marginally worse prognosis that is entailed by apicectomy (Figure
2.6). The same consideration applies to patients, such as the mentally handicapped, whose treatment often has to be done under general anaesthesia, and where it is impractical to tak~ radiographs and difficult to arrange repeated appointments.

Crowned Teeth

The presence of artificial crowns or bridgework is seldom a good reason for not attempting, as first choice, conventional root canal treatment. If an apicectomy is necessary, an orthograde + retrograde approach should be made. The damage done to a restoration as the result of preparing an access cavity must be set against the better prognosis when the root canal has been thoroughly cleansed and completely obturated (see
