## 9

Endodontic procedures inside the pulp chamber and root canal system require high levels of precision. Performing endodontic therapy is like working inside a blind space wherein procedures are traditionally done by tactile sensation and with the help of radiographs. The greatest challenge a clinician faces while trying to achieve clinical perfection is in trying to visualize the operating site with clarity. This is not possible beyond a point due to the inherent limitation of the human visual spectrum.

The advent of magnification aids has greatly revolutionized dentistry and especially endodontics. Either using a dental loupe (Fig. 9.1) or preferably employing a dental operating microscope (DOM) (Fig. 9.2) can achieve enhanced visualization in clinical dentistry. The introduction of DOMs has revolutionized a new era in endodontics.

### DENTAL LOUPES

Loupes can be classified by the optical method in which they produce magnification and also based on how the lenses are mounted on to the frame.
I.
Based on the kind of lenses being employed, there are three types of binocular magnifying loupes:
1.	 Diopter, flat-plane, single-lens loupes

•	 Their only advantage is that they are the most

economical of all the options available.
•	 However, as the operator needs to be close to the

viewed operating site, these loupes are associated with compromised posture and musculoskeletal stresses and fatigue.
2.	 Surgical telescopes with a Galilean system (two-lens

system)

•	 They are the most recommended in dentistry and

are available in a magnification range of 2× to
4.5× (Fig. 9.1a).
•	 They are relatively compact and lighter in weight.
3.	 Surgical telescopes with a Keplerian system

•	 They employ refractive prisms and are heavier

(Fig. 9.1b).
•	 They can provide magnification up to 6× and are

sometimes used with a head band.
II.	 Loupes can also be classified based on how the lens are

mounted to the frame:
1.	 Flip-up loupes
2.	 Through the lens (TTL) loupes
Comparative assessment of features between these two types of loupes is given in Box 9.1.

### DENTAL OPERATING MICROSCOPE

The introduction and acceptance of DOM has been one of the most important dental innovations in the past three decades.
Currently, every endodontist is expected to learn and use a
DOM in order to improve clinical proficiency and deliver better treatment outcomes for the patient.

### PARTS OF THE BODY OF A MICROSCOPE

The body of the microscope has four main parts (Fig. 9.2).

### 1. Eyepiece Lens

•	 The operator views the field through the eyepiece lens. Both

the eyepiece and the objective lens have convex lenses.
•	 Eyepieces are generally available in powers of 10×, 12.5×,

16×, and 20×. The most commonly used are 10× and 12.5×.
•	 Eyepieces have adjustable diopter setting to adjust for

accommodation (ability to focus the lens of the eyes).

### 2. Binoculars

•	 The function of binoculars is to hold the eyepieces.

•	 They need to be adjusted according to the interpupillary

distance of the operator. As in a typical pair of field binoculars, adjusting the distance between the binocular tubes sets the interpupillary distance.
•	 This is done with the help of a small knob or manually

until the operator is able to visualize the area of interest with a single focus from both the eyepieces.
•	 Inclinable tube microscopes are recommended in dentistry

as they allow the clinician to adjust the angle of the binocular according to the clinical situation. They can often provide the operator with additional postural comfort during long procedures.

Clinical Note

The diopter settings and interpupillary distance settings would vary from one operator to the other and need to be changed whenever a new operator wants to work on a microscope.

### 3. Magnification Changer

•	 Magnification changers are located within the head of the

microscope. The magnification is increased or decreased by rotating a dial.
•	 A magnification of 2–2.5× would enable the dental oper-

ator to see multiple quadrant areas in focus. This is the magnification normally used in general dental practice and is recommended for new users. At magnifications of 3.5×, the field becomes restricted to a single quadrant, while at a magnification beyond 3.5×, the view becomes increasingly restricted until only a single tooth is seen.

### 4. Objective Lens

•	 The objective lens is the first optical component that the

image information crosses on its path from the object to the eye. The lenses vary in their focal distance (focal length).
•	 The focal length influences the working distance (i.e., the

distance between the object in the treatment field and the surface of the lens), the magnification, and the resolution.
•	 The most common working distances are 200, 250, and

300 mm.
•	 Unlike an objective lens with fixed focal length, you can

also have a variable focal length objective lens. Therefore, it can be set to a range of working distances to meet different application and ergonomic requirements. The focal range of such a variable focal length objective lens can be varied from 200 to 300 mm (Fig. 9.3).

Clinical Note

•	 Depth of field refers to the ability of the magnifying system to

focus on objects that are both near and far without having to change the lens position.

––
Magnification increases → Smaller field of view → Depth of field decreases.
––
Magnification decreases → Larger field of view → Depth of field increases.
•	 The use of magnification causes a restriction in the depth of

field and, as the magnification increases, the depth of field decreases to the point that only a small object is sharply in focus and everything around is out of focus.

### I. MAGNIFICATION

Magnification is determined by four key variables in a dental microscope.

### 1. Power of the Eyepiece

Eyepieces are generally available in powers of 10×, 12.5×,
16×, and 20×. The most commonly used are 10× and 12.5×.

### 2. Focal Length of the Binoculars

Binoculars often come in different focal lengths. In choosing binocular focal lengths, it is important to remember that the longer the focal length, the greater the magnification and the narrower the field of view. Shorter-length binoculars allow the operator to have a wider field of view and to be little closer to the patient.

### 3. Magnification Changer Factor

Magnification changers are available as three-, five-, or six-step manual changers. Manual step changers consist of lenses that are mounted on a turret.

A conventional three-step changer has one set of lenses and blank space on the turret and thus has three fixed powers of magnification. A five-step or six-step manual changer has a second set of lenses mounted on the turret and produces five or six fixed powers of magnification, respectively.

### 4. Focal Length of the Objective Lens

As the focal length increases, it implies that the distance of the lens from the object of interest is also increasing. Hence,

•	 Increasing the focal length of the objective lens

### II. ILLUMINATION

One of the advantages of using a microscope is its ability to provide coaxial illumination. Coaxial illumination means that the light-source path and the line-of-sight path are similar.
This enables a microscope to illuminate the object of interest clearly without any shadows.

Powerful LEDs are used as the light source in conventional microscopes.

### III. DOCUMENTATION

Documentation of clinical work and the ability of a co-observer to visualize what a clinician is seeing in real time are possible by attaching a beam splitter between the magnification changer and the binocular of a microscope.

The beam splitter divides the incoming light into two parts in prefixed ratios (e.g., 50:50). One part of the light goes to the eyepiece in the binocular while the other part is sent to the documentation port/co-observer (Fig. 9.4).

### IV. MOUNTING A MICROSCOPE

To reduce the inadvertent movement of the body of the microscope, it should be firmly fixed to a rigid surface.
Hence, to have adequate movement around the working area of the patient without restricting access to the oral cavity for the operator, the body of the microscope is supported by a counterbalanced arm that can be attached to the floor, wall, or ceiling (Fig. 9.5).

### CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

The use of a DOM enables a clinician to perform a wide variety of complex clinical procedures in a very comfortable posture (Fig. 9.6). It also enables a clinician to visualize the area of interest in various levels of magnification (Fig. 9.7).

The use of a front surface mirror (Fig. 9.8a) helps in clear visualization when using a DOM while special hand instruments such as the micro-openers are useful while negotiating calcified canals (Fig. 9.8b). The various clinical applications of a DOM are given in Box 9.2.

### CLINICAL USES OF A MICROSCOPE AT DIFFERENT

### LEVELS OF MAGNIFICATION

### Low Magnification (2.5× to 5×)

It is ideal for tooth examination and initial access preparation. The wider field of view is useful to gain orientation in relation to adjacent teeth and anatomic structures.

### Medium Magnification: (5× to 12×)

It is most commonly used for most nonsurgical and surgical endodontic procedures. This range of magnification provides adequate field of view and depth of field.

### High Magnification (12× to 20×)

It is used sparingly during minute procedures such as instrument retrieval. It is also used during documentation of clinical work. However, the limited field of view and loss of focus during minor movements make clinical work at these levels of magnification a challenge.
