## 21

Endodontic treatment is a highly predictable therapy with excellent prognosis. More than 90% of cases would heal if the therapy were done meticulously following proper protocols. Symptoms of postendodontic discomfort are usually attributed to occlusal disturbances and transient apical periodontal inflammation.
Discomfort following endodontic treatment is usually present for a very short duration (few days to a week) and should resolve without the need for any clinical intervention.

Patients in whom endodontic therapy was previously done and are showing signs and symptoms of postendodontic disease would warrant retreatment procedures. The common causes for endodontic failures are given in Box 21.1.

The clinician when encountering a failing root canal– treated tooth has the following options of clinical management:
i.
Keeping the patient under observation

•	 Usually preferred when the patient is not convinced

about the need to treat the tooth
•	 Usually chosen when the tooth is clinically asymptomatic ii.	 Nonsurgical endodontic retreatment

•	 It is the ideal treatment plan for an endodontically

treated tooth that is clinically symptomatic.
•	 It is preferred over surgical endodontic retreatment if

the clinician is able to shape and clean the root canal system predictably. A clinical case with a 14-year

follow-up exemplifies the predictability of nonsurgical endodontic retreatment (Fig. 21.1).
iii.	 Surgical endodontic retreatment

•	 Usually recommended in retreatment cases with

complex anatomy that makes it difficult to reach the apical third of the root canal
•	 Also recommended in cases with errors done by a

previous operator that make nonsurgical endodontic retreatment technically difficult
•	 Use of surgical dental operating microscope highly

recommended in such cases iv.	 Extraction of the tooth

•	 It is usually recommended when both nonsurgical

and surgical endodontic treatment are not possible or when the tooth is not restorable prosthodontically. It is also recommended when the patient is no longer interested in wanting to save the tooth.
•	 Although prosthodontic rehabilitation with implants

or fixed partial dentures is a predictable alternative after tooth extraction, every clinician should always first attempt to preserve the natural dentition and use extraction as the last treatment plan option.
Definition: According to the American Association of
Endodontists (AAE), endodontic retreatment is a procedure to remove root canal filling materials from the tooth, revise the shape, and obturate the canals; it is usually accomplished because the original treatment appears inadequate or has failed or because the root canal has been contaminated by prolonged exposure to the oral environment.

### OBJECTIVES

•	 Achieve adequate access opening of the pulp chamber in

order to access the obturated root canals as well as trace any missed canals.
•	 Ensure complete removal of all restorative and obturating

materials used by the previous operator.
•	 Disinfect the root canal system thoroughly to eliminate

microorganisms that have either survived the previous treatment or have re entered the root canal system.
•	 Shape, clean, obturate and create optimal coronal seal of

the retreated root canal system to achieve long- term clinical success.

Common Causes of Failure of Root Canal Treatment

•	 Operator-related causes of failure

––
Improper isolation (not employing rubber dam isolation)
––
Missed root canal causing pulpal inflammation or reinfection
––
Inadequate cleaning of the root canal causing persistence of infection (most common cause of failure)
––
Improper shaping causing canal blockade/ledge formation
––
Instrument separation that would prevent complete disinfection of the canal
––
Improper management of perforations (pulp chamber perforation/root canal perforation)
––
Inadequate sterilization protocols of instruments
––
Lack of proper postendodontic coronal seal causing coronal leakage and reinfection
•	 Non-operator-related causes of failure

––
Persistent periradicular infection
––
Extraradicular cysts
––
Vertical root fractures

### FULL-COVERAGE RESTORATION

When a patient presents himself/herself with an existing crown in the tooth that is planned for endodontic retreatment, the best solution is to remove the crown and proceed with endodontic treatment. However, if the marginal integrity of the full-coverage restoration is satisfactory, it is advisable to retain the same, and create the access through the coronal restoration.

Hence, one of the challenges in retreating endodontically failed teeth is that most of these teeth would have a full-coverage restoration that would make access into the pulp chamber difficult. The major disadvantages of access through full-coverage crowns/fixed prosthesis are the following:
•	 Restricted visibility
•	 Difficulty in maintaining orientation and parallelism of

the bur according to the long axis of the tooth, especially in maxillary lateral incisors and mandibular first premolars
•	 Increased chances of iatrogenic mishaps
•	 Difficulty in removal of canal obstructions and posts
•	 Increased probability of missing untraced canals, recur-

rent caries, and crown fracture.

Clinical Note

•	 To cut through metal crowns, carbide burs are recommended.

Examples include Talon burs (Tri Hawk) or Transmetal bur (Dentsply Sirona) (Figs 21.2 and 21.3).
•	 To remove porcelain from porcelain-fused-to-metal (PFM)

crowns, the use of round diamonds with copious coolant water spray is recommended.
•	 Special instruments that aid in the lifting of the crown without

the fear of damaging the underlying tooth structures are also recommended. These include special chisels and forceps or instruments such as WAM Key (Fig. 21.4).

### OBSTRUCTION FROM PREVIOUS

### OBTURATING MATERIALS

When retreatment of a previously endodontically treated tooth becomes necessary, the filling material must be removed or bypassed; otherwise, salvaging the tooth from extraction may require an endodontic surgical procedure. Because most teeth to be retreated are sealed with gutta-percha, and in some cases silver points, the following sections discuss the removal of these materials from root canals.

### REMOVAL OF GUTTA-PERCHA

Gutta-percha and sealer can be removed by the application of the following:
i.
Rotary instrumentation ii.	 Solvents iii.	 Heat carrier instruments iv.	 Ultrasonics v.	 Combination of the above
•	 One of the most common methods for removing gutta-

percha obturation is by using rotary instrumentation.
This can be achieved with normal nickel titanium shaping instruments; however, there are specific rotary retreatment files that are also available, for example, HyFlex
Remover (Coltene) and ProTaper Universal retreatment files (Dentsply Sirona) (Fig. 21.5).
•	 One of the other methods for removing gutta-percha from

the orifice and middle root canal is to use a solvent, which softens the gutta-percha and permits its removal through sequential instrumentation.
•	 Chloroform was the most popular solvent because of its

efficiency. However, the use of chloroform is no longer recommended because of its reported toxicity and potential carcinogenicity.
•	 Some of the popular commercially available solvents used

are Endosolv E and Endosolv R (Septodont) (Fig. 21.6).
The Endosolv E is used for dissolving obturation materials based on zinc oxide and eugenol while Endosolv R is recommended for resin-based pastes and sealers.
•	 Other gutta-percha solvents that are also available include

xylene, eucalyptol, and halothane.

•	 Solvents should be employed cautiously and should be

administered a few drops at a time. The operator must use caution because these solutions frequently escape from the syringe needle without any apparent pressure on the plunger and there is no control over the depth of action of these solvents; hence, they are not recommended for apical third gutta-percha removal (Fig. 21.7).
•	 Gutta-percha can also be removed from the pulp chamber

by using a heat carrier and searing the exposed guttapercha. However, this technique is slower and would not allow complete removal of the obturating material from

### REMOVAL OF SILVER POINTS

A silver point is not removed as easily as a gutta-percha cone unless the butt end of the silver cone extends into the pulp chamber. In such cases, the butt end of the silver point is vibrated with an ultrasonic scaler to break the cementing media. The cone is then grasped with a pair of narrow-beaked (Stieglitz) pliers and is removed (Figs 21.8 and 21.9). If the silver cone extends only slightly into the pulp chamber, it can often be removed by vibration with an ultrasonic scaler until it becomes loose.

### RECOMMENDED CLINICAL PROTOCOL FOR

### RETREATMENT CASES

•	 Most clinicians use a combination of the above-men-

tioned available retreatment techniques according to the complexity of each retreatment case.
•	 A solvent (few drops only) is initially used to soften the

coronal third of the obturating material. Care should be taken to avoid the use of solvents in the middle and apical thirds of a root canal.
•	 After initial softening of the coronal third gutta-percha, a

stiffer smaller sized hand instrument is used to negotiate and create a glide path inside the obturated canal.

•	 Rotary or retreatment files are then used to remove the

remaining gutta-percha and negotiate the canal till the established working length.
•	 Residual sealer and obturating material need to be

removed from the canal using a combination of hand files along with copious irrigation. Stiffer hand instruments such as the Micro-Debriders (Dentsply Sirona) are also useful (Fig. 21.10).
•	 Frequent irrigant activation is recommended to ensure

that the remnants of obturation do not block the canal inadvertently.
•	 Sequential radiographs would also help a clinician in negoti-

ating the canal as they proceed apically till the working length.
•	 Secondary endodontic infections are more resistant to

therapy; hence, it is recommended to perform multiple sitting endodontic therapy for infected retreatment cases.

### REMOVAL OF SEPARATED INSTRUMENTS

Instruments separate or break only when they are used incorrectly or overused. Most common causes of instrument separation are the following:
•	 Inadequate access opening
•	 Absence of glide path
•	 Overuse of the instrument
•	 Excessive apical pressure during shaping
•	 Larger sized/larger taper instrument in extremely thin and

curved canals

The best method to overcome this problem is to avoid instrument separation. Box 21.2 provides guidelines for the same. If the instrument fractures in narrow canals or in the critical apical third of the canal, it becomes very difficult to either remove or bypass the instrument.

Separated instruments in the coronal third of the canal (Fig. 21.11b) can be removed and the canal can be negotiated till the apex. However, if the separation occurs beyond the curvature, in the middle and apical third of the canal (Fig. 21.11a), or in narrow canals, then attempts to bypass the instrument should be undertaken carefully with smaller sized instruments before attempting to retrieve the instrument.

Clinical Note

•	 Cyclic fatigue or multiple use of the same instrument is the

primary cause of instrument separation.
•	 Other causes include those related to torsional fatigue and

carelessness in the instrumentation technique.
•	 Hedstrom files are no longer indicated for shaping the root

canal system due to increased incidence of shaping errors.

The prognosis and management of a separated instrument inside a canal are dependent on the following:
•	 Level of instrument separation in the canal (coronal,

middle, or apical third)
•	 Size of the instrument
•	 Degree of infection beyond the level of the separation
•	 Operator skill and necessary armamentarium including

dental operating microscope
•	 Patient motivation and decision of the future course of

treatment
These and other factors which should influence the clinician’s decision on the management of this error are summarized in Figure 21.12.

### INSTRUMENT REMOVAL TREATMENT PLAN

Based on these factors, one of the following three treatment plans can be attempted:
1.	 Instrument retrieval using ultrasonics or wire loop and

tube technique
2.	 Bypassing the instrument and making it part of the obtu-

ration (Figs 21.13 and 21.14)
3.	 Surgical intervention in the form of hemisection of the

root or root resection in roots with apical third instrument separation

Incidence of Instrument Separation

•	 Create a glide path and patency with small hand files.
•	 Ensure straight-line access and good finger rests.
•	 Use a crown-down shaping technique depending on the instru-

•	 Use stiffer, larger, and stronger files (such as orifice shapers) to

•	 Avoid jerking or hurrying of instrumentation.
•	 The files used in narrow and curved canals have to be replaced

immediately.
•	 Examine the used files regularly, preferably under magnification.
•	 The canal should be flooded with sodium hypochlorite as the

instrument is passed through the canal.
•	 Avoid keeping the file in one spot, particularly in curved canals.
