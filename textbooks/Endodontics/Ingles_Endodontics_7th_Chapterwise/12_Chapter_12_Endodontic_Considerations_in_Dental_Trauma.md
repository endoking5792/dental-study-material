## ASPECTS UNIQUE TO DENTAL

## TRAUMA

Most dental trauma occurs in the 7- to 10-year-old age group owing to falls and accidents near home or school.1,2 It occurs primarily in the anterior region of the mouth, a-ecting the maxilla more than the mandible.3

,us, in many cases after a dental traumatic injury, endodontic therapy is provided to caries-free, single rooted, young permanent teeth. ,erefore, maintenance of the tooth is particularly important in these young individuals. Luckily, the potential for a successful endodontic outcome is very good if timely and correct treatment is provided soon after the injury.

## CROWN FRACTURES

,e primary aim from an endodontic point of view is to maintain pulp vitality after fractures involving the crowns of teeth.

Crown infractions—“incomplete fracture or crack of enamel without loss of tooth structure”4—and uncomplicated crown fractures—“fractures of the enamel only or enamel and dentin without pulp exposure”4—are injuries that have little danger of resulting in pulp necrosis. In fact, the biggest danger to the health of the pulp is iatrogenic, caused during esthetic restoration of these teeth. ,erefore, meticulous follow-up, over at least a 5-year period, is an important endodontic preventive measure. Endodontic intervention should be considered at any follow-up time, when reactions to sensibility tests change, apical periodontitis develops, the root appears to have stopped development, or the pulp is obliterating.

### Complicated Crown Fractures

By de.nition, complicated crown fractures involve enamel, dentin, and pulp4 and occur in 0.9% to 13% of all dental injuries.5–7 A crown fracture involving the pulp, if left untreated, will always result in pulp necrosis.8 However, the manner and time sequence in which the pulp becomes necrotic allow a great deal of potential for successful intervention in maintaining pulp vitality.

,e .rst reaction after an injury is hemorrhage and local in/ammation. Subsequent in/ammatory changes are usually proliferative but can be destructive. A proliferative reaction is favored in traumatic injuries since the fractured surface is usually /at, allowing salivary rinsing with little chance of impaction of contaminated debris. ,erefore, unless impaction of contaminated debris is obvious, it is expected that within the .rst 24 hours after the injury, a proliferative response with in/ammation will extend no more than 2 mm into the pulp (Figure 12-1).9–11 In time, however, microbial infection will result in local pulp necrosis and a slow apical spread of pulpal in/ammation.

### Treatment

Treatment options are (1) vital pulp therapy comprising pulp capping, partial pulpotomy, and full pulpotomy or (2) pulpectomy. ,e choice of treatment depends on the stage of development of the tooth, time between the accident and treatment, concomitant periodontal injury, and the restorative treatment plan.

### Stage of Development of the Tooth

Loss of vitality in an immature tooth can have catastrophic consequences. Root canal treatment on a tooth with a “blunderbuss” canal is time consuming and di0cult. Of more importance, however, is the fact that necrosis of an immature tooth leaves it with thin dentinal walls that are susceptible to fracture both during and after an apexi.cation procedure.12 ,erefore, every e-ort must be made to keep the pulp vital at least until the apex and cervical root have completed development. Pulpectomy in a mature tooth has a high success rate,13 but vital pulp therapy (rather than removal) on a mature tooth performed under optimal conditions can also be carried out successfully.14,15 ,erefore, vital pulp therapy can be an option under certain circumstances, even though a pulpectomy is the treatment a-ording the most predictable success. In an immature tooth, vital pulp therapy should be attempted, if at all feasible, because of the tremendous advantages of maintaining the vital pulp.

### Time Between the Accident and Treatment

For 48 hours after a traumatic injury, the initial reaction of the pulp is proliferative, with a depth of no more than 2 mm of pulpal in/ammation (Figure 12-1). After 48 hours, chances of direct microbial contamination of the pulp increase as the zone of in/ammation progresses apically.16 ,us, with time, the chance of success in maintaining a healthy pulp decreases.

### Concomitant Attachment Damage

A concomitant periodontal injury will compromise the nutritional supply of the pulp. ,is fact is particularly important in mature teeth, in which the chance of pulp survival is not as good as for immature teeth.17,18

### Restorative Treatment Plan

In a mature tooth, pulpectomy is a viable treatment option, unlike an immature tooth, in which the bene.ts of maintaining vitality of the pulp are great. However, if performed under optimal conditions, vital pulp therapy after traumatic exposures can be successful. ,us, if the restorative treatment plan is simple, and a composite resin restoration will su0ce as the permanent restoration, this treatment option should be given serious consideration. If a more complex restoration is to be placed (e.g., a crown), pulpectomy would be the more predictable treatment method.

## TREATMENT METHODS

### Pulp Capping

Pulp capping implies placing a dressing directly onto the pulp exposure. As indicated by the success rate of this procedure (80%) compared with that of partial pulpotomy (95%),35 it appears that a super.cial pulp cap should not be considered after traumatic pulp exposures. ,e lower success rate is not di0cult to understand since super.cial in/ammation develops soon after the traumatic exposure. ,us, if the treatment is at the super.cial level, a number of in/amed (rather than healthy) pulps will be treated, lowering the potential for success. In addition, a microbial-tight coronal seal is much more di0cult to achieve in super.cial pulp capping since there is no cavity depth to create this microbial-tight seal, as there is with a partial pulpotomy.

It should be noted that the success rates mentioned previously are of studies performed in the 1970s20 with calcium hydroxide as the pulp capping agent and a restoration above with a high probability of leakage. Recent case series studies34 on capping the in/amed pulp suggest that the bioceramic materials o-er a better success rate than previously thought.
Studies particularly on vital pulp therapy on traumatized teeth with in/amed pulps are yet to be performed but there is a good chance these too will show an improved success rate if bioceramic base is used before the .nal restoration.

### Partial Pulpotomy

Partial pulpotomy implies the removal of coronal pulp tissue to the level of healthy pulp tissue. In traumatic injuries, this level can be accurately determined owing to the knowledge of the reaction of the pulp after a traumatic injury. ,is procedure is commonly called the “Cvek pulpotomy.” For more details, see Chapter 27.

### Full Pulpotomy

,is procedure involves removal of the entire coronal pulp to the level of root ori.ces. ,is level of pulp amputation is chosen arbitrarily because of its anatomic convenience. ,erefore, since the in/amed pulp sometimes extends past the canal ori-
.ces into the root pulp, many “mistakes” are made, resulting in treatment of an in/amed rather than a nonin/amed pulp.
,is procedure is indicated when it is predicted that the pulp is in/amed to deeper levels of the coronal pulp or with symptoms of irreversible pulpitis. Traumatic exposures after 72 hours and a carious exposure with symptoms of irreversible pulpitis are two examples in which this type of treatment may be indicated. While the more predictable procedure would be pulpectomy in mature teeth, the bene.ts outweigh the risks for this treatment in immature teeth with incompletely formed apices and thin dentinal walls. For more details, see Chapter 27.

### Pulpectomy

Pulpectomy implies removal of the entire pulp to the level of the apical foramen. ,e indications are for complicated crown fracture of mature teeth (if conditions are not ideal for vital pulp therapy). ,is procedure is not di-erent from root canal treatment of a vital, nontraumatized tooth.

## HORIZONTAL ROOT FRACTURES

!is injury implies fracture of the cementum, dentin, and pulp. !ey are relatively infrequent injuries, occurring in less than 3% of all dental injuries.42 Incompletely formed roots with vital pulps rarely fracture horizontally.42
When a root fractures horizontally, the coronal segment is displaced to a varying degree, but, generally, the apical segment is not displaced. Because the apical pulpal circulation is not disrupted, pulp necrosis in the apical segment is extremely rare. Pulp necrosis develops in the coronal segment owing to its displacement but occurs in only about 25% of cases.43 !e clinical presentation is similar to that of luxation injuries. !e extent of displacement of the coronal segment is usually indicative of the location of the fracture and can vary from none, simulating a concussion injury (apical fracture), to severe, simulating extrusive luxation (cervical fracture). Radiographic examination for root fractures is extremely important. Since root fractures are usually oblique (facial to palatal), one periapical radiograph can easily miss its presence. It is imperative to take at least three angled radiographs (45, 90, and 110°) so that at least at one angulation the X-ray beam will pass directly through the fracture line to make it visible on the radiograph (Figure 12-6).

Today cone-beam tomography (CBCT) has made the diagnosis of horizontal root fractures less of a challenge and allowed a much better assessment in terms of treatment strategy and prognosis prediction. CBCT is still relatively rare in the armamentarium of the people who see these injuries, so the periapical radiographic strategies for diagnosis remain extremely important.

### Treatment

Emergency treatment involves repositioning of the segments in as close a proximity as possible and splinting to adjacent teeth for 2 to 4 weeks with a functional splint.44,45
!is splinting protocol has recently changed from the 2 to
4 months of rigid splinting that had been recommended for many years.44,45 If a long time has elapsed between the injury and treatment, it will likely not be possible to reposition the segments close to their original position, compromising the long-term prognosis of the tooth.

### Endodontic Essentials

If the coronal segment is reduced quickly and a functional (nonrigid) splint is placed for 2 to 4 weeks, pulp necrosis is remarkably low. ## LUXATION INJURIES

### De"nitions

1. Concussion implies no displacement, normal mobility,

sensitivity to percussion.
2. Subluxation implies sensitivity to percussion, increased

mobility, no displacement.
3. Lateral luxation implies displacement labially, lingually,

4. Extrusive luxation implies displacement in a coronal

De#nitions 1 through 5 describe injuries of increasing magnitude in terms of the intensity of the injury and subsequent sequelae.

Luxation injuries result in damage to the attachment apparatus (periodontal ligament and cemental layer), the severity of which is dependent on the type of injury sustained (concussion least, intrusion most). !e apical neurovascular supply to the pulp is also a"ected to varying degrees, resulting in altered or total loss of pulp vitality in the tooth. If the luxation injury is mild, the in%ammatory response to the external root surface will be small and the pulp will maintain vitality. In%ammation owing to the trauma will thus be selflimiting, and cemental (favorable) healing will result.

If, on the other hand, the luxation injury is severe, the damage to the external root surface may be so di"use that healing with new cementum alone may not be possible. If bone attaches directly to the root (ankylosis), the root will eventually be replaced by bone through physiologic resorption and apposition of the bone (osseous replacement). In addition and of special interest to the endodontist, with a severe luxation injury, the neurovasculature tissues of the pulp may be severed, resulting in a necrotic pulp. If this necrotic pulp becomes infected, the microbial toxins will pass through the tubules and the damaged cemental covering of the root, resulting in potentially catastrophic periradicular in%ammation with bone and root resorption. For more details, see Chapter 15.

!ere are two types of trauma-related resorption in which the pulp plays an essential role:

1. In external in%ammatory root resorption, the necrotic,

infected pulp provides the stimulus for periodontal

in%ammation. If the cementum has been damaged and the intermediate cementum penetrated, as when the tooth undergoes a severe traumatic injury, the in%ammatory stimulators in the pulp space are able to di"use through the dentinal tubules and stimulate an in%ammatory response over large areas of the periodontal ligament. Owing to the lack of cemental protection, periodontal in%ammation will include root resorption and the expected bone resorption.
2. In internal in%ammatory root resorption, the in%amed

pulp is the tissue involved in resorbing the root structure. !e pathogenesis of internal root resorption is not completely understood. It is believed that coronal necrotic infected pulp provides a stimulus for pulpal in%ammation in the more apical parts of the pulp. If in
“rare” cases, the in%amed pulp is adjacent to a root surface that has lost its predentin protection, internal root resorption will result. !us, both the necrotic infected pulp and the in%amed pulp contribute to this type of root resorption.

### Pulp Canal Obliteration

Pulp canal obliteration is common after luxation injuries, if not severe enough to sever the neurovascular supply apically. !e frequency of pulp canal obliteration appears inversely proportional to that of pulp necrosis. !e exact mechanism of pulp canal obliteration is not known. ## ADJUNCTIVE THERAPY

Systemic antibiotics given at the time of replantation and prior to endodontic treatment are e"ective in preventing microbial invasion of the necrotic pulp and therefore subsequent in%ammatory resorption.82 Tetracycline has the additional bene#t of decreasing root resorption by a"ecting the motility of the osteoclasts and reducing the e"ectiveness of collagenase.83 !e administration of system antibiotics is recommended beginning at the emergency visit and continuing until the splint is removed.84 For patients not susceptible to tetracycline dentin staining, doxycycline twice daily for

Penicillin V 1000 mg and 500 mg every 6 hours for 7 days has also been shown to be bene#- cial. !e microbial content of the sulcus also should be controlled during the healing phase. In addition to stressing to the patient the need for adequate oral hygiene, chlorhexidine rinses for 7 to 10 days are useful.

Bryson et al.69 found bene#t in removal of the pulp contents at the emergency visit and placing Ledermix (not available in the United States) into the root canal. !is product contains a tetracycline corticosteroid combination that has been shown to move through the dentinal tubules. It appears that any corticosteroid alone (without tetracycline) is equally e"ective in shutting down the in%ammatory response and decreasing the incidence of osseous replacement.85

!e need for analgesics should be assessed on an individual case basis. Mild analgesics are usually adequate, or a nonsteroidal anti-in%ammatory drug may be recommended. !e patient should be sent to a physician for consultation regarding a tetanus booster within 48 hours of the initial visit.

### Second Visit

!is visit should take place 7 to 10 days after the emergency visit. At the emergency visit, emphasis is placed on the preservation and healing of the attachment apparatus.

## INTERNAL ROOT RESORPTION

Internal root resorption is rare in permanent teeth. Internal resorption is characterized by an oval-shaped enlargement of the root canal space.4 External resorption, that is much more common, is often misdiagnosed as internal resorption.
Internal root resorption is characterized by resorption of the internal aspect of the root by multinucleated giant cells adjacent to granulation tissue in the pulp. For more details, see
Chapter 15.

Internal root resorption is usually asymptomatic and is
#rst recognized clinically through routine radiographs. Pain may be a presenting symptom if perforation of the crown occurs and the metaplastic tissue is exposed to the oral %uids.

For internal resorption to be active, at least part of the pulp must be vital, so a positive response to pulp sensitivity testing is possible. !e coronal portion of the pulp is often necrotic, whereas the apical pulp that includes the internal resorptive defect remains vital. !erefore, a negative sensitivity test result does not rule out active internal resorption. It is also possible that the pulp becomes nonvital after a period of active resorption, giving a negative sensitivity test, radiographic signs of internal resorption, and radiographic signs of apical in%ammation. Traditionally, the pink tooth has been thought pathognomonic of internal root resorption.
!e pink color is due to the granulation tissue in the coronal dentin undermining the crown enamel (Figure 12-16).
!e pink tooth can also be a feature of cervical subepithelial external in%ammatory root resorption that must be ruled out before a diagnosis of internal root resorption is made.

### Radiographic Appearance

!e usual radiographic presentation of internal root resorption is a fairly uniform radiolucent enlargement of the pulp canal (Figure 12-17A). Because the resorption is initiated in the root canal, the resorptive defect includes some part of the root canal space. !erefore, the original outline of the root canal is distorted. Only on rare occasions, when the internal resorptive defect penetrates the root and impacts the periodontal ligament, does the adjacent bone show radiographic changes.
