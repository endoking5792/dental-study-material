## Endodontic infection, treatment outcomes, and

## survival of endodontic practice

Progress in endodontics has been driven by the triumvirate dynamics of patients’ needs, dentists’ needs, and growth in the knowledge of the subject. These primary drivers are moderated by what patients are prepared to undergo; the nature and expense of provision in the form of materials, equipment, and devices by the dental commercial sector; and the progress in clinical application of new research findings. The relative dominance of influence of each of these factors has varied through time.

At the start of the last century, medical and dental practices were largely guided by prevailing philosophies and the strength of expert opinion rather than by the scientific evidence that today’s standards require. As an example, at the beginning of the 1900s, fears of fatal oral sepsis85 from deficient root canal treatments led to the widespread extraction of pulpless teeth, even though this practice was founded on flawed research. In the USA, endodontics almost disappeared from many dental schools as the “focal infection” theory reigned for about 40 years,74 until around 1940.

Extinction of the endodontic discipline was prevented by diligent dental practitioners in Europe and the USA. These individuals had the foresight to meticulously record their treatments and measure their outcomes in order to demonstrate the effectiveness of controlling root canal infection through the institution of bacterial culture tests prior to obturation as a matter of routine practice. A major mark of success of such endeavors was the granting of specialist status to endodontics by the USA in 1952.

As a prelude to this, a population boom and the need for the provision of healthcare to deprived millions had been the hallmark of the post-World War II era in many countries, where the focus on such needs improved the general and dental health of their respective

populations. This focus on health provision has continued in a variety of ways throughout the world ever since, with countries adopting different models of funding and priorities, depending on their specific needs. Despite the emergence of new diseases, some directly impacting the practice of dentistry (hepatitis B and C, HIV/AIDS,
COVID-19), and the rise in prevalence and incidence of lifestyle diseases (obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases), more people are surviving longer into old age. Parallel improvements in dental medicine have also resulted in an increase in demand for more complex dental therapies to retain functional and esthetic dentitions much later into people’s lives. Such changes have driven a host of reevaluations of the altered economic implications of healthcare on society. The cost-effectiveness (efficacy and efficiency) of treatment procedures for the management of diseases has therefore begun to be more sharply scrutinized. This, in turn, has fueled the era of evidence-based medicine and dentistry, with its emphasis on guidelines, costs, benefits, outcomes of treatment, and prevention. A stricter standardization of practice, accompanied by a punitive medicolegal system, has in some parts of the world influenced practice scope. To enhance outcome predictability, a need arose to match case complexity with the training competence of dental practitioners at the specialty or subspecialty levels.

In contrast to other disciplines, the focus on the evidence of outcomes for root canal treatments happened much earlier due to the focal infection challenge. However, individual small studies failed to provide confident estimates of intervention outcomes in the wider population, leading to the need for pooled outcome data to achieve greater power. Attempts to pool outcome data resulted in the realization that the collective data pool from different centers was heterogeneous and precluded definitive conclusions in formal metaanalyses. This has led to attempts to constrain published data to standardized formats to facilitate more meaningful pooling. Such approaches, however, may have their drawbacks, as they may constrain originality and innovation in research question formulation and analysis.

In the last 20 years, the association between focal infection83 and systemic disease has been propelled to the fore again. While in some quarters this has raised old fears,125 in general it has prompted the urgency for establishing a better evidence base for judging the impact of endodontic infection on systemic health.

Curiously, a new threat to the survival of the endodontic discipline was posed by the cost/economic pressure exerted on treatment planning decisions that centered on the question of whether to “save the tooth” or “extract and replace” with an apparently more predictable implant-supported crown.254 Rather belatedly, the science of evidence-based practice helped to avert further irrational options or appraisals that leant toward extraction of savable teeth;51,75,89,94,168 the pendulum has swung firmly back in favor of maintaining predictably restorable teeth, even if they require endodontic management.

The endodontist must always be ready to play a critical and influential role in restorative treatment planning decisions and avoid being relegated to the subordinate role of a “root canal therapist” who provides the technical execution of a procedure prescribed by another dentist. The endodontist must, therefore, display and deploy a sound knowledge of the basic sciences that underpin clinical practice and be a well-rounded clinician with good general treatment-planning skills.

### Types of endodontic treatment

Endodontists manage inflammation of the pulp, and, sequentially, the periradicular tissues. The direct spread of inflammation across the cementum from the pulp to the periodontium is precluded by its selective permeability, leading to localized periapical inflammation at the involved root canal system termini. In contrast to periodontal

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1110 -->

disease affecting the same tissue from the gingival direction, the localized nature of periapical disease makes it potentially more predictably manageable.231 The goal of endodontic treatment is expressed as either the prevention or the cure of apical periodontitis.
Treatment to control reversible pulpal inflammation before it affects the periapical tissues is called vital pulp therapy, as it aims to maintain the health of the residual, unaffected pulp tissue. More advanced inflammation affecting the pulp, judged not to be amenable to returning to functional health (irreversible pulpitis) and on the verge of involving the apical tissues, may require complete extirpation and root canal treatment. The precise clinical and histologic definition of reversible and irreversible pulpitis remain elusive because of the lack of absolute correlation between histologic and clinical measures, leading to the paradox of potentially treating irreversible pulpitis through vital pulp therapy.186,246 Apical periodontitis caused by established intraradicular infection in the apical root canal anatomy is managed by root canal treatment. Persistent apical periodontitis following technically inadequate root canal treatment may require root canal retreatment. Persistent apical periodontitis following “technically satisfactory” or “technically unsatisfactory but best possible” root canal treatment may require root canal retreatment or periradicular surgery to manage the source of the persistent disease. The widely used phrase “endodontic treatment” is therefore a collective and nonspecific term for a range of different procedures directed at managing pulp inflammation (of variable severity and extent) or periapical inflammation.

The ideal outcome of endodontic treatment is the progressive resolution of inflammation, accompanied by complete regeneration of the involved tissues. However, persistent severe infection or inflammation may jeopardize attempts at healing and instead result in repair. None of the involved “endodontic tissues” are normally within direct sight of the clinician due to their investment in hard (tooth structure or alveolar bone) and soft (gingival/mucosal) tissues;
consequently, surrogate clinical measures are employed to evaluate their state of disease or resolution.

### Types of endodontic treatment outcome

An outcome measure for a treatment intervention may constitute any consistently anticipated and measurable consequence of the treatment. By this definition, the following may all be regarded as outcome measures: prepared shape of the root canal system, consequences of root canal debridement, bacterial load reduction, technical quality of root filling, periapical healing, tooth survival, and patient perception of how the tooth feels. The most important outcome is the resolution of the problem for which the treatment was performed; namely, periapical inflammation. The ultimate clinical measures, therefore, assess the prevention or resolution of disease (or inflammation). As periapical lesions arise from the interaction between microbiota (and their products) and the host defenses, their prevention or resolution depends on preventing or terminating such interaction.

Chronic inflammation, being typically clinically asymptomatic (unless superimposed by acute exacerbation), is consequently challenging to detect without direct imaging and requires indirect or associated (proxy) measures. The process calls upon the clinician to triangulate various sources of information to form a judgment about the presence or absence of persistent disease.

The prevention of apical periodontitis encompasses the prevention of dental disease, since it is a sequel of other primary diseases (caries, tooth surface loss, periodontal disease, traumatic injuries).
More specifically, the prevention of apical periodontitis is the prevention of the spread of pulpitis to apical periodontitis through preemptive vital pulp therapy or partial/total pulpectomy. In most instances, the prior absence of apical periodontitis implies an absence of apical bacterial colonization, usually coupled with vital, healthy pulp tissue in the apical part of the root. Asepsis is the prime requirement in the management of such a problem, rather than focusing on the precise technical details of instrumentation or irrigation protocol, as long as it is effective in the aseptic removal of the pulp tissue. This is validated by the high chance of retaining

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1112 -->

periapical health (judged by conventional radiography), which is 90% to 99%, regardless of the clinical protocol used.143

When teeth are associated with established periapical lesions, the challenge is the removal of the established bacterial biofilm in the apical root canal anatomy in order to facilitate the switching off of the periapical inflammatory or immune response. The posed challenge is putatively even greater when the periapical lesion is larger, as it is associated with a more diverse infection244 that is more difficult to eradicate.31 A variety of protocols have been adopted across the world to achieve this general aim, with a matching variation in treatment outcomes; a picture that contrasts starkly with that for teeth without preexisting apical periodontitis.

The precise dynamics of the healing process after root canal treatment are not clearly elucidated; nevertheless, ideal healing should include periapical tissue regeneration with cementum formation over the apical termini, isolating the root canal system from the periapex. More commonly, the inflammatory process in the apical tissues typically persists, with gradual resolution over 12 months or even longer,138 due to persistent residual infection in the apical anatomy and possibly extruded root filling material.120,138,264 Although the majority of periapical lesions heal within 1 year,10,142 healing can take anything up to 4 years or longer.242 On the other hand, the longitudinal follow-up78 of cases presenting with a widened apical periodontal ligament for 10 years revealed unfavorable future healing in a small proportion of the cases (4 cases out of 14; 28%). The elongated duration of periapical healing is typical and contrasts with healing following periodontal treatment, which takes 3 to 4 months.177

Partly due to this healing latency, it may have been considered necessary in the past to adopt the culture test during root canal treatment as an interim measure of chemomechanical efficacy. This approach fell out of favor in contemporary practice for a variety of reasons, not least because root canal treatment had already become widely accepted.129 The technical quality of root filling and the absence of clinical signs and symptoms may be taken as the

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1113 -->

immediate outcome measures at the completion of a root canal treatment.20,21

The definitive outcome measure, however, is periapical healing, since the treatment is aimed at the resolution of periapical disease (Fig 20-1).144 Clinical judgment of prospective anticipated periapical healing is based on the absence of signs of infection and inflammation, including pain; tenderness to pressure or percussion of the tooth; tenderness to palpation of the related soft tissue; absence of swelling; and sinus and radiographic demonstration of the reduction in size of the periapical lesion (if sufficient time has lapsed), with a completely normal periodontal ligament space as the end point.

The periapical status of root-treated teeth is typically assessed using two-dimensional (2D) conventional radiographic imaging, despite the reduced sensitivity of this method due to the anatomical superimposition of structures, especially in the posterior part of the mouth.86 This is because the outcomes are still discernible with a lower dosage of ionizing radiation. Three-dimensional (3D) imaging, such as cone beam computed tomography (CBCT), requiring only 8% of the effective dose of conventional computed tomography,112 can overcome such superimposition and improve sensitivity,46,161,173,229,239

but at the cost of higher ionizing radiation than conventional radiography. Although the value of clinical outcome data from conventional radiographic techniques has been questioned,282 the routine use of CBCT is not recommended56,81,203 due to its two to three times higher radiation dosage.5,81 CBCT data on periapical healing100,101,109,163 naturally yields lower healed rates and longer durations for complete healing. It is noteworthy, however, that the periapical diagnostic accuracy of CBCT was lower for root-filled teeth than for those that were not root filled,100 while 42% of periapical lesions observed by CBCT following root-end surgery were found to have no inflammation in the biopsy specimens enucleated during repeated surgery.101

Periapical healing is judged to be successful when both radiographic and clinical criteria have been satisfied;63,144 a small proportion of cases can present with persistent symptoms, despite complete radiographic resolution of the periapical lesion.176

## Rationale for evaluating outcomes

Confidence and assurance are desirable in the validity and predictability of an offered procedure such as root canal treatment.
This is typically obtained from pooled outcome data drawn from a number of suitable studies, resulting in a large sample of patients and managed by an extensive cohort of dentists, all following consensus guidelines. Pooled homogenous outcome data also facilitate the analysis and ranking of factors that exert a dominant influence on outcomes. Such data can inform treatment protocol refinement for progressive perfection of technical performance. Weighing the relative importance of individual factors through research can help to pinpoint the key biologic, technical, and clinical factors at play, and how to best manage their deployment clinically.

Population-based outcome data may not placate patients about their personal care by a given dentist, whereas personal audit outcome data of that dentist may persuade them that their dentist’s skill and knowledge are indeed adequate for their care provision.
Such data also serve the dual purpose of motivating and directing

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1120 -->

practitioners to refine their technique and knowledge to further improve their outcomes and make them more predictable.33

Predictability and consistency of personal treatment outcomes also aids practitioners in refining their prognostication skills (predicting, projecting, prophesizing, or foretelling the likely outcome of treatment). Insight about factors affecting treatment outcome further helps to refine and fine-tune that process.

The overall prognosis of a tooth is dependent on three individual prognoses (including that for the endodontic, periodontic, and restorative aspects), each of which subsumes a further set of subsidiary factors that influence the overall summative prognosis. In addition, the strategic importance of the involved tooth should be considered relative to its position in the dental arch and its potential contribution to the dynamic occlusion and overall dental function.

Clinically, there may be two distinct ways to estimate prognosis:
1) to clinically apply heuristic principles and intuitively gauge the effect of dominant factors; or, on the other extreme, 2) to mathematically input comprehensive data into an algorithmic model to calculate the estimated outcome. At present, only the former approach is possible.

## Types and levels of clinical outcome evidence

The outcome of endodontic treatment may be assessed by the same four dimensions used in other medical disciplines.11 The first dimension is physical/physiologic and relates to the presence or absence of pulpal or periapical health, disease, pain, and function.
The second dimension assesses longevity or tooth survival, the third relates to economics and assesses direct and indirect costs, and the fourth examines psychologic aspects involving perceptions of oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) and esthetics.

Dentists’ insight into whether or not a procedure works derives from multiple sources, and the dominance of influence over their thinking varies throughout their careers. At the beginning of such a dental career, this insight is received as dogma from teachers, which in itself may have been synthesized from a mixture of sources. As dentists begin to actually practice, they learn firsthand what works and what does not. This helps them to refine their concepts and ideas from an empirical perspective and may lead to satisfaction with their

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1123 -->

approach until it is challenged by colleagues, patients, or through peer-calibration at postgraduate lectures and workshops. Such challenges are either met with indifference or with the motivation to enquire more deeply into the truth of the matter. In search of such truth, dentists may encounter various types and levels of evidence to inform their enquiry. Different types of studies have been ranked into a hierarchy (Fig 20-5) based on the methodological quality of their design, validity, and applicability to patient care.

The highest level of evidence is derived from the synthesis of quality primary data by a select group of workers using a systematic and accepted process. It identifies appropriate material, screens for matching entry criteria, and filters for quality. The process also extracts useable raw data and pools it in order to calculate an estimated overall outcome, based on which agreed guidelines may be drawn. The purpose is to use data to inform and recommend best practice. The intention is to propagate a standardization of approach to raise overall levels of performance by the dentist population working for the overall patient population. The flaw in the system is that progressive correction in dental practice by an individual dentist, a craft professional, is not purely information based. Correction requires integrated modification of numerous domains through dedicated, diligent practice, of which the given information (guideline) forms only a small part.33 Paradoxically, the best study outcomes are influenced by best individual practitioner performances in the studied cohorts, yet in the hierarchy of evidence, their opinion is relegated to the bottom of the pyramid.

A surgical intervention is subject to enormous variation in the way it is performed, depending on individual interpretation and execution of the protocol by each operator, even when a standardized treatment protocol is followed. Root canal treatment is a sequential, multistep procedure, each prospective step being dependent on the previous one for its efficacy. From a research perspective, characterizing and accurately recording any variation in protocol execution is challenging because many aspects and steps must be diligently recorded. It is important to consider the effect of individual steps (factors) alone as well as any interaction between interdependent steps inherent in the procedure. These issues may be controlled in well-designed and executed randomized controlled trials but are at risk of bias due to recruitment and drop-out problems, particularly in small-scale trials.
Strict participant selection criteria may also limit the generalizability of findings. In addition, the effects of preoperative factors and some treatment steps cannot be randomized and can only be accounted for or investigated in observational studies. Well-designed randomized controlled trials and population-based observational studies, therefore, provide useful complementary data.24

## Outcomes of nonsurgical root canal treatment

The number of studies and extent of investigation of nonsurgical root canal treatment are more comprehensive than is the case for other endodontic procedures. The studies provide a breadth and depth of insight, although the quality and scope of the research does not always reach the highest defined hierarchical levels.

Numerous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have been performed on the outcomes of root canal treatment. ## Factors affecting periapical health or healing

## following root canal treatment

The factors influencing the healing of periapical lesions following root canal treatment (or the maintenance of periapical health in the absence of lesions) may be classified into patient factors (age, sex, general health, tooth anatomy, preoperative pulpal and periapical status), treatment factors (operator characteristics, canal shaping/enlargement, irrigation, medication, culture testing, obturation), and postoperative restorative factors (type of restorative material, full or partial coverage, time of permanent restoration, abutment or non-abutment, occlusal dynamics). Some of these

factors have a profound impact on periapical healing, while others have a negligible effect. Patient factors characterizing the nature of disease showed the most potent effect (periapical status), while most of the treatment factors, individually, were found to exert a weak effect; the exceptions were the apical extent of root canal treatment relative to the root canal terminus, the quality of the root filling, and the quality of the postoperative restorative care (which showed a profound influence on periapical health).

Effect of age, sex, and health

The key demographic factors of a patient’s age and sex, where assessed, have no significant influence on outcomes, although it may be anticipated that youth and old age may produce some effect due to differing immune responsiveness. Paradoxically, the host immune response characterized by the general health of the patient shows only a weak influence.
Although specific health conditions

(diabetes,51,61 compromised innate immune response117) apparently have a significant influence, there is limited evidence on the pathway and strength of effect.
Emerging evidence suggests that polymorphisms of various genes involved in periapical healing may have an effect on outcomes58,122,133,169,187,216,221,222 (Table 20-3).
The probable importance of the host immune response in periapical healing is iterated by the statistically significant clustering of like outcomes (fast, slow, or delayed healing) associated with multiple teeth within the same patient.142

Effect of tooth type

The common perception that single-rooted teeth should exhibit higher rates of periapical healing than multirooted teeth proves to be untrue.
It may just be that the simpler canal anatomy of single-rooted teeth, coupled with easier access, allows practitioners to feel more comfortable to complete root canal treatment on anterior teeth.
Conversely, the more difficult access to posterior teeth, coupled with multiple roots with more severe canal curvatures, may pose greater challenges, perception of difficulty, and likelihood of failure.105 Those studies that reported better outcomes for anterior teeth generally have not considered the confounding effect of periapical disease.
Having accounted for such confounding effects, it is found that tooth type does not exert a strong influence on periapical healing rates. The explanation for a more or less equal success rate for both anterior and posterior teeth may lie in the similar canal complexities of the more hidden apical anatomy of these different tooth types than those more readily seen coronally.10

Effect of pulpal and periapical status

The preoperative pulp status, whether vital or necrotic, does not have any influence on the outcome of root canal treatment (Fig 20-8), unless there is a superimposed periapical lesion. The most dominant influence on periapical healing is exerted by the presence (Fig 20-9) and size (Fig 20-10) of a periapical lesion,142,143 which must therefore

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1133 -->

be accounted for when analyzing the influence of any other factor. For example, the perception that pulp necrosis compromised treatment outcome was confounded by periapical status; having accounted for it, the clinical results tallied with the finding that pulp necrosis per se did not impact periapical health.131 The profound influence of a periapical lesion may be correlated with infection in the apical canal anatomy, which is difficult to eradicate.138,264 The negative influence of larger periapical lesions may be correlated with the greater diversity of bacteria (number of species and their relative abundance) in such teeth,244 making the infection more likely to persist.31 Other explanations may also be advanced such as that larger lesions may be associated with longer-standing root canal infections, allowing deeper penetration of bacteria into dentinal tubules and the accessory anatomy,213 where mechanical and chemical decontamination procedures may not readily reach. Furthermore, larger lesions may also potentially represent cystic transformation,135

which may more readily hinder healing.136 Finally, the host response may also play a part, as patients with larger lesions may innately respond less favorably to residual bacteria.138

Other preoperative factors such as pain, tooth tenderness to percussion, soft tissue tenderness to palpation, swelling, sinus tract, periodontal probing defects of endodontic origin, and root resorption are correlated with or are manifestations of periapical disease.280

Thus, they may complement the influence of the “presence and size of the periapical lesion” in predicting the course of periapical healing.
So far, preoperative pain,62 sinus tract,142 swelling,142 and apical resorption242 have been found to act as prognostic factors, significantly reducing the probability of periapical healing after root canal treatment.

The negative influence of sinus tract and swelling represents chronic and acute forms of suppuration and proliferation of microbiota into the periapical tissues, presumably hindering periapical healing directly, although the precise mechanisms remain unclear.

### Treatment factors

Clinical versus biologic conundrum

The paradox of the importance of biologic insight over technical skill in the performance of root canal treatment was highlighted a century ago: “We are not trained to think in terms of biological concepts but we are to act in mechanical procedures” (Noyes, 1922).22 Despite the improvements in the technical quality of root fillings through training in nickel titanium (NiTi) instrumentation and single-cone root filling, a parallel improvement in periapical healing status96 was not evident, reinforcing the above comment by Noyes.22

Most clinicians would anticipate or would hope that the treatment they provide for their patients would have a major influence on the biologic outcome of periapical healing. While it is evident that root canal treatment procedures have an impact on root canal microbiota through load reduction,28-31 the precise relationship between the practical steps and bacterial demise are unclear and uncharted.
Given the technical complexity of root canal treatment procedures, the dentist’s mind is predominantly preoccupied with gaining access to the root canal system, finding all the canals, negotiating them without creating a blockage, enlarging them without creating an iatrogenic error, and filling the canal system completely to achieve radiographically satisfactory fillings. Only rarely will dentists have infection control uppermost in their minds during the execution of a procedure. The question arises as to whether there is any evidence that “biologically oriented” practitioners might enjoy higher rates of periapical healing in their treated cases compared with those who are
“technically oriented.”

It is not only the refined and insightful technical execution that matters, but also how the overall understanding of the biologic problem influences intraoperative decision making by the operator, and crucially, the motivation and integrity with which the procedure is performed (elements difficult to measure).

The present authors conducted a study at the London Eastman using multilevel modeling to account for the relative influence of operator, patient, tooth, and procedure on periapical healing and iatrogenic outcomes. The study revealed only a moderate degree of operator influence (14% of total variation) on the periapical healing outcome, and an even smaller degree (4% of total variation) on iatrogenic errors.285
Meanwhile, patient-level characteristics accounted for 17%, and tooth-level characteristics for 69% of the total variation of the healing outcome, while patient-level (26%) and tooth-level (70%) characteristics accounted for most of the variation in the iatrogenic error outcome. The findings imply that the dominant influence on outcome arises from factors not under the direct control of the operator. Contemporary protocols do not yet allow absolute control over the predictable management of cases with high biologic and technical complexity.

a) Effect of rubber dam isolation
It is surprising that the role of the commonly recommended, but often abandoned, use of rubber dam in modern root canal treatment is devoid of solid outcome data to support its adoption. The principal justification for rubber dam use is based on patient safety and the medicolegal implications of root canal instrument inhalation by the patient.55 However, by “mining” available data, one study on retreatment263 was found to have significantly higher success rates for root canal retreatment when rubber dam was used compared with cotton roll isolation. A second observational study reported significantly higher success rates for post placement in root canal-

<!-- PDF page 1140 -->

treated teeth68 when rubber dam was used compared with when it was not. Overall, the prevailing view in the profession is that from the numerous perspectives of safety, medicolegal compliance, improved access, control and disinfection of the operative field, and some evidence of improved treatment outcome, rubber dam isolation is the preferred approach.

b) Effect of access cavity preparation
Access cavity design is influenced by the precise anatomical juxtaposition of the pulp chamber and its proximity to the tooth surface, providing the most direct and straight-line access to the apical parts of the root canal system. The location and direction of the initial and final cut are tailored to balance the needs of access with the risk of damaging tooth structure that may affect its strength or esthetics, leading to traditional access cavity designs. Traditional versus straight-line access cavity designs influence the ability to plane canal walls during root canal instrumentation.115

More recently, the availability of CBCT imaging and 3D data sets has led to the notion of designing far more conservative access cavities (called “conservative access cavities,” “ultra-conservative access cavities,” or “ninja access cavities”) for the purposes of preserving dentin in order to increase tooth survival.175 A prime goal of the restoration of root-treated teeth is tooth structure preservation and protection, so that in biomechanical function, such teeth would exhibit more favorable stresses;277 however, whether they increase fracture strength in vivo remains a moot point.154,196

The crucial issue with conservative access cavity designs is whether they compromise root canal system preparation, debridement, and obturation. At present, these questions are only answered by laboratory studies,260 and longer-term clinical trials are awaited. There are no periapical healing or tooth survival data at present to support more conservative access cavity preparations.

Having accessed the pulp chamber, a primary requirement is optimal visualization of that chamber to identify all canal orifices so as to facilitate their negotiation and enlargement to prerequisite dimensions. The virtues of magnification and illumination during this phase, and indeed for most of the root canal treatment procedure, are almost universally extolled by endodontists162 because of the feeling of more control over the process. However, a systematic review failed to identify objective evidence for this view.48 The use of magnification has not been found to have a significant influence on the outcome of root canal treatment,142 although it may assist in the location of additional canals, contributing a small difference to the probability of periapical healing.142 A definitive view on the benefit of a microscope may emerge through a randomized controlled trial, but on current evidence and the fact that the main barrier to improved periapical healing is the control of apical intraradicular infection, a positive effect is highly unlikely.

d) Effect of mechanical root canal enlargement and its extension to the canal terminus
The purpose of canal enlargement is to facilitate the delivery of an irrigant for disinfection and finally the root filling material to the entire extremities of the root canal system. The precise dimensions of the optimal shape to facilitate these elements remains a matter of debate. Review of the relevant in vitro and clinical studies reveals an absence of definitive evidence.17 Furthermore, it is impossible to divorce considerations of mechanical enlargement from root canal irrigation because they are intimately interlinked.

A variety of instruments of different cutting designs, tips, tapers, diameters, and materials of construction have been employed to mechanically enlarge the root canal system to a defined size and taper.205 While laboratory studies may testify to their properties, efficacy, and utility,84 their influence in efficacy of clinical canal enlargement appears only in two nonrandomized prospective studies.96,142 In the first,142 the better success rates for NiTi

instruments (hand or rotary) compared with stainless steel instruments were attributed to the prior tactile skills development when using stainless steel files by the senior students. The ability to gain and maintain apical patency as well as to avoid procedural errors would be better instilled in such students. A further confounding factor may be that such senior students would be more likely to have already established a better understanding of biologic rationale and intraoperative decision making. NiTi instruments also appear capable of achieving the same in selected mild- to moderate-complexity primary root canal treatment cases undertaken by undergraduates.174

It is well accepted that root canal debridement should be extended to the terminus of the canal system. The goal is expressed in different ways as the extension to the “apical constriction,” or to
“0.5 to 2 mm from the radiographic apex,” or to the “cementodentinal junction” (European Society of Endodontology, 2006).55 The guideline is underpinned by the twin observations that there was a twofold reduction in periapical healing when patency to the canal terminus was not achieved or when the canal was obstructed.28,30,132 Lack of mechanical negotiability of canals may be attributed to many factors, including the presence of undetected acute curvatures, division of the main canal into a fine plexus of apical canals, natural obstructions (due to “denticles” or tertiary dentin), or dentinal/organic debris accumulation due to poor instrumentation or irrigation.

A number of studies have considered the effect of apical size of canal preparation on treatment outcome.82,91,142,198,236,242
A randomized controlled trial showed that the enlargement of the canal to three sizes larger than the first file to bind apically was, on average, the ISO 30, and it was sufficient for periapical healing.198

Observational studies82,142,242 not specifically designed to investigate the effect of apical canal size (but which nevertheless measured this parameter) found no statistically significant influence attributable to this factor. However, interestingly, they all reported the same inverse counterintuitive trend of decreasing periapical healing rates with the increasing size of apical preparation. A speculative explanation for this clear trend from three independent studies is that canal

Coupled with the apical irrigation stagnation zone, the generated stagnant dentinal and organic debris may congeal, first into a slurry and then into what is clinically termed
“dentin mud,” to create either a passable (“pickable”) or a definitive blockage. Under such circumstances, the impatient or neophyte dentist may fail to resist the temptation to force instruments to their designated lengths, resulting in the classically described procedural errors of apical transportation, canal straightening, and perforation.
This proposal does not, however, explain the higher failure in initially large canals, where presumably immature roots present a different debridement challenge. In such cases, where the canal shape is less amenable to planing by conventional instruments, it is speculated that an intracanal brush with a larger effective diameter may provide better debridement. The available study findings reject the intuitively
“logical” notion of more effective bacterial debridement with larger apical preparations.32,160,188

Just as chemical and mechanical (or chemomechanical) debridement are better considered together, apical preparation size should be considered alongside the taper of the entire canal.
Guideline55 recommendations stipulate only that canal preparation should be tapered from crown to apex, without recommending taper size. Procedural errors during root canal preparation are common among junior dentists and are typically classified into canal blockage, uncontrolled canal shape (ledge formation, apical zipping and transportation, straightening of canal curvature), tooth/root perforation (sub-classified into pulp chamber or radicular level), or separation of instruments. Amongst these, the effect of canal blockage has been reported above, while the influence of uncontrolled canal shape has not been specifically investigated.

Iatrogenic root perforations result in significantly lower chances of periapical healing.40,43,87,119,142,225 The further specific effects of location and size of the perforation, any time lapse before the defect repair, and adequacy of the perforation seal have not been reported.

e) Effect of root canal irrigation
A diverse range of chemical agents has been used to irrigate root canal systems in clinical practice, singly or in various combinations, many (but not all) of which have been investigated to varying extents.

f) Effect of number of treatment visits
The disinfection requirements for teeth without established root canal infection (such as when elective pulpectomy is performed) is very different from the requirements for teeth that have established root canal infection and apical periodontitis. In the latter teeth, even rigorous disinfectant irrigation is not completely effective in eliminating all the adherent bacterial biofilm,138 leaving residual bacteria to multiply and recolonize the canal system.27,29 It is therefore desirable to spread treatment over at least two visits and to use the interappointment period to dress the canal with a long-lasting or slowrelease antibacterial agent capable of destroying or incapacitating residual bacteria, as well as to take the opportunity to gauge the initial periapical response before root filling. Calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) has served in this capacity for many years because of its ability to dissolve organic tissue, kill bacteria, detoxify antigenic material, and act as a slow-release agent because of its low solubility in an aqueous environment. However, its antibacterial ability has come under close scrutiny recently, with advocates suggesting that the material is not suitable for purpose.200 A final resolution to this debate is yet to be derived based on robust clinical evidence.

Despite the definitive evidence that bacteria remain in the root canal system following chemomechanical debridement, there remain advocates of single-visit treatment. Clearly, for those cases lacking infection in the apical root canal anatomy, marked by an absence of a preoperative periapical lesion, the case for single-visit treatment is strong because it may better serve to prevent bacterial contamination. However, the biologic rationale for single-visit treatment is less clear in cases in which a preoperative periapical lesion exists. There is ongoing controversy and debate between specialists and dentists about the merits and demerits of singleversus multiple-visit treatments.
The debate is fueled by considerations of cost-effectiveness,207 practical sense, economic sense, postoperative pain,149 and biologic rationale.237 Various randomized controlled trials 66,130,159,167,171,255,279,281 on the subject have found no significant influence attributable to the number of visits, but these studies all lacked statistical power and were compromised by potential bias related to recruitment and management protocols for cases not completed within the designated number of visits.59,60,201,243

The debate about the merits of single- or multiple-visit treatments is likely to continue unabated, given the respective strengths of the motivational drivers among the opposing groups. The issue may only be resolved by properly documented, large randomized controlled trials (currently unavailable), because unrecorded confounders (operator skill, biologic or technical case complexity, patient and recruitment bias) underlying the factor “number of treatment visits” will continue to play out their biasing effect in nonrandomized studies.

h) Effect of quality control of root canal disinfection
The focal infection era (discussed above) spawned the technique of quality checking bacterial disinfection using an interappointment culture test prior to root filling. Obturation would only be deployed upon the return of a negative culture test result, “confirming” the absence of bacteria in the sampleable part of the root canal system.25,64,134 This quality control practice fell out of clinical favor due to the perceived deficiencies involved, coupled with the perceived predictability and favorable prognosis of root canal treatment without microbiologic sampling.
Cost-effectiveness and the economic imperative played a part in the establishment of this trend. Apart from having a low benefit–cost ratio, microbial sampling procedures were considered lengthy, difficult, inaccurate, and resource-rich in terms of laboratory support.128,129

j) Effect of root filling material and technique
Investigation of the effects of the obturation technique and root filling material on treatment outcome are complicated by the interaction between core root-filling material, sealer (acting as a luting agent), and the technique for their placement. k) Effect of apical extent of root filling
Of the many intraoperative factors under the control of the operator, the apical extent of root-filling material has been the most frequently and thoroughly investigated, probably because in retrospective studies it offers a readily and constantly available, non-degrading assessment measure. For the purposes of statistical analyses, the apical extent of root fillings has been classified into three categories:
1) more than 2 mm short of the radiographic apex (short); 2) 0 to 2

<!-- PDF page 1156 -->

mm within the radiographic apex (flush); and 3) extended beyond the radiographic apex (long).143 This parameter has a significant influence on periapical healing rates, regardless of periapical status.142,143

“Flush” root-fillings yielded the highest success rates (81%), while
“long” root-fillings yielded the lowest (66%).143

Retrospective root canal treatment outcome studies were unable to distinguish between the effects of the apical extent of instrumentation versus the apical extent of obturation because the relevant information was unrecorded. The prospective London
Eastman study,142 however, did measure these parameters separately and found them both to affect periapical healing, independently and significantly. The separate measures correlated with each other, consistent with the fact that canals are normally filled to the same extent as canal preparation. A single measure – “apical extent of root filling” – was therefore considered to be able to inform about both the apical extent of canal cleaning as well as obturation.
The unrecorded exceptions would be overextension of instrumentation or extrusion of cleaning agents during canal preparation without rootfilling extrusion. The same argument would apply for root-filling material extrusion without prior apical disturbance during preparation.

This issue of the apical extent of root-filling material has generated an extraordinary level and extent of debate in the discipline between polar opposite views, despite the extremely clear and onesided outcome data. Radiographic evidence of “sealer puffs” extruding through the main apical foramina and lateral/accessory canals has been pursued with zeal, as a “school of thought,” in the undaunted belief of its value as “good practice” by some endodontists. Their argument is that the ability to extrude the sealer is a measure of the debridement quality of the important apical root canal system, and they ardently argue that, as a consequence, healing would predictably follow, albeit with some delay.
Unfortunately, such advocates fail to advance clinical research evidence for their views.

The collective outcome data are categorically clear, however, that the extrusion of root-filling material hinders periapical healing.

Extrusion of cleaning, medication, or filling materials (core or sealer) beyond the apical terminus into the surrounding tissues may cause delayed healing or treatment failure. The mechanisms of action are persistent inflammation and foreign body reaction.98,137,227,286

Guttapercha may be contaminated by magnesium and silicon particles from the talc used in their manufacture (at least historically), and can induce a foreign body reaction.137 Experimental implantation of large pieces of gutta-percha subcutaneously in guinea pigs became encapsulated in collagen, but fine particles of gutta-percha induced an intense, localized tissue response.227 Nevertheless, clinical data do not show an exactly parallel effect on periapical healing based on the extent of extrusion.142,143 Clinical studies may have the additional problem of potential bacterial contamination of the extruded guttapercha.

The effects of sealer extrusion into the periapical tissues have drawn contradictory conclusions. Friedman et al62 found that the extrusion of a glass ionomer-based sealer significantly reduced periapical healing rates, while Ng et al142 reported that the extrusion of a ZOE-based sealer showed no significant effect on periapical healing, although only about a quarter of the cases exhibited such extrusion. The discrepancy may arguably be attributed to the difference in sealer type and the duration of treatment follow-up.
Radiographic assessment of sealer resorption may be complicated by the radiolucency of its toxic components and the insufficient sensitivity of radiographic methods to detect trace amounts.142 The radiographic disappearance of extruded sealer may be due to the disintegration or dispersal of the radiopaque component (barium sulphate) but may still be resident in the vicinity.137

It was found that some extruded sealers (glass ionomer-based,62

ZOE-based,86 silicone-based,86 Endomethasone [Septodont, SaintMaur-des-Fossés, France23]) were not resorbed from the periapical tissues after 1 year. In another study, traces of Ca(OH)2-based sealer (Sealapex; SybronEndo – Sybron Dental Specialties,
Glendona, CA, USA) could still be detected after 3 years.199 Longer-

l) Effect of quality of root filling
One of the widely investigated parameters of root canal treatment has been the radiographic measure of “quality of root filling.” This is, once again, due to its ready, constant, and undegraded availability in retrospective studies. “Quality of root filling” describes the radiographic perception of its completeness in extending to the apical and lateral boundary walls of the canal system without voids or extrusion. The stated rationale for complete obturation of the root canal system is to prevent bacterial recontamination from any residual canal infection or newly invading bacteria from coronal, apical, or lateral sources. Both residual or new microbial invaders may be prevented from recolonizing the root canal system by a “tight” seal with the canal wall and an absence of voids within the body of the material. “Quality of root filling” may be taken as a surrogate measure for either a poor root-filling technique or even a poor root canal treatment procedure in its entirety, since good obturation is reliant upon properly executed preliminary steps in canal preparation.
A systematic review143 reported that the criteria for judging the quality of root fillings remain poorly defined,43,82,225 with the use of vague phrases such as “unsatisfactory root filling,” “inadequate seal,”
“poor apical seal,” or “radiographic presence of voids.” The main problem, however, is that it is an attempt to grade the 3D relationship

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1159 -->

between root-filling material and the canal system using a 2D detection method that lacks adequate sensitivity. Despite the severe inadequacies in the approach to judging root-filling quality,
“satisfactory root fillings” are nevertheless found to be consistently associated with significantly higher success rates than “unsatisfactory root fillings.”143

m) Effect of acute exacerbation during treatment
Any invasive operative procedure is liable to elicit some pain or discomfort by virtue of the inherent injury triggering an inflammatory response, and in addition will be superimposed upon any preexisting inflammatory and prevailing immune response. If the preexisting pain is severe, treatment should not make it substantially worse. However, when preoperative pain is absent, mild, or moderate, treatment may induce pain or make it worse for 24 to 48 hours;253 this is normal, and patients should be forewarned and forearmed, psychologically and, if necessary, pharmaceutically. In rare situations, the trajectory of pain may worsen over the following days, which may be due to adverse alterations in the microbiota, an immune reaction, or neuropathic sensitization. The precise etiopathogenesis of such interappointment pain is ill-defined but could be triggered by chemical, mechanical, or microbial injury to the periradicular tissues, conditioned by psychologic209,210 or neurologic influences.148 Although such pain did not have a significant association with periapical healing in two studies,91,225 the London Eastman study142 found that pain or swelling occurring in 15% of cases after chemomechanical debridement significantly reduced the probability of periapical healing. The explanation for this may reside in incomplete chemomechanical debridement leading to a shift in canal microbial ecology, favoring the growth of more virulent microorganisms or the extrusion of contaminated material during canal preparation or obturation, resulting in an acute or chronic foreign body reaction or (transient) extraradicular infection. The exact biologic mechanisms of failure in these cases remain obscure and warrant further investigation.

### Post root canal-treatment factors

Effect of quality and type of restoration

The quality and type of coronal restoration after root filling has a major impact on the probability of periapical healing. Teeth with
“satisfactory” coronal restorations exhibited threefold better periapical healing compared with those with “unsatisfactory” restorations.143

The definition of the term “satisfactory restoration” varies in detail, for example: 1) no evidence of marginal discrepancy; 2) no evidence of marginal discoloration; 3) no evidence of recurrent caries; and 4) absence of a history of decementation.82,185

Furthermore, the criteria for “unsatisfactory restorations”66,168 do not account for the presence of an intact inner core. To obviate this problem, the London Eastman study142 adopted the following standards for unsatisfactory restorations: 1) obvious signs of exposed root filling; and 2) potential leakage indicated by marginal defects and history of decementation. Applying these standards, the study revealed an extremely profound effect (OR = 10.7; 95% CI:
3.7, 31.5) of restorative status on the probability of periapical healing.

Other studies made different comparisons such as permanent versus temporary restorations,43,62,142 crown versus plastic restorations,43,62,142,225 presence versus absence of posts,62,142 and non-abutment versus abutment.142,225 Permanently restored teeth were associated with significantly higher periapical healing rates than their temporarily restored counterparts,43,62 but this was not always the case.37,142 The type of permanent restoration43,62,142,225 had no significant influence on periapical healing.

Provision of a sub-seal over the root filling to avert the impact of leakage seems wise, should the restoration margin fail or the restoration be lost altogether. Effect of imposed functional occlusal stresses on root-treated teeth

The stresses imposed on root-treated teeth are a function, firstly, of the manner in which the patient uses them, and secondly, their functional prominence in the occlusal scheme. The pattern of static and dynamic occlusal loading stresses in teeth is dictated by holding or guiding contacts as well as their status as single units or abutments (bridge/denture).
Prosthetic abutments (fixed or removable) may bear more unfavorable loads, as may last-standing teeth in the dental arch.121 Evidence suggests that such teeth exhibit lower periapical healing rates, presumably through the development of cracks and fractures due to fatigue.225

## Outcome of nonsurgical root canal retreatment

Root canal retreatment (or secondary root canal treatment) is deployed when primary root canal treatment fails to resolve periapical disease. It is considered appropriate to retreat the tooth using conventional approaches first, particularly when the primary root canal treatment proved to be technically unsatisfactory (Fig 20-12a).
This requires the removal of any obstructing restorative material plus

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1163 -->

previous root-filling material to re-access the root canal system in its entirety, particularly the contaminated apical anatomy. Ideally, this should be achieved without any dentin removal. The next steps involve re-gauging the prepared dimensions of the canal system to check or modify its shape into an optimal form (if not previously so), and correct any iatrogenic procedural errors, including blockages, to facilitate better irrigation and optimal re-obturation (Fig 20-12b and c). The periapical healing rates of root canal retreatment are generally perceived to be slightly lower compared with primary treatment due to persistently obstructed access to the apical infection and/or potentially more resistant microbiota.

The mean weighted probability of complete periapical healing is
78% (Fig 20-13), about 6% lower than in the case of primary treatment on teeth with apical periodontitis (Fig 20-14).140,142 On the other hand, the survival rate of teeth having undergone root canal retreatment is similar to that for primary root canal treatment.141

The factors influencing the outcome of periapical health and tooth survival after root canal retreatment were otherwise identical to those affecting primary root canal treatment. Of the potential prognostic factors unique to retreatment cases, the main one showing significant influence on outcome after treatment was the ability to remove or bypass preexisting root-filling material or a separated instrument during retreatment to achieve canal patency at the terminus.142

## root canal retreatment

The technical elements of mechanical and chemical canal preparation have become more efficient and predictable over the past two decades, but these improvements have not accrued a parallel increase in the probability of periapical healing for either primary (Fig

It is notable that all the factors having a strong influence on periapical health after root canal treatment are associated in some way with root canal infection. ## Outcome of periapical surgical treatment

When the orthograde approach (root canal treatment or retreatment) to the apical root anatomy (and infection) is irretrievably obstructed, or there is reason to suppose that the periapical tissues need to be excised, debrided, or sampled due to a suspected extraradicular

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1171 -->

infection or other pathoses, a periapical surgical or retrograde approach may be more appropriate. The knowledge and skillset for periapical surgery are different from those for root canal treatment and may not fall within the competences of all dentists. This is increasingly so because the high success rates of root canal retreatment have led to a reduction in the need for periapical surgery.
Patients whose psychologic bearing on root canal treatment is generally negative (wrongly, as they usually find) may view the option of surgery even more unfavorably.52,65,191

### Outcomes of historical versus contemporary protocols

Systematic reviews evaluating the prognostic factors for periapical surgery47-49,146,211,212,257-259,272 have the limitations, firstly, that the duration of follow-up after treatment is not controlled, and secondly, that the radiographic criteria applied to judge successful periapical healing is mostly “loose.” A meta-analysis by the present authors of extracted data revealed the weighted pooled probability of radiographically judged complete healing to be 69% (95% CI: 65%,
73%) after periapical surgery with retrograde cavity filling (Fig 2016). The forest plot in Figure 20-16 shows higher periapical healing rates in more recent studies. The trend, but not the actual proportion, is consistent with the much higher pooled probability of success of
92% (95% CI: 86%, 95%) quoted in the meta-analysis of prospective outcome data of surgical endodontic treatments performed using the so-called “modern” technique (involving magnification, root-end resection with minimal or no bevel, retrograde cavity preparation with ultrasonic tips, and modern retrograde root canal filling).258,259

Setzer et al212 drew a similar conclusion by comparing data pooled separately for the “microsurgery” (94%; 95% CI: 89%, 98%) or
“traditional” root-end surgery (59%; 95% CI: 55%, 63%) approaches.
However, this analysis may have exaggerated the differences due to differences in design, case selection, follow-up duration, and prior nonsurgical treatment.

### Outcomes related to duration of follow-up

The meta-analysis of the present authors (referred to above), stratified by the duration following surgical treatment, showed that complete periapical healing plateaued after 2 years postoperatively (Fig 20-17), being 51% (95% CI: 42%, 60%) after 6 months, 70% (95% CI: 65%, 74%) after 12 months, 75% (95% CI: 65%, 83%) after 24 months, 76% (95% CI: 55%, 93%) after 48 months, and
70% (95% CI: 64%, 77%) after more than 48 months. It is therefore advisable to follow up periapical surgery cases for a minimum of 2 years, and, better still, for up to 4 years, as suggested in quality guidelines,55 or even longer in specific cases. Although the predictive value of the cases classified as healed at 1 year and remaining so over the 10-year follow-up period was high (87%), many of the delayed failures were detected between 5 and 10 years postoperatively, and were associated with maxillary incisors affected by trauma in childhood, or maxillary premolars and mandibular molars with complex apical root canal anatomy.271

Effect of tooth type

On the whole, root-end surgery outcome studies published up to the early 1990s mainly incorporated single-rooted anterior teeth, but since then posterior teeth have been increasingly managed and evaluated for outcome. The higher success rates associated with

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1176 -->

In stark contrast, maxillary lateral incisors were more frequently associated with scar tissue healing,72,192 attributed to the palatal curvature of the root apex and a consequent higher propensity for perforation of the palatal cortical plate, resulting in “through and through” periapical defects after labial surgical access. Despite these observations, the present authors’ meta-analysis found that tooth type had a minimal effect on root-end surgery outcome.124 However, roots with an isthmus between two canals in molar teeth were associated with a higher chance of failure, most frequently attributable to vertical root fracture.92 According to current practice, the isthmus was included in the retrograde preparation to form a cavity that might have weakened the root end and rendered it more susceptible to fracture.92,271 Therefore, a revised guideline for canal isthmus management may be needed.

Effect of periapical status

The extent and nature of the preoperative periapical disease – characterized by clinical (swelling, sinus, pain) and radiographic (size of radiolucency) presentation, extent of bone defect, and histopathologic diagnosis – has a profound influence on periapical surgery outcome. This is consistent with the trends observed for conventional root canal treatment. The size of the preoperative periapical bony defect has a strong negative impact on root-end surgery outcome, although the exact biologic causes are ill-defined.
Speculative reasons to explain this observation include the possibilities that pathologic tissues in larger periapical lesions may be more difficult to remove in their entirety and require a longer duration for complete bone repair, potentially by secondary intention fibrous healing, particularly when both buccal and palatal cortical plates have been eroded.

Endodontic surgery involves the elevation of healthy soft tissue to expose the periapical lesion, which may be complicated by the prior

and natural absence of labial bone, known as root dehiscence. This results in the so-called apicomarginal defect, which normally heals with the attachment of junctional epithelium along the denuded root surface and into the periapical bony crypt,4 resulting in periapical scar healing.99,234 The suggestion that such an attachment may be more susceptible to periodontal breakdown257 does not, however, accord with the periodontal literature.18

Swelling, sinus tract, or symptoms at the time of periapical surgery have a significant negative impact on periapical healing,99,269

possibly because they represent “extraradicular” infection, which may be more difficult to eradicate.272

Biopsied periapical tissues most frequently return a histopathologic diagnosis of “granuloma” or “cyst;”80 however, once successfully enucleated, and once the infection source has been controlled, the nature of the diseased tissue is unlikely to influence healing.80,107,124,291

Effect of prior endodontic and restorative status

The quality of previous root canal treatment (including coronal restoration and root filling) as well as the history of nonsurgical retreatment or periapical surgery influence surgical outcome.
Although not many studies have provided information about the restorative status, an adequate coronal restoration is considered a prerequisite for performing periapical surgery.178 A satisfactorily disinfected and obturated root canal system is sometimes considered to be a prerequisite for periapical surgery,114 but a key indicator for the approach is the inability to conventionally access the apical canal terminus. A number of studies using multivariable regression models to control confounding showed no significant influence of the density124,178 or extent124,233,267 of preexisting root fillings. However, the Toronto study14,276 reported that a preoperative root filling of adequate length was associated with an increased odds of disease persistence after surgery compared with teeth with short or overextended preexisting root fillings. In this study, though, a root-end

Arguably better circumstances for healing may be provided by performing more complete retrograde root canal treatment incorporating canal instrumentation and obturation.182

Effect of repeat surgery

Technically well-performed root-end surgery may still fail due to persistent periapical disease; the causes include residual intra- or extraradicular infection or new intraradicular infection (through coronal leakage or root fracture). Repeat surgery adopting a different strategy or approach may succumb to the same failure, explaining the consistently reported poorer prognosis in such cases124,172,272 (Fig

### Surgical treatment factors

The influence of new treatment protocols adopting magnification, microsurgical instruments, and new bioactive root-end filling materials have been tested since the 1990s.

Effect of magnification and illumination

Adoption of magnification and illumination to view the surgical site allows better surgical management of the soft tissue, bony crypt, root surface, and root canal. Meta-analyses124,256,267 of data on the use of magnification show a significantly higher success rate compared with not using it. However, a comparison of the type of magnification (magnifying loupes, surgical microscope, or endoscope)47,48,259

showed no significant difference, except in one report,258 which found that a surgical microscope or endoscope achieved significantly better outcomes than magnifying loupes. However, the finding did not hold

Other contemporary adoptions or changes to root-end surgery procedures include back exhaust air-rotor handpieces for osteotomy and apicectomy, minimal bevel root-end resection, ultrasonic root-end cavity preparation, and contemporary retrograde filling materials.

Effect of postoperative surgical condition

After surgery, serohemorrhagic exudate under the flap, or even an abscess, was not significantly associated with periapical healing.194

The influence of the quality of coronal restoration at review is consistent with the literature on nonsurgical root canal treatment.

## Summary of outcomes of periapical surgery

The improvement in outcome of periapical surgery has been attributed to modern surgical techniques plus a greater biologic awareness by clinicians.212,272 In addition, and perhaps even more importantly, case selection may be more critical in excluding potential failures, inferring that prognostication may have improved.

## Concluding remarks

Endodontic treatment may be summarized as the management of pulpal or periapical infected wounds through the application of ageold surgical principles of infection control and wound healing management. The procedures are tried and tested and predictably successful, albeit complicated by the location of the wound surface within or beyond the root.
