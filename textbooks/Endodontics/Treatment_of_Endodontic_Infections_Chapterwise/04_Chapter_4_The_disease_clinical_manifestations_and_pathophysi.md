Now that the two sides of the game have been introduced and discussed in the previous chapters (invaders vs defenders), it is time to focus on the outcome (disease) resulting from the encounter of these sides. In this chapter, the different forms of apical periodontitis are discussed along with their respective pathogenetic and pathophysiologic events as well as clinical and histopathologic features. Since apical periodontitis develops after pulp necrosis and infection, a brief discussion of the pulp response to caries exposure (the most common cause of pulp inflammation and necrosis) is necessary.

## Pulp response to infection

As with any other connective tissue in the body, the dental pulp responds to tissue injury with inflammation. Bacteria in caries lesions represent the most common source of aggression and antigens to the pulp (Fig 4-1).

Fig 4-1 Caries lesions represent the most common source of bacterial aggression and antigens to the pulp (courtesy Ricardo Carvalhaes Fraga).

Ricucci and Siqueira92 revealed that, as soon as the enamel is penetrated by caries, a localized inflammatory response can be observed in the subjacent pulp tissue. As the caries process advances to the dentin, causing tissue destruction and approaching the pulp, the inflammatory response increases in magnitude.
However, inflammation does not usually become severe to the point of being considered irreversible until the pulp is exposed (Fig 4-2).

The exposed pulp tissue comes into direct contact with oral bacteria from caries lesions, saliva, and/or plaque accumulated on the exposed surface, and almost invariably undergoes severe inflammation, followed by necrosis and infection (Fig 4-3). The time that elapses between pulp exposure and infection of the entire canal

<!-- PDF page 162 -->

is unpredictable, but it is usually a slow process that very often occurs by tissue increments.24,48,53,84,121

The microbiota associated with deep caries has been shown to be very diverse and is composed of a mixture of cariogenic and endodontic bacteria.16,17,28,55,61,62,70,96,97 Rôças et al95 examined samples taken from the deepest layer of dentinal caries lesions associated with exposed pulps in teeth with symptomatic irreversible pulpits and reported that half of the advanced caries lesions were dominated by lactobacilli. In the other specimens, the most dominant genera were Pseudoramibacter, Olsenella, Streptococcus, and
Stenotrophomonas.

Exposure of the pulp to bacteria induces local inflammation in the area subjected to aggression. Typical vascular events take place, including vasodilation and increased vascular permeability, resulting in exudation. This leads to the formation of edema with a consequent increase in tissue pressure, which may be critical to tissues encased in a low-compliance environment. If the aggression is severe, tissue pressure may exceed that of thin-walled venules, which are compressed and collapse. Consequently, drainage is impeded, and stagnation of blood flow not only promotes increased blood viscosity, but also impairs removal of waste products. This may lead to cell death and tissue necrosis. In addition, several bacterial products are toxic to host cells and may contribute to necrosis in the area.
Neutrophils attracted to the area may also contribute to tissue damage by releasing enzymes and oxygen-derived products that degrade tissue components (Fig 4-4). The intensity and duration of the aggression will influence the severity of the tissue response.

Indeed, the tissue pressure near the inflamed site is almost normal and shows no signs of severe inflammation, indicating that tissue pressure changes do not spread rapidly.48 A pressure difference of 8 to 10 mm Hg has been measured between inflamed and noninflamed adjacent areas of the pulp.41,121,131 In the area a few millimeters away from the inflamed area, tissue pressure has been shown to be very close to the normal pulp pressure. This difference in pressure can be a result of several edema-preventing mechanisms involved in the maintenance of the physiologic normality of the tissue that is not being directly insulted. The increase in tissue pressure may, in turn, force fluid back into lymphatics and capillaries in the nearby noninflamed tissue, thus lowering the tissue pressure.42,120,121

Resilience of the ground substance of the pulp tissue may help prevent the spread of pressure throughout the pulp.131

## Apical periodontitis

In a nutshell, apical periodontitis can be regarded as a sequel to caries. If left untreated, caries can lead to pulp inflammation. The reversibility of caries-associated pulp inflammation very often

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 170 -->

depends on the occurrence of exposure. In other words, pulpitis (pulp inflammation) can be reversed (by eliminating the cause) if the caries process has not caused pulp exposure. When pulp exposure occurs, the inflammation becomes irreversible in the sense that only removal of the caries tissue will not provide a predictable outcome, given that a focus of necrosis with bacterial colonization, of varying extent, has established in the pulp tissue. As mentioned before, the pulp inflammation, necrosis, and infection process advances toward the apical part of the canal from tissue compartments until the entire coronal and radicular pulp have become necrotic and infected. At that point, the periradicular tissues are also affected (see Fig 4-6). In some cases, apical periodontitis may develop even before necrosis/infection has reached the apical foramen (Fig 4-7).6,88,91,117

The development of apical periodontitis is a consequence of the innate and adaptive immune responses to intraradicular bacteria trying to prevent the spread of the infection to the bone and other host sites.101,123,125

### Response of periradicular tissues to bacterial injury

The intensity of bacterial aggression depends on the number and virulence of pathogenic bacteria. Bacterial aggression toward the periradicular tissues can give rise to an acute (symptomatic apical periodontitis or acute apical abscess) or a chronic (asymptomatic apical periodontitis or chronic apical abscess) inflammatory response.

In endodontic infection, bacteria are located in a strategic and privileged position within a root canal system containing necrotic tissue (Fig 4-8). Phagocytes have limited access to the bacteria because an active circulation no longer exists in the canal (the pulp is necrotic!). On the other hand, bacteria that leave the root canal for the periradicular tissues are immediately (and usually effectively) combated by host defense mechanisms. These mechanisms are represented initially by the innate immunity and further by the adaptive immunity, both of which are mobilized to the region in an attempt to contain the spread of the infection present in the untreated canal.108,111,122 Although the source of the infection is not effectively eliminated by the immune defenses, the host manages to establish an equilibrium, which is often characterized by chronic inflammation.
When the root canal is adequately treated, the clinician promotes an imbalance in favor of the host, and healing of the periradicular tissues is initiated. Therefore, apical periodontitis can be regarded as a defense barrier established by the immune system with the clear goal of confining the infection to the root canal and protecting the rest of the body from these bacterial invaders.

The host defenses against infection, in chronological sequence, are innate immunity and adaptive immunity (Table 4-1).108 Innate immune response is the first line of defense. After they invade the tissue, bacteria are immediately combated by resident tissue macrophages and the complement system is activated by the alternative and/or lectin pathways. The encounter of bacteria with these host defense mechanisms triggers the production and release of chemical mediators of inflammation, which induce vascular changes in the microcirculation and recruit more defense cells and molecules to the site of invasion. Bacteria not eliminated by the immediate innate mechanisms induce an acute inflammation, with all the vascular and cellular elements described in Chapter 3. These events occurring in the apical periodontal ligament give rise to the condition clinically characterized as symptomatic (or acute) apical periodontitis (Fig 49).

If the inflammatory response does not succeed in significantly reducing the intensity of aggression coming from the root canal, the process advances and exacerbates, giving rise to an inflammation characterized by purulent exudate (pus). In other words, an acute apical abscess forms (see Fig 4-9).

As the acute response cannot eradicate the source of infection in the necrotic root canal, the inflammatory process becomes chronic.
Asymptomatic (or chronic) apical periodontitis contains elements of the adaptive immune response as well as of the healing process (Fig
4-10). As the infection persists in the root canal system, the chronic process gives rise to bone resorption, leading to the establishment of an apical granuloma (Fig 4-11). Furthermore, the lesion becomes epithelialized as the epithelial cell rests of Malassez start to proliferate in the granuloma. Continuous epithelium proliferation over time results in the formation of a cyst cavity lined by epithelium – the apical cyst (see Fig 4-11). In some cases, the chronic inflammatory

### The acute periradicular response

The inflammatory response to tissue injury can be regarded as a double-edged sword. On the one hand, it provides protection against infection and/or prepares the injured area for repair of the tissue architecture. On the other hand, it can result in undesirable effects such as pain and intensified tissue damage.

Bacteria invading the periradicular tissues are initially faced by the complement system and macrophages, which are part of the preinflammatory innate response (Fig 4-12). Bacteria infecting the root canal are usually organized in a mixed community, the pathogenic ability of which relates to the accumulation and summation of virulence factors and antigens from the different component species.
When this bacterial “soup” comes into contact with the apical pulp and the periradicular tissues, antigens and virulence factors diffuse through the tissues to activate host defenses (Table 4-2). These factors include cellular constituents (such as lipopolysaccharide
[LPS], peptidoglycan, lipoteichoic acid, fimbriae, membrane proteins, and DNA), metabolic end-products (such as butyrate, propionate, and sulfur compounds), and N-formylmethionyl peptides. Bacterial cell

Once activated by bacterial structural components, the phagocytic and antigen-presenting capabilities of macrophages increase.
Activated macrophages also have pronounced biosynthetic activity, releasing a number of mediators, including the cytokines interleukin-1 (IL-1), IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), the chemokine IL8/CXCL8, lipid mediators (prostaglandins and leukotrienes), lysosomal enzymes, oxygen metabolites, nitric oxide, and plateletactivating factor (PAF).8,37,40,71,139 These mediators can elicit a series of proinflammatory effects (Fig 4-13).

Bacterial surface components such as peptidoglycan, LPS, and lipoteichoic acid can also activate the complement system via the alternative pathway, while bacteria that express mannose on their surfaces can activate the complement system via the lectin pathway.
Both pathways lead to important biologic effects of the complement system, represented by cytolysis of target cells and opsonization for further enhancement of phagocytosis. Polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) are the first leukocytes to migrate to the affected area. PMNs exposed to IL-8 and TNF-α are activated to mediate a respiratory burst that generates oxygen radicals, and to release their stored granule contents, thus contributing to the elimination of bacteria from the site. PMNs can kill invading bacteria, not only by phagocytosis and degranulation but also by forming neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which are large extracellular web-like chromatin structures that contain cytosolic and granule proteins.13 DNA in NETs originates from the neutrophil nucleus and mitochondria. NETs form via two pathways: one is a cell death pathway termed NETosis that involves several nuclear events and plasma membrane rupture; the other is independent of cell death and involves the release of nuclear chromatin and granule proteins.77

## Symptomatic apical periodontitis

If bacteria egressing from the apical foramen are highly aggressive, an acute inflammation will develop in the apical periodontal ligament, giving rise to the condition clinically diagnosed as symptomatic apical periodontitis. Its characteristic features – increased vascular permeability, edema formation, and increased tissue hydrostatic pressure – result in the compression of sensory nerve fibers and pain. Some chemical mediators (e.g. bradykinin) released in response to bacterial infection can also cause pain by exerting direct effects on the nerve fibers. Others, such as prostaglandins, can sensitize the nerve fibers to make them more susceptible to the effects of bradykinin. Bacterial factors can also directly sensitize nociceptors.27,32 However, since room for expansion of the periodontal ligament during inflammation is limited, the compression of nerve endings seems to play a more significant role in causing pain than the direct effects of host and bacterial mediators.106,130

### Histopathologic features

Histopathologic analysis of specimens from patients with symptomatic apical periodontitis reveals hyperemia and the presence of an intense inflammatory infiltrate in the apical periodontal ligament predominantly containing PMNs. Collagen fiber lacerations due to edema may also be observed (Fig 4-19).

### Signs and symptoms

Symptomatic apical periodontitis is usually associated with intense, pounding, spontaneous, and localized pain. The affected tooth is also tender to percussion. The patient may report that the tooth seems to have “grown” or “pushed out” of the alveolus. Actually, the tooth extrudes slightly to accommodate the edema fluid formed in the apical periodontal ligament. Chewing usually provokes or exacerbates the pain.

### Pulp tests

As symptomatic apical periodontitis is usually associated with pulp necrosis, pulp tests are usually negative, but may be positive in rare cases where the condition arises in teeth with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis.

## Acute apical abscess

If the acute inflammatory response associated with symptomatic apical periodontitis does not succeed in coping with infection and reducing the intensity of bacterial aggression, a more pronounced response characterized by purulent inflammation will ensue. Along with bacterial proteolytic enzymes, PMNs release lysosomal enzymes and oxygen radicals that promote soft tissue liquefaction and pus formation, giving rise to an acute apical abscess. Although this process is usually effective in reducing the infection load, the price (loss of integrity of tissue architecture) may be too high. In some cases, the host defenses lose control, allowing the infectiousinflammatory process to spread to anatomical spaces in the head and

### Clinical examination

Depending on the stage of the abscess, fluctuating or non-fluctuating intraoral or extraoral swelling may be observed (Fig 4-23). Swelling is usually localized but may become diffuse and spread to other head and neck sites. In the abscess initial stages, no swelling is present, and the clinical features resemble those of symptomatic apical periodontitis except for the fact that pus is detected in the canal after opening the tooth. In an intermediate stage, the abscess evolves to an indurated but not fluctuant swelling (cellulitis). In the advanced stage, pus perforates the periosteum and drains to the submucosal or subcutaneous soft tissue, generating a point of fluctuation. In some cases, the tooth can be mobile and slightly elevated in the socket.
The location of swelling is determined by the relation of the root apex to the muscle attachments.

## The chronic periradicular response

The bacterial cell surface is a very complex mosaic of different molecules, the vast majority of which are considered foreign to the host. Some of these molecules can be released to join other secreted bacterial products, which in most instances are also considered foreign. Thus, intraradicular mixed biofilms represent an exuberant source of antigens that accumulate in the biofilm mass to form an antigenic “soup” in constant contact with the periradicular tissues.
Asymptomatic apical periodontitis develops as an immune response to this chronic antigenic stimulation from the infected root canal.

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 205 -->

For the development of the adaptive immune response, the host must be sensitized to the bacterial antigens. Sensitization occurs at the level of draining regional lymph nodes. In these secondary lymphoid organs, recirculating naive B and T cells have more chances to encounter the antigen to which they are specific and then undergo all the processes they require to become activated.

During the acute inflammatory response, a large number of antigen fragments are generated as a result of bacterial killing. These antigens accumulate in the inflammatory exudate, are captured by dendritic cells, and drain through lymphatics to the local lymph nodes (Fig 4-25). The lymph from the pulp and periodontal tissues of most teeth drains to the submandibular lymph nodes. The submental lymph nodes drain the lymph from the mandibular incisors, while the deep cervical lymph nodes drain the molar region.

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 207 -->

Once entrapped in the specialized regions of a lymph node, the probability of an antigenic molecule being presented to circulating naive lymphocytes or resident memory cells specific to the antigen is far greater than in the periradicular tissues. This is because lymphocytes are constantly circulating through the lymph nodes.
Thus, for the initiation of the adaptive immune response against endodontic pathogens, antigens must be taken to regional lymph nodes where recognition and lymphocyte activation can occur.1

Actually, a study revealed that immune cells rarely proliferate in the periradicular area.114 Proliferation occurs at a distance, in lymph nodes, after antigen recognition; effector cells subsequently enter the circulation and migrate to the periradicular region.114 However, some final differentiation to effector cells may occur at the peripheral inflamed tissues, as must be the case for plasma cells present in apical periodontitis lesions.

When a lymphocyte specific for a given antigen contacts the antigen in the lymph node, the clone is expanded by intense proliferation. One activated lymphocyte can give rise to about 1,000 daughter cells with the same antigenic specificity. These cells then differentiate in effector cells. B cells give rise to plasma cells, which secrete antibodies, while T cells differentiate into effector cells that activate other immune cells (CD4+ T lymphocytes) or destroy target cells infected by the virus (CD8+ cytotoxic lymphocytes). After exposure to the antigen, lymphocytes can take 4 to 5 days to proliferate and differentiate.1,71 For this reason, the adaptive immune response is referred to as a late response. Some cells do not undergo the final differentiation stage and give rise to memory cells.

After activation, effector cells express specialized adhesion molecules on their surface that affect the pattern of recirculation.
After leaving the lymph node, effector cells are attracted by chemokines to the injury site, which is the source of the antigen that activated them (see Fig 4-25). There, the antigen is present in high concentrations and the microcirculation is already altered by the previous inflammatory response. Effector immune cells then become established in the periradicular region with the clear objective of

## Asymptomatic apical periodontitis

A chronic inflammatory response can start from the onset or be due to a reduction of injury intensity promoted by a previous acute episode that nevertheless did not manage to eliminate the source of infection. Monocytes, T cells, B cells, and plasma cells are attracted to the area and accumulate in the periradicular tissues adjacent to the apical foramen. From there, bacterial antigens emerge and diffuse through the periradicular tissues. A chronic inflammatory response can develop and slowly expand without eliciting significant signs and symptoms. Initially, asymptomatic (chronic) apical periodontitis is established and is characterized radiographically by a widening of the
PLS as a result of initial bone resorption.

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

In response to the root canal infection, chemical mediators are released in the periradicular tissues by macrophages, T cells, and other cells and may reach concentrations needed to stimulate osteoblasts/stromal cells to express RANKL, which is critical to osteoclast activation (Fig 4-28).115,116 Bone is resorbed and replaced by an inflamed granulation (or granulomatous) tissue basically composed of immunocompetent cells such as lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages, and neutrophils as well as components of the repair process, including fibroblasts and newly formed nerves and blood vessels. The bone resorption process creates enough space to accommodate a large number of defense cells in the area adjacent to the apical foramen in an attempt to wall off the root canal infection and impede it from disseminating to the bone. A situation then arises in which a balance is achieved between aggression and defense. In the periphery of this granulomatous tissue, collagen fibers are deposited to encapsulate the lesion. As the disease progresses, bone resorption continues until a frank radiolucent lesion surrounding the root apex becomes evident (Fig 4-29). This condition is histopathologically known as apical granuloma. The lesion becomes radiographically detectable as soon as enough bone is destroyed in the process. ### Root resorption

Root resorption, mostly involving cementum but also affecting dentin, may be observed in some teeth associated with asymptomatic apical periodontitis (Fig 4-32). However, root resorption is usually discrete, even when associated with large bone lesions.107 Depending on the amount of calcified tissue that is lost, root resorption can be detected in periapical radiographs. However, in many cases, the condition can only be disclosed in histologic sections. Laux et al54 histologically examined 114 teeth and found that only 19% of them were diagnosed as having apical resorption on the basis of radiographs, whereas histologically 81% of the teeth exhibited inflammatory apical root resorption. Vier and Figueiredo133 examined 74 root apices of teeth with apical periodontitis lesions using light microscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and reported that 75% of the roots showed internal apical resorption.

Cementum is usually less affected by resorption than bone. The probable reasons include:11,66

presence of precementum: On the periodontal surface of the alveolar bone, resorption occurs at sites where the bone surface is not covered by osteoid. Mineralized cementum is covered by precementum, which consists of a nonmineralized matrix that is

presence of epithelial cell rests of Malassez in the periodontal ligament, which have been ascribed a protective function against tooth resorption (Fig 4-33);
presence of the cementoblast layer, which lines the root surface and can also be protective. Cementoblasts do not respond to resorptive stimuli as bone cells do.

### Epithelial proliferation and cyst formation

In response to the inflammatory reaction in the periodontal ligament, epithelial cells that are remnants of the Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath are induced to proliferate. These cells are known as cell rests of Malassez. In physiologic conditions, they are quiescent and do not exhibit mitotic activity. However, during a chronic inflammatory response, several bacterial and host factors can activate epithelial proliferation.

Epidermal growth factor (EGF) is a peptide that can be produced by activated macrophages in an inflamed area. EGF is a potent growth factor to epithelial cells. Cell rests of Malassez have been shown to express receptors for this growth factor.58 Keratinocyte growth factor (KGF), a cytokine, is also mytogenic to epithelial cells and can be produced by fibroblasts stimulated by IL-1 and TNF released during inflammation. Gao et al35 reported that KGF synthesis was pronounced in areas near epithelial proliferation in granulomas and adjacent to the epithelial lining of cyst cavities. Other mediators produced by cells occurring in apical granulomas (lymphocytes, fibroblasts, and mainly macrophages) can also stimulate epithelial proliferation, including TNF, IL-1, IL-6, PGE2, and

insulin-like growth factor.56 Bacterial components such as LPS can also induce epithelial cell growth.65

Epithelial proliferation in granulomas gives rise to strands or islands of organized epithelium, a condition histologically known as epithelialized granuloma (Fig 4-34). It is believed that epithelial proliferation is another host defense mechanism to wall off the source of infection communicating through the apical foramen.

Whichever the process that leads to the development of the cyst cavity, apical cyst formation can be chronologically divided into three phases:102

In the first phase, the “dormant” cell rests of Malassez start to proliferate, stimulated by chemical inflammatory mediators produced and released in the lesion in response to bacterial infection.

Expansion of the cystic lesion may be related to an increase in the osmotic pressure generated by the decomposition of inflammatory cells accumulated in the cyst lumen. This would enormously increase the number of macromolecules within the cyst lumen. In favor of this theory is the fact that decompression (marsupialization) procedures usually succeed in reducing the lesion size. On the other hand, the observation that pocket cysts opened to the canal lumen also show a tendency to increase in volume might question the possibility that the increase in osmotic pressure is responsible for the lesion growth. Production of proresorptive inflammatory mediators in the periradicular tissues is possibly the main cause of growth of apical periodontitis lesions, including granulomas; consequently, it is also expected to be the main factor involved with cyst expansion.

### Histopathologic features

A chronic inflammatory infiltrate is observed in the periodontal ligament adjacent to the apical foramen and ramifications. The amount of bone resorption depends on the stage of the disease:
incipient asymptomatic apical periodontitis shows small areas of bone destruction, while granulomas and cysts exhibit larger areas.
Resorbed bone is replaced by inflamed granulation (granulomatous) tissue, which is composed of defense cells and elements involved in tissue repair.

Granuloma. Granulomatous tissue in the periphery of a granuloma is separated from the bone by a fibrous capsule basically composed of collagen. Inflammatory cells correspond to about 50% of the lesion components. Macrophages, lymphocytes, and plasma cells dominate, and PMNs can usually be seen in areas adjacent to the portals of exit of bacteria (apical and lateral foramina). Mast cells can also be found. If strands of epithelial proliferation are observed without cavity formation, the lesion is called epithelialized granuloma.

Cyst. Histologically, apical cysts are very similar to granulomas except for the presence of an epithelium-lined pathologic cavity containing a fluid or semi-solid material mostly composed of degenerated epithelial cells (Fig 4-35). The lining is usually stratified squamous epithelium of variable width. In rare instances, apical cysts lined with ciliated columnar epithelium have also been found.87

Cholesterol clefts may be observed (Fig 4-36). As with granulomas, the most peripheral zone of the cyst is composed of a collagen capsule separating the lesion from the healthy bone (Fig 4-37).

Another variation of apical periodontitis is the chronic apical abscess or suppurative chronic apical periodontitis. This condition results from the gradual egress of irritants from the canal to the periradicular

tissues, with consequent formation of pus within the granuloma.
Alternatively, it may be a consequence of chronification of an acute apical abscess.

### Histopathologic conditions

The chronic apical abscess is a granulomatous lesion containing areas of liquefying necrosis with disintegrating PMNs surrounded by macrophages and PMNs. A sinus tract lined by epithelium or inflamed connective tissue communicates these areas of liquefying necrosis to the periphery.

In a histobacteriologic study, Ricucci et al85 described the pattern of intraradicular and extraradicular infections in untreated and treated teeth with sinus tracts and chronic apical abscesses. Bacteria were found in the main apical root canal of all specimens and within ramifications of most specimens. Extraradicular bacteria were detected in 83% of the cases, in most of them organized in biofilms attached to the outer root surface (71%). In many cases, the biofilm showed mineralized areas (calculus). They concluded that teeth with chronic apical abscesses had a very complex infection in the apical part of the root canal system and the periradicular lesion, with predominance of bacterial biofilm structures.

### Signs and symptoms

A chronic apical abscess is usually asymptomatic. The condition is related to intermittent or continuous drainage of exudate through a sinus tract with a usually intraoral but occasionally extraoral exit (Fig
4-39).

### Clinical examination

The affected tooth frequently has an extensive caries lesion or large coronal restoration. A sinus tract, which may or may not be actively draining, is located at the level of the alveolar mucosa (Fig 4-40).
Some cases present with a sinus tract on the facial skin. The tract can be traced by inserting a gutta-percha point in it and then taking a radiograph (see Fig 4-40; Figs 4-41 and 4-42). This procedure, commonly referred to as fistulography, is of great value as it allows one to determine the origin of the sinus tract, which is not always adjacent to the involved tooth. Occasionally, the exudate can also drain through the gingival sulcus of the involved tooth, which may be misdiagnosed as marginal periodontitis. However, a pocket is usually absent.

### Pulp tests

Pulp tests are negative because the pulp is necrotic.

### Periradicular tests

Percussion and palpation. Both are also usually negative, but mild sensitivity may be present in some cases.
