## Intracanal medication

Intracanal medication comprises the placement in the root canal of a medicament, which is usually left in place between appointments to exert the desired therapeutic effects. The use of an intracanal medicament is indicated in certain routine or occasional situations.
Routine intracanal medication is an important adjunct to chemomechanical preparation in the treatment of infected root canals of teeth with primary apical periodontitis and in the retreatment of teeth with posttreatment apical periodontitis. It serves to supplement the antibacterial effects of chemomechanical procedures and predictably disinfect the root canal system. As discussed in Chapter
11, infected root canals are ideally treated in two visits: the first is used for root canal instrumentation and medication, and the second (usually scheduled 1 to 2 weeks later) for root canal obturation.
Indications for occasional intracanal medication are discussed next, and those for routine intracanal medication are discussed later in this chapter.

### Indications for occasional intracanal medication

Occasional intracanal medication is indicated:

For treatment of vital teeth in cases where endodontic treatment cannot be completed in a single visit. In this situation, a calcium hydroxide paste is placed in the canal as a temporary filling that functions as a physicochemical barrier to prevent or delay bacterial contamination of the canal between appointments.220

Any contamination that might occur between appointments is usually due to leakage or fracture of either the temporary restorative material or the tooth crown.
For treatment of cases with persistent symptoms or exudation.
In these cases, the intracanal medication acts indirectly on inflammation by helping to eliminate its primary cause, which is residual bacteria in the apical canal.160 Topical anti-inflammatory drugs that act directly on inflammation (e.g. corticosteroids) are generally not recommended because they focus on the effect

### Indications for routine intracanal medication

Routine intracanal medication is indicated for treatment or retreatment of infected teeth with apical periodontitis. The primary objective of interappointment medication is to eliminate residual biofilms and microorganisms that survived the chemomechanical preparation. The secondary purpose is to establish a physicochemical barrier to prevent reinfection of the canal by bacteria from saliva between appointments.

Irrigants such as NaOCl and chlorhexidine have excellent antimicrobial activities and rapid effects against a large spectrum of species commonly found in endodontic infections. However, these effects are mostly observed when there is optimal contact area with the microbial cells. During irrigation, the antimicrobial solution reaches the apical part of the canal by convection, whereby the active particles are transported quickly by fluid motion. However, in areas more distant from the main canal, the irrigant needs to diffuse from the main canal to reach and kill bacteria. Diffusion is much slower

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 770 -->

than convection and thus requires time to be effective. In other words, the antimicrobial irrigant should have enough time to diffuse in order to reach and kill bacteria located in dentinal tubules, recesses, ramifications, isthmuses, etc.

In the clinical setting, however, the short time that irrigants remain in the canal during preparation is a major limitation. The irrigant remains in the canal for about 10 to 30 minutes (the time it usually takes to prepare most canals), whereas an intracanal medication is generally left in the canal for 7 to 14 days. This substantial time difference is the main reason why intracanal medication can successfully enhance disinfection. When used for equal treatment periods, NaOCl has higher antimicrobial activity than calcium hydroxide, but this comparison is not practically meaningful. A more realistic comparison would be NaOCl (30 minutes) versus NaOCl (30 minutes) plus calcium hydroxide (7 days). As the intracanal medication remains in the canal longer than the irrigation solution, it has more time to diffuse and then to reach and eliminate bacteria in areas inaccessible to chemomechanical procedures.

By filling the entire extent of the prepared canal, intracanal calcium hydroxide paste acts as a temporary root canal obturation in the

<!-- PDF page 772 -->

sense that it physically fills the space and prevents seepage of exudate from periradicular tissues or leakage of saliva from a leaky temporary restoration into the canal between sessions. Exudates and saliva can supply substrate to residual bacteria, and saliva can be a source of reinfection. The antibacterial effects of the intracanal medicament also help to prevent reinfection. It has been reported that if the canal is left empty after chemomechanical preparation of teeth with apical periodontitis, bacterial counts can reach levels similar to preoperative levels during the interval between visits.32,33,233

## Calcium hydroxide

Since its introduction by the German dentist Bernhard Hermann in
1920,85 calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2, has been widely used in endodontics for a myriad of indications.55,83 Calcium hydroxide is a strong alkaline substance with a pH of approximately 12.5. In the presence of water, it dissociates into hydroxyl and calcium ions. Most of the biologic effects of calcium hydroxide are related to its alkaline pH (hydroxyl ions).205

Most bacteria are unable to survive in a highly alkaline environment such as that created by calcium hydroxide.15 Since the pH of calcium hydroxide is about 12.5, several bacterial species commonly found in infected root canals are eliminated after a short period of direct exposure to this substance.31

The antimicrobial activity of calcium hydroxide depends on the release of hydroxyl ions in an aqueous environment. Hydroxyl ions are highly oxidant free radicals with extreme reactivity with several biomolecules. As this reactivity is high yet indiscriminate, the free radicals rarely diffuse away from the site of generation. Lethal effects of hydroxyl ions on bacterial cells are probably due to the following mechanisms:205

Damage to the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane. Hydroxyl ions from calcium hydroxide induce lipid peroxidation, resulting in the destruction of phospholipids, which are structural components of the cytoplasmic membrane. Hydroxyl ions remove hydrogen atoms from unsaturated fatty acids, generating a free lipid radical. This free lipid radical reacts with oxygen to form a lipid peroxide radical, which in turn removes another hydrogen atom from a second fatty acid, generating another lipid peroxide. Thus, the peroxides themselves act as free radicals, initiating an autocatalytic chain reaction, resulting in the further loss of unsaturated fatty acids and extensive membrane damage.62,100

Protein denaturation. Alkaline substances such as calcium hydroxide can induce the breakdown of ionic bonds that maintain the tertiary structure of proteins. The affected protein maintains its covalent structure, but the polypeptide chain is randomly unraveled in a variable and irregular spatial conformation. These changes frequently result in loss of biologic activity. In addition, if the protein is an enzyme, the cellular metabolism can be disrupted. Any structural proteins present in the bacterial cell membranes may also be damaged by the hydroxyl ions.
DNA damage. Hydroxyl ions react with bacterial DNA, resulting in the splitting of the DNA strands.62,89 Consequently, they inhibit

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 775 -->

DNA replication and result in disarranged cellular activity. Free radicals may also induce lethal mutations.

In vitro studies have demonstrated that calcium hydroxide has lethal effects on bacterial cells.31,68,200,239 Optimal effects were observed when there was direct contact between calcium hydroxide and test bacteria in solution. Under these conditions, the concentration of hydroxyl ions is very high and incompatible with bacterial survival.
Clinically, such direct contact is not always possible. Clinical findings regarding the effectiveness of calcium hydroxide in significantly supplementing the antibacterial action of the chemomechanical procedures by increasing the number of culture-negative canals are somewhat inconsistent.148,190,203,232,259,272 This indicates that this substance has its own limitations.

In addition to the difficulties in establishing optimal contact between the intracanal medicament and intracanal bacteria, other factors may contribute to the limitations of calcium hydroxide in promoting predictable root canal disinfection. These are described in the following paragraphs.

The ability of calcium hydroxide to kill bacteria depends on the availability of hydroxyl ions in solution, which is highest at the site where the paste is applied (the main root canal). Calcium hydroxide exerts antibacterial effects in the root canal as long as a very high pH is sustained. If calcium hydroxide must diffuse to tissues but the hydroxyl concentration is decreased due to the action of buffering systems (bicarbonate and phosphate), acids, proteins, and carbon dioxide, its antibacterial effectiveness may be reduced or impeded.205

An intracanal medicament must be able to dissolve and diffuse into the root canal system in order to exert successful antimicrobial action. A saturated aqueous suspension of calcium hydroxide has a high pH and, thus, a great cytotoxic potential. Nevertheless, this highly alkaline substance owes its biocompatibility to its low water solubility and diffusibility.197 Due to these properties, the cytotoxicity of calcium hydroxide is limited to the tissue in its direct vicinity. On the

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 776 -->

other hand, the same low solubility and diffusibility make it difficult for this substance to induce the rapid and significant increase in pH needed to eliminate bacteria present in biofilms, dentinal tubules, tissue remnants, and anatomical variations. Likewise, the serum and dentin serve as buffers that control pH changes, and consequently reduce the antimicrobial effectiveness of calcium hydroxide.79,133,158

Due to these factors, calcium hydroxide is a slow-working antimicrobial agent that requires prolonged exposure to allow for the saturation of dentin and tissue remnants. Long-term use of calcium hydroxide is necessary to maximize disinfection of the root canal system.

Infected dentinal tubules may then serve as reservoirs of bacteria, with the potential to cause persistent infection and posttreatment apical periodontitis.113,161,164 Intratubular infection is also the main cause of external inflammatory root resorption.20 Therefore, the intracanal medication should be able to reach and eliminate bacteria located deep within tubules. The pH tolerance ranges of different microorganisms vary, but most human pathogens grow well within a range of 5 to 9 pH.137

Some strains of Escherichia coli, Proteus vulgaris, Enterobacter aerogenes, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa can survive at a pH of 8 or 9.15 These bacterial species have occasionally been isolated from infected root canals and were usually associated with secondary infections.227 Certain bacteria such as some enterococci can tolerate very high pH levels, e.g. pH 9 to 11. Fungi generally also exhibit a wide pH range and can grow within a range of pH 5 to 9.15

Some microbial species have demonstrated resistance to calcium hydroxide. Enterococcus faecalis and some Candida species can be highly resistant to the alkaline effects of calcium hydroxide.31,52,78,199,260 The ability of E. faecalis to resist high pH values seems to be related to a proton pump that drives protons into the cell to acidify the cytoplasm.52 E. faecalis and Candida species have been found in root canal-treated teeth with posttreatment disease.155,167,170,216,226,242

### Inactivation of bacterial virulence factors

Structural components of bacterial cells are important virulence factors that induce indirect damage by stimulating an inflammatory response (see Chaps 2 and 3). Lipopolysaccharides (also called endotoxins) and lipoteichoic acid (LTA), which are components of the cell wall of Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, respectively, are prime examples.

The lipid A domain of the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) molecule has been regarded as the main factor responsible for the biologic effects of this molecule.165,166 In vitro studies have demonstrated that calcium hydroxide can inactivate LPS by inducing the alkaline hydrolysis of ester bonds, resulting in the release of free hydroxy fatty acids with no or reduced toxic and proinflammatory effects.28,91,129,180,181,195,243 Although this inactivating effect has been observed in vitro under conditions of optimal contact between LPS

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 779 -->

and calcium hydroxide, there is no clinical study consistently showing that this occurs under the real conditions normally existing in root canal systems. As discussed above, it is highly unlikely that enough calcium hydroxide can reach LPS molecules present in areas distant from the main canal to inactivate these molecules. A clinical study revealed that intracanal LPS levels were reduced but still relatively high after chemomechanical preparation, and that they remained virtually unchanged after intracanal medication with calcium hydroxide, chlorhexidine, or a combination of both.257

LTA is a glycerol phosphate polymer linked to fatty acids. It resembles LPS in certain respects and has proinflammatory properties. Consequently, LTA may be regarded as a Gram-positive counterpart of LPS. LTA contributes to the pathogenicity of E.
faecalis19 and may participate in the pathogenesis of apical periodontitis.214 Studies have demonstrated that calcium hydroxide can detoxify LTA and attenuate its proinflammatory ability.17,18 These effects are supposedly related to the deacylation of LTA under high alkaline conditions. Deacylated LTA does not stimulate Toll-like receptor 2, the host molecule responsible for recognition of and response to LTA and the consequent release of proinflammatory cytokines.18

It remains to be determined whether these inactivating effects of calcium hydroxide on LPS and LTA also occur in vivo, and, if so, what the actual relevance might be for the long-term treatment outcome. If calcium hydroxide can inactivate these substances, the clinical significance of this inactivating action remains to be shown. Moreover, there is no clear indication that residual LPS or LTA can induce or maintain periradicular inflammation beyond a certain point in time in the absence of living bacteria. Moreover, many other virulence factors can also play an important role in the pathogenesis of apical periodontitis.214 In fact, the “soup” of bacterial virulence factors concentrated in biofilms and emanating from them is more likely to be involved in disease causation.215

### Antimicrobial protocol with the CHPG paste

CHPG paste is part of a comprehensive antimicrobial treatment protocol that includes other important strategies, such as large apical preparation35,174,183,204 and irrigation with NaOCl (Table 14-1).33,217

This protocol, which has been taught for about 25 years in the present authors’ dental school, has promoted excellent outcome results, even in the hands of inexperienced undergraduate students.
This is attested by clinical studies of the incidence of postoperative pain,218 antimicrobial effectiveness,211 and long-term outcome of the initial treatment of teeth with apical periodontitis.229

Incidence of postoperative pain. The present authors found that, when the aforementioned protocol was used by undergraduate students for initial treatment of teeth with primarily infected root canals, mild pain occurred in 10% of cases, moderate pain in 3%, and severe pain (flare-ups) in only 2% (Fig 14-13).218 Eighty-five percent of the treated teeth were asymptomatic after application of this treatment protocol.

### Calcium hydroxide in chlorhexidine

Chlorhexidine is highly effective against oral bacteria and fungi.45,132,201,237,256 Its antimicrobial activity is influenced by pH (optimal range: pH 5.5 to 7) and is greatly reduced or abolished in the presence of organic matter.119 The effects of combining calcium hydroxide with chlorhexidine (CHCx paste) have been extensively studied in vitro. Several studies have demonstrated that the addition of chlorhexidine significantly increases the antimicrobial effects of calcium hydroxide in paste.53,72,73,157,230

One clinical study274 demonstrated that a mixture of 2% chlorhexidine and calcium hydroxide was at least as effective as calcium hydroxide in an inert vehicle in the disinfection of root canaltreated teeth with apical periodontitis. The incidence of culturepositive canals after 7 to 10 days of treatment with CHCx paste was
35%.274 Another clinical study by Siqueira et al,212 who evaluated the antibacterial effectiveness of a chlorhexidine-based protocol for treatment of primary infections, found positive cultures in 54% of cases after chemomechanical preparation using 0.12% chlorhexidine as the irrigant compared with only 8% after 7-day intracanal medication with calcium hydroxide paste in 0.12% chlorhexidine (Fig
14-16). A statistically significant reduction in both bacterial counts and the number of bacteria-positive cases was observed after the intracanal medication with CHCx.

A clinical study using molecular microbiology evaluation of samples by Paiva et al140 used an antimicrobial protocol consisting of rotary instrumentation, 2.5% NaOCl irrigation, final rinse with 2% chlorhexidine, and 1-week intracanal medication with calcium hydroxide in 2% chlorhexidine. This antimicrobial treatment caused a substantial decrease in bacterial diversity and significantly reduced

### Intentional apical extrusion of calcium hydroxide

Some clinicians believe that intentional apical extrusion of calcium hydroxide in cases of apical periodontitis may be of some benefit.
The supposition is that calcium hydroxide may exert its known antimicrobial activity and an as-yet-unproven but unlikely antiinflammatory effect in the periradicular tissues. This assumption is not substantiated by scientific evidence, and there is actually no reason why apically extruded calcium hydroxide should have any effect on the outcome of endodontic treatment. Although it has been suggested that calcium hydroxide might have direct anti-inflammatory effects,83

this has never been consistently proven. Actually, calcium hydroxide pastes may reduce inflammation indirectly by acting on the cause, i.e.
bacteria present in the canal. Nevertheless, this cannot be regarded as an anti-inflammatory property, as antibiotics and disinfectants also kill bacteria and are not classified as anti-inflammatory agents.

It is highly unlikely that extruded calcium hydroxide will eliminate microorganisms located outside the root canal, which are either adhered to the apical root surface or invading the apical periodontitis lesion. Buffering substances and systems are expected to function in the periradicular tissues, represented by the bicarbonate system, phosphate system, proteins, and carbon dioxide, which impede a significant increase in pH. Moreover, apically extruded calcium hydroxide paste is likely to be quickly diluted and inactivated in tissue fluids and exudate. In order to achieve the pH levels needed to exceed the tissue buffer capacity and thereby eliminate extraradicular bacteria, a very large amount of paste would have to be extruded.
However, this would be highly toxic to the host tissues due to the

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 791 -->

absence of selective toxicity against microorganisms. Drastic consequences might arise. Calcium hydroxide combined with other antimicrobial substances cannot be expected to be effective either, as it would be inactivated by tissue proteins and other tissue components. Furthermore, given the low prevalence of extraradicular infections, the routine use of this approach is highly questionable.

Although extrusion of a small amount of calcium hydroxide does not offer any plausible benefit, it usually does not represent a clinical problem either (Fig 14-18).43 In fact, the extrusion of paste suggests that the entire extent of the root canal is properly filled with the paste.
This is a goal to be reached when using calcium hydroxide paste as an intracanal medication, and the unique result to be expected from this substance is that it will exert its effects within the confines of the root canal system. However, in certain cases, gross apical extrusion of calcium hydroxide paste may have disastrous consequences.41,112

## Clinical recommendations

### Noninfected root canals

In vital cases, the infection (if present) is usually restricted to the surface of the exposed pulp tissue, and the radicular pulp may be inflamed but free of infection. Once this superficial infection is eradicated by access preparation, excision of the coronal pulp, and profuse irrigation of the pulp chamber with NaOCl, the subsequent treatment steps are performed in an aseptic environment. After preparation, the root canal should be obturated immediately if time and clinical conditions allow. If this is not possible, an intracanal medication should be placed and another appointment scheduled for the filling procedures. The medication mainly serves to prevent contamination of the prepared canal between appointments and prevent postoperative pain in cases that are incompletely instrumented. Therefore, if chemomechanical preparation was completed but obturation had to be postponed, the root canal should

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 792 -->

be filled with a calcium hydroxide paste in glycerin to fill the space and act as a physicochemical barrier against bacteria. In cases of incomplete instrumentation, a topical corticosteroid solution may be placed in the canal to prevent postoperative pain.

### Infected root canals

As mentioned before, teeth with infected root canals and primary/posttreatment apical periodontitis should ideally be treated/retreated in two visits. At the first appointment, the root canal is completely prepared, the smear layer is removed, and a CHPG or
CHCx paste is placed in the entire extent of the prepared canal. At the second appointment, the medication is removed and the canal

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 794 -->

obturated. If preparation was incomplete, there will not be sufficient room for the paste to be applied, and tissue remnants can significantly dampen its effects. In these cases, final irrigation is performed with NaOCl solution, excess solution is removed by aspiration, and the root canal is coronally sealed. No significant antibacterial effects of NaOCl are expected under these conditions, but this may be better than leaving the canal empty. At the second appointment, root canal preparation should be completed and an intracanal CHPG or CHCx dressing placed.

Although syringes containing calcium hydroxide pastes in inert vehicles are available on the market for direct application to canals, there is no such availability of CHPG or CHCx pastes. Therefore,
Lentulo spirals are the instruments preferably used to place these pastes in prepared canals (Fig 14-19). After smear layer removal, excess irrigant is aspirated from the canal, which is further dried using one or two paper points. There is no need to completely dry the canal because some residual moisture is desirable to improve calcium hydroxide diffusibility. When CHCx paste is used, the canal should be rinsed with chlorhexidine solution after smear layer removal, and excess solution should be removed by aspiration before paste placement.

The largest Lentulo spiral that reaches 2 to 3 mm short of the working length without binding to the canal walls is used to apply the calcium hydroxide paste. The paste should be mixed on the surface of a sterile glass slab. CHPG is prepared using equal volumes of
CPMC and glycerin (one drop of each is enough for most canals).
The two substances are mixed first, and then calcium hydroxide powder is gradually added until a consistency similar to that of most root canal sealers or zinc phosphate cement is attained. CHCx paste is prepared by adding calcium hydroxide to one or two drops of
0.12% to 2% chlorhexidine solution until a creamy consistency, similar to toothpaste, is achieved.

The paste is placed in the canals using the selected Lentulo spiral filler coupled with a low-speed handpiece (Fig 14-20). Two or three applications may be necessary for proper root canal filling. The paste

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 795 -->

is then packed with a cotton pellet at the level of the canal entrance, and a radiograph is taken to ensure proper placement of the paste in the canal. The access cavities must then be cleaned of calcium hydroxide paste residue and restored with temporary cement to at least a 3- to 5-mm thickness. The second appointment is scheduled for at least 1 week later. During this appointment, the calcium hydroxide paste is removed by using the last instrument used in apical preparation or one size larger, under copious irrigation with NaOCl (if the CHPG paste was used) or 2% chlorhexidine (if the CHCx paste was used). The smear layer is once again removed before filling. This second step of instrumentation for calcium hydroxide removal has been regarded as extremely important to improve disinfection in a two-visit treatment protocol.36

## Optimizing single-visit disinfection

The question as to whether the endodontic treatment of teeth with apical periodontitis should be concluded in one or two visits is still one of the greatest current controversies in endodontics.57,111,184,240,249

The establishment of treatment protocols that can predictably disinfect the root canal in one visit has the potential to help resolve this discussion. The idea of speeding up root canal disinfection while maintaining the same efficacy observed in two-visit treatment sounds interesting and has been pursued. The question then is how quickly one can predictably disinfect root canals.

Optimized single-visit disinfection (OSD) is an intra-appointment approach intended to supplement the disinfecting effects of chemomechanical preparation by applying intracanal medication briefly (for a few minutes) or by performing an additional procedure at the same visit. OSD has the potential to eliminate the need for interappointment medication by expediting disinfection.

Early attempts at OSD consisted of short-term intracanal medication in which 5.25% NaOCl was applied for 30 minutes (changed every 5 minutes)120 or calcium hydroxide was applied for 10

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 796 -->

a) b) b)

minutes232 immediately following preparation. However, both of these approaches failed to significantly enhance the elimination of intracanal bacterial populations. Other strategies have been proposed, and while some have produced promising ex vivo results, others have shown only incipient clinical results. It is worth pointing out that most of these strategies are still under investigation, and there is only limited information from clinical trials about their effectiveness.

The main strategies currently proposed for OSD include:

Final rinse with chlorhexidine.
Mechanic or ultrasonic activation of NaOCl or other irrigants.
Laser and PDT.

All of these OSD approaches are recommended for use following chemomechanical procedures and smear layer removal.

### Final rinse with chlorhexidine

Studies have demonstrated no significant difference in the antibacterial effectiveness of NaOCl and chlorhexidine when used as irrigants during chemomechanical preparation.49,90,169,228,272 NaOCl remains the first-line endodontic irrigant of choice because of its soft tissue dissolving ability – a property not shared by chlorhexidine – which may enhance root canal cleaning.23,126

### Mechanical activation of NaOCl

### a) XP-endo Finisher instrument

The XP-endo Finisher instrument was introduced with the purpose of enhancing root canal cleaning and disinfection after chemomechanical preparation. This is a size 25, non-tapered instrument made of NiTi
MaxWire alloy (Martensite-Austenite ElectropolishFleX), which is straight in a martensite phase at room temperature but undergoes a change to the austenite phase at body temperature, developing a spoon shape. When rotated and moved up and down in the root canal, the new shape of the instrument allows it to expand and contract, not only to increase the touching of the canal walls but also to shake the irrigant solution.44 XP-endo Finisher was shown to render the mesial root canal system with significantly lower levels of hard tissue debris compared with conventional irrigation and a modified self-adjusting file (SAF) system protocol.107 Studies evaluating the removal of the filling material during retreatment revealed that XP-endo Finisher used after preparation significantly

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 803 -->

enhanced root canal cleaning.10,193 With regard to disinfection, ex vivo studies reported good results for XP-endo Finisher.9,16 However, the supplementary use of this instrument was not effective in disinfecting the isthmuses of the mesial root of mandibular molars.9 In a randomized clinical study using molecular microbiology analysis,
Amaral et al11 observed a marked additional bacterial reduction when the XP-endo Finisher instrument was used after instrumentation.

### b) EndoActivator

Some authors recommend filling the canal with NaOCl after preparation and then sonically activating this substance.177,178 Sonic frequencies range from 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Several sonic endodontic devices operate at 1 to 6 kHz, but some can operate at far lower frequencies. For example, the EndoActivator operates at 167 Hz or
10,000 cpm.

The EndoActivator system consists of a sonic handpiece (167 Hz or 10,000 cpm) and plastic tips of variable sizes. It is designed to energize intracanal substances such as NaOCl so as to generate a hydrodynamic phenomenon that could theoretically maximize the biologic effects of the substance.176 However, an in vitro study failed to find any significant advantage of using this system to increase the antibacterial effects of NaOCl.27 Moreover, it has been demonstrated that final ultrasonic activation of irrigants (see below) is more effective than sonic activation in cleaning root canals.92,179

### Ultrasonic activation of NaOCl

Ultrasound is a sound energy with a frequency greater than 20 kHz, which is beyond the limit of human hearing. Although ultrasound has been widely used and tested for root canal instrumentation and
“passive” irrigation, few consistent clinical studies on its antibacterial efficacy have been published. Sjögren and Sundqvist233 found that ultrasonic instrumentation with 0.5% NaOCl irrigation eliminated

Endodontic instruments are employed in ultrasonic units oscillating at frequencies ranging from 25 to 30 kHz.75,252 Ultrasonic irrigation of the root canal can be performed with or without simultaneous ultrasonic instrumentation. The term passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI) has been proposed to describe the latter approach, in which
“passive” relates to the non-cutting action of the ultrasonically activated instrument.252,263 PUI can refer to either intracanal placement of irrigant with a syringe followed by ultrasonic activation or continuous delivery of irrigant through an ultrasonic handpiece.75

In addition to warming and moving the irrigant to areas of complex anatomy, ultrasound seems to exert further antibacterial and cleaning effects through acoustic streaming and cavitation (Fig 1423).1,3,117,252 Acoustic streaming and cavitation may disturb and deagglomerate endodontic biofilms, flush out the detached bacterial cells from the canal, and render the resultant planktonic bacteria more susceptible to the enhanced antibacterial effects of warmed
NaOCl.5,75,93 Cavitation may produce a transient weakening of the bacterial cell wall and cytoplasmic membrane, making the bacteria more susceptible to NaOCl.37,75 The efficacy of acoustic streaming and cavitation depends on the free displacement amplitude of the

However, most of these studies demonstrated that none of the tested irrigation devices was able to completely remove debris from artificial irregularities in the apical part of the root canal.171,172

In vitro findings on the effectiveness of PUI using NaOCl in reducing bacterial populations have been somewhat inconclusive. One study showed that it was superior to syringe irrigation,88 but others found no significant differences.208,244 Harrison et al81 reported that
PUI was as effective in bacterial reduction as 1 week of calcium hydroxide intracanal medication, but neither led to complete bacterial elimination in all cases.

The teeth were anatomically matched based on micro-CT scans, experimentally contaminated, instrumented with NaOCl irrigation, and then subjected to the supplementary approaches. A study evaluated the outcome of the root canal treatment of single-rooted teeth with apical periodontitis either with or without the use of PUI to activate the irrigant.108 Satisfactory results (absence or reduction of the lesion) were observed in 95% of the teeth in the PUI group and in 88% in the group without PUI, with no significant difference. The authors concluded that the use of additional ultrasonic activation of the irrigant did not improve the success rate of root canal treatment.

### Laser and photodynamic therapy

Light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation (LASER) is a mechanism used to transform light of several frequencies into chromatic radiation in the visible, infrared, and ultraviolet regions, with all waves in phase and capable of inducing power and heat when focused on a certain area.95 Different lasers have been tested for various potential endodontic applications, including their ability to disinfect root canals. Although promising results have been achieved in vitro,56 root canal disinfection with lasers may be a problem in narrow canals. In addition, there is the risk of thermal damage to the periodontal tissues. The high cost of laser devices is another

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 808 -->

impediment to their widespread clinical use. Most importantly, there are no clinical studies consistently showing the safety and effectiveness of the laser devices used for intracanal disinfection.

Photon-induced photoacoustic streaming (PIPS) (Fotona,
Ljubljana, Slovenia) is based on the direct shock waves generated in a liquid by an Er:YAG laser energy at sub-ablative power levels. The laser system is equipped with a fiber optic tip, and sub-ablative parameters are used with minimal thermal effect. Under laser application, the flow dynamics of the irrigant is increased, and it is expected to cause the lysis of bacteria and destroy biofilms. Some laboratory studies have shown that PIPS has good antibacterial activity, including against biofilms attached to dentin and bacteria in dentinal tubules.7,16,70,98,134 A clinical study reported that PIPS improved disinfection and exerted no influence in the incidence of postoperative pain.115

Low-intensity lasers have been suggested for use in photodynamic therapy (PDT), or photoactivated disinfection. PDT utilizes light of a specific wavelength to activate a nontoxic photoactive dye (photosensitizer) in the presence of oxygen. The transfer of energy from the activated photosensitizer to available oxygen results in the formation of highly reactive oxygen species such as singlet oxygen and free radicals that can kill microorganisms by damaging essential cellular molecules, including proteins, membrane lipids, and nucleic acids.97 Most photosensitizers are activated by red light at wavelengths of 630 to 700 nm. Photosensitizers commonly used in PDT include phenothiazine dyes (methylene blue and toluidine blue), phthalocyanines, chlorines, porphyrins, xanthenes, and monoterpene.97 Nanoparticles have also been associated with photosensitizers for use in PDT.138,187,188 Diode laser systems have been widely used in PDT because they are easy to handle, portable, and cost-effective.

### GentleWave Multisonic Wave System

The GentleWave Multisonic Wave System (Sonendo, Laguna Hills,
CA, USA) is a device developed for cleaning and disinfecting the root canal through the generation and propagation of a broad spectrum of sound waves (multisonic waves) (Fig 14-26). It was initially designed as an innovative noninstrumentation approach, but changes in the protocol included an initial instrumentation up to a small file size (e.g.
no. 20). Multisonic waves are generated at the tip of the GentleWave handpiece, which is placed inside the pulp chamber. The irrigant is continuously supplied by the console to the handpiece. Therefore,
NaOCl is delivered to the root canal through the handpiece and activated by a wide spectrum of acoustic waves. The optimized irrigation solution enters the canal, creating a powerful shear force that causes hydrodynamic cavitation in the form of a “cavitation cloud.” There is a continuous formation of microbubbles that, combined with multisonic energy and fluid dynamics, result in the dissolution of pulp tissue and the removal of bacterial biofilms.80,124,186

This system has found enthusiastic users among those who adhere to the philosophy of minimally invasive endodontics, since it requires minimal instrumentation. There are studies indicating good outcome results after the use of GentleWave.191,192 However, there is a need for studies evaluating its antimicrobial efficacy as well as its long-term results compared with techniques commonly used in endodontics.

## Concluding remarks

Although most OSD approaches have shown the potential to improve cleaning and disinfection in a single visit, the majority of results come from ex vivo studies, and as such represent low-level evidence. There

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 814 -->

is no consistent clinical evidence showing that these OSD approaches can predictably reduce the bacterial bioburden to levels significantly below that achieved by conventional instrumentation/irrigation. Thus, even though research has been directed toward expediting disinfection, current evidence indicates the need for an interappointment antimicrobial medication to enhance disinfection of the root canal system in teeth with primary or posttreatment apical periodontitis. Too much time is still spent (and usually wasted!) arguing about whether or not an interappointment intracanal medication should be used. More time and effort should be spent finding more effective and predictable ways to consistently disinfect root canals in a single visit. This is a very interesting and important area for current and future research.
