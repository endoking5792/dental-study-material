One of the most noble and important tasks of the dental profession is to relieve pain and suffering. About 85% to 90% of all emergency cases seen in the dental office involve pain of pulpal or periradicular origin,16,83 and many of these patients require root canal intervention to alleviate their symptoms.

Endodontic emergencies consist of pulpal and periradicular conditions associated with pain and/or swelling that require immediate diagnosis and management. Successful emergency treatment leading to the remission of symptoms is highly rewarding for both the patient and clinician and significantly enhances the patient’s confidence in the dentist. Conversely, failure to provide pain relief results in frustration for both the patient and clinician and may undermine the patient– dentist relationship.

This chapter highlights the principles and practice of the management of endodontic emergencies of infectious origin. Many alternative treatment options are presented, taking into consideration possible technical limitations, time constraints, and difficulties posed by the complex anatomy (curvature, atresia and/or calcification, etc) of the root canal.

## Emergency versus urgency

The differentiation between emergency and urgency is not just a matter of semantics. It allows the clinician to better deal with the patient’s problem and find a solution of mutual convenience. An urgent condition is less critical and does not necessarily require immediate attention; however, if left untreated, it may evolve into a more severe critical condition (or emergency). Situations requiring immediate treatment to relieve pain and suffering are regarded as emergencies.
Therefore, the difference between emergency and urgency is basically a matter of severity.

Many pulpal and periradicular disease conditions prompt the patient to seek treatment for pain and/or swelling. Whereas a few of these conditions do not require endodontic intervention for pain relief and constitute cases of urgency, true emergencies usually require root canal intervention for the relief of symptoms.

## Pulpal pain – treatment principles

Sensory innervation of the pulp and periradicular tissues originates from the maxillary and mandibular divisions of the trigeminal nerve (fifth pair of cranial nerves) (Fig 18-1). Trigeminal sensory neurons have a primary afferent projection that terminates as free nerve endings in the pulp and periodontium. Approximately 1,000 to 2,000 nerves enter a single tooth; of these, 80% are unmyelinated fibers (C fibers and sympathetic nerves) and 20% are myelinated fibers. Pulpal pain is the result of the stimulation of two types of trigeminal sensory nerve fibers, namely A-δ fibers and C fibers.

A-δ fibers are myelinated, rapid-conduction fibers (6 to 30 m/s) with diameters ranging from 1 to 5 μm and a low excitability threshold (Table 18-1).47,86 After leaving the Rashkow’s nervous plexus, located at the subodontoblastic cell-free zone, these fibers lose their sheath of Schwann cells and terminate as free nerve endings at the odontoblastic layer and the pulpal border of dentin. A-δ fibers mediate the sharp, transient pain typical of dentin sensitivity. Once exposed to the oral cavity, dentin is sensitive to stimuli, even in the absence of pathologic changes in the pulp.

In the early stages of pulp inflammation (reversible pulpitis), the excitability threshold of A-δ nerve fibers may decrease, making them more susceptible to external stimuli (mainly cold). Pain resulting from the stimulation of A-δ nerve fibers is usually provoked, sharp, fast, and of short duration, i.e. it subsides immediately or shortly after the

Dentin hypersensitivity and reversible pulpitis do not require endodontic intervention.

### Dentin hypersensitivity

### Features

Dentin hypersensitivity is characterized by acute, sharp pain of short duration that is provoked by mechanical, osmotic, thermal, and bacterial stimuli. It is very often associated with gingival recession, which in about 10% of cases promotes exposure of dentin at the neck of the tooth due to a lack of coaptation between the enamel and

cementum (Fig 18-2).46 Dentin hypersensitivity can affect 42% of individuals between 18 and 35 years of age.94

### Management

It has been recommended that treatment for dentin hypersensitivity begins with the patient using a toothpaste with a desensitizing agent (arginine/calcium carbonate, potassium or strontium salts, stannous fluoride),34,51,54,73,97 which can relieve symptoms when used twice per day, massaging the affected tooth for about 1 minute. Dentifrices with arginine/calcium carbonate have shown excellent results in relieving dentin hypersensitivity.39,95 Good results have also been reported for compounds containing strontium acetate or stannous fluoride.39,95 Potassium nitrate has been effectively used in the composition of some dentifrices for having the recognized ability to reduce sensory activity of the dentinal nerve fibers.36-38,64

If the hypersensitivity does not resolve or if it becomes more pronounced, treatment at the dental office should be performed,50

which generally consists of promoting occlusion of the exposed tubules by mechanical or chemical means (Fig 18-3).13,31,52,64,87

Tubular occlusion can be induced by:

Mechanical means, using resin, glass-ionomer cement, or a varnish;30,48,75 or
Chemical means, by the precipitation of calcium salts, usually following the application of oxalates3,14,22,41 or fluorides.41,56

Laser has also been proposed for the management of dentin hypersensitivity, showing promising results.9,21,28,57,61,75 Its effects can occur directly on nerve fibers (low-power laser) or by inducing mechanical blockage of dentinal tubules (high-power laser).

### Reversible pulpitis

### Features

Reversible pulpitis occasionally causes pain similar to that described for dentin hypersensitivity. Clinical and radiographic examinations reveal a deep caries lesion or a recently placed or fractured restoration. The pulp is not yet exposed.

### Management

Management consists of removing the carious tissue or defective restoration, cleaning the cavity (rinsing with 0.2% chlorhexidine), and placing a temporary or definitive restoration (Fig 18-4). In teeth with deep cavities and close proximity to the pulp, it is recommended to line the cavity floor with a calcium hydroxide cement before restoring the cavity (Fig 18-5).

## Periradicular pain – treatment principles

Bacterial aggression to the periradicular tissues induces the release of a myriad of chemical mediators of inflammation.59,77 Many of these substances act on the microcirculation to cause vasodilatation and increased vascular permeability, resulting in the exit of fluids from the vessels (exudation) and edema formation.33 This results in an increase in tissue hydrostatic pressure and compression of sensory nerve fibers. Such increased pressure is critical to tissues such as the periodontal ligament, which have a limited space to expand during inflammation. Therefore, increased tissue pressure resulting in nerve compression is the main cause of periradicular pain associated with acute inflammation.

In addition to the increase in tissue pressure, host mediators (e.g.
bradykinin and histamine) and bacterial factors (e.g.
lipopolysaccharides) may cause pain through the direct activation of nociceptors.15,70,85 Other host mediators such as prostaglandins do not activate fibers directly, but sensitize them, reducing their activation thresholds and making them more susceptible to mediators that elicit pain.

In cases of abscess and/or cellulitis, it is of paramount importance to perform incision and drainage to reduce tissue pressure (and pain) and decrease the number of bacteria, bacterial products, and host mediators in the affected area. In cases where systemic antibiotic therapy is indicated, drainage decreases the “inoculum effect” and improves circulation to the affected area, resulting in enhanced antibacterial efficacy and better delivery of the antibiotic.6

Since periradicular pain of inflammatory origin is usually caused by bacteria infecting the root canal, the ideal treatment should encompass elimination or maximal reduction of intracanal bacterial populations, especially those located at the most apical part of the canal. Therefore, for a predictably effective emergency treatment, the inflammatory exudate should be properly drained and the root canal completely instrumented at the emergency visit. Due to time constraints related to most emergency conditions, some alternative

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1031 -->

strategies may be necessary. Opening the pulp chamber and cleaning the most coronal two-thirds of the root canal may promote pain relief due to differences in periradicular and atmospheric pressure. As the former is higher, the periradicular exudate tends to seep into the canal, which may be decisive for pain relief, even in amounts too small for the clinician to notice.

In these necrotic cases, a crown-down instrumentation technique should be used to prepare the canal. The coronal segments of the canal are first cleaned and disinfected, and then the most apical part is instrumented, provided the available time, operator skills, and root anatomy permit. Large apical instrumentation and patency files are recommended to enhance bacterial elimination (see Chap 12) and establish a route of drainage through the canal. Some clinicians fear that apical preparation at the emergency visit may promote apical extrusion of infection debris, predisposing to exacerbation of the inflammatory response. In fact, the risk of apical extrusion is basically the same at the emergency visit as at any other subsequent visit. In the present authors’ opinion, there is a lower risk of exacerbation at the emergency visit, when the periradicular tissues are already acutely inflamed and are, theoretically, better prepared for additional aggression. Moreover, postoperative symptoms can be prevented or controlled by long-acting local anesthetics such as bupivacaine, and by prescribed analgesics such as 400 to 600 mg ibuprofen.24,49 Other
NSAIDs, including diclofenac, naproxen, and ketoprofen, are also very effective in controlling postoperative endodontic pain. In cases of severe pain, the patient is recommended to take 650 to 1,000 mg acetaminophen in between doses of ibuprofen.91 Patients with NSAID intolerance may need to use 650 to 1,000 mg acetaminophen, combined with an opioid (60 mg codeine or 10 mg hydrocodone).91

If the root canal is only partially instrumented at the emergency visit, remission of symptoms may still occur. As mentioned before, access opening generates a pressure difference (periradicular and atmospheric), which may induce drainage of the periradicular inflammatory exudate into the canal; even if minimal and sometimes not noticeable clinically, this drainage can be of a sufficient magnitude

to reduce tissue pressure in the inflamed periradicular tissues.
However, remission of symptoms is much more predictable when the canal is cleaned and disinfected to its full extent. This was demonstrated in a clinical study of patients with symptomatic apical periodontitis.58 Success of the emergency treatment was significantly greater when the canals were fully prepared and medicated with calcium hydroxide than when the preparation was not carried out and the patient was only medicated with analgesics.

It is also important to point out that even though symptoms may resolve after incomplete canal preparation, there is an increased risk of a flare-up in the next appointment scheduled for root canal preparation. This is because teeth with preoperative symptoms of apical periodontitis are at higher risk of a flare-up,29,69,78,89,98 which is usually caused by the apical extrusion of infected debris during root canal instrumentation.65 The clinician and the patient should be aware of this risk, which might minimize frustration in case a flare-up occurs.

### Features

Treatment varies according to the stage of the abscess (initial, intermediate, or advanced).

### General remarks on abscess management

Whenever possible, the clinician should wait until all purulent exudate has drained via the canal and/or incision. Complete drainage is usually characterized by a transition from purulent to bloody to serous exudate. Once drainage is completed, the canal should be cleaned and shaped, medicated, and coronally sealed. Leaving the tooth open for drainage can cause further problems by allowing intracanal bacterial populations to increase significantly, by allowing new bacteria (including enteric bacteria that are seldom found in primary infections but are able to cause difficult-to-resolve secondary infections) to enter the canal,5,55,72 or by allowing substrates needed for bacterial growth to seep into the canal; all these factors create a need for additional appointments and thus increase the risk of further exacerbation.68,92,93 Leaving the tooth open is justifiable only when there is no available time to clean and shape the root canal or when drainage does not cease in the expected time, which is very rare. If this is the case, the clinician should warn the patient of possible complications.

The most important elements of effective patient management are correct diagnosis, proper drainage, and effective infection control. In an otherwise healthy patient, chemomechanical preparation of the infected root canals and incision and drainage of the area of

periradicular swelling usually lead to rapid improvement of clinical signs and symptoms. Most endodontic infections can be treated effectively without adjunctive antibiotics.

For effective drainage, a stab incision is made through the periosteum in the most dependent site of swelling. The incision must be long enough to allow blunt dissection using a curved hemostat or the insertion of a periosteal elevator under the periosteum for drainage of pockets of inflammatory exudate. A rubber dam drain or a Penrose drain should be used in patients with progressive abscess or cellulitis to maintain an open pathway for drainage. In cases of localized fluctuant swelling, drain placement may not be indicated if complete evacuation of the purulent exudate is believed to have occurred.

Antibiotics are not recommended for irreversible pulpitis, acute apical periodontitis, draining sinus tracts, flare-up prevention, or postincision and drainage of a localized abscess in an otherwise healthy patient.20,27,35,44,53,90 In these situations, there is no significant benefit of antibiotics, and they may place the patient at risk of antibiotic side effects or microbial resistance. The use of antibiotics is discussed in more detail in Chapter 19.

Analgesics (not antibiotics) are indicated for the treatment of pain.
Antibiotics are recommended for the treatment of progressive or persistent infections with systemic signs and symptoms such as fever (temperature > 38° C), malaise, cellulitis, and muscle trismus. In these cases, antibiotic therapy is used as an adjunct to effective disinfection of the root canal.

In cases of fluctuant abscess, incision for drainage is a widely recommended procedure to expedite the resolution of the pathologic condition. However, in cases of cellulitis, characterized by an indurated swelling, there is controversy about whether performing an incision is the best approach. This controversy is accentuated by the lack of solid scientific evidence providing support for either approach.

The arguments in favor of incision of indurated cellulitis emphasize the importance of searching for purulent exudate and/or creating a pathway of drainage to prevent the further spread of the abscess,

reducing the increased tissue pressure associated with edema, and thus providing significant pain relief. Furthermore, the incision would permit drainage of the accumulated inflammatory mediators associated with the abscess, improving circulation to the tissues and providing better delivery of systemic antibiotics to the infected site.
However, all these arguments have not been supported by scientific evidence.

As far as the incision and drainage approach to treat cellulitis is concerned, claims against it state that it can disrupt the physiologic barriers and promote diffusion and extension of the infection.42 One of the few studies on the subject included patients in need of emergency treatment who presented with cellulitis (64%) and fluctuant abscesses (36%) and received either penicillin or clindamycin.8 Complete root canal preparation was performed and the patients were randomly divided into two treatment groups:
incision and drainage with drain placement, and a mock incision and drainage procedure with mock drain placement. All patients received a combination of ibuprofen/acetaminophen and, if needed, an opioidcontaining escape medication. The findings indicated that patients who received a mock incision and drainage procedure with mock drain placement showed a better outcome than the other group.
Another study revealed no significant difference in the length of hospital stay for patients with cellulitis or fluctuant swelling who were treated with or without incision and drainage.18 These findings serve to question the need to perform incision and drainage, not only in cellulitis cases but also in cases of fluctuant abscess. Aziz62 recommended that deep neck infections that present with an indurated swelling (cellulitis stage) in a clinically stable patient should be treated with intravenous antibiotics alone (in addition to the removal of the cause). If fluctuation is apparent, however, management should include immediate surgical drainage.

## Flare-ups

Flare-ups are characterized by the development of severe pain and/or swelling between treatment appointments.60,80 The typical patient returns on the same day or on the day following professional intervention complaining of severe pain or exhibiting facial swelling.
Flare-ups are not pathologic entities but events characterized by the development of a symptomatic apical periodontitis or acute apical abscess secondary to treatment. Flare-ups are caused by bacterial and/or iatrogenic factors (overinstrumentation, apical extrusion of irrigants, etc).45,60,65,67,80

There are numerous studies evaluating the influence of diverse factors in the incidence of flare-ups. The most significant candidate risk factors include the occurrence of preoperative pain and infected root canals (necrotic pulps and retreatment cases).43,69,78,84,89

Systemic diseases do not seem to be a risk factor for flare-ups,78

although there are reports that diabetic patients may be more prone to developing this condition.19 The incidence of flare-ups has been shown to range from 2.5% to 16%.25 A meta-analysis of flare-up studies revealed a prevalence of 8.4%.88

### Microbial causes of flare-ups

Bacterial infection of the root canal is the main cause of periradicular inflammation.
Whatever the bacterial mechanisms may be, interappointment flare-ups are the result of an exacerbation of periradicular inflammation in response to a sudden increase in the number of bacterial irritants. A new situation arises in which the host is challenged by a higher number of bacterial cells and/or products than it is used to. As a consequence, the balance between bacterial aggression and host defense is disrupted in favor of the former. The host mounts an acute inflammatory response at the periradicular tissue level in an attempt to reestablish equilibrium. Some special situations during endodontic treatment can facilitate the development of flare-ups of infectious origin. These situations include a) apical extrusion of debris, b) a change in the intracanal microbiota and/or environmental conditions, and c) secondary intraradicular infection (contamination).

### Apical extrusion of debris

Extrusion of infected debris to the periradicular tissues during chemomechanical preparation is allegedly one of the principal causes of postoperative pain.60,65,67 In teeth with asymptomatic apical periodontitis, a balance has been established between bacterial aggression from the infected canal and host defenses at the periradicular tissue level. If bacteria are forced into the periradicular tissues during chemomechanical preparation, the host will be challenged by a larger number of irritants than before. Due to this transient disruption of the balance between aggression and defense, the host will mobilize an acute inflammatory response in an attempt to reestablish the equilibrium (Fig 18-11).

The risks of flare-ups during treatment of infected canals can be even higher in the event of overinstrumentation. Exacerbations caused by overinstrumentation are likely to develop as a result of mechanical injury to the periradicular tissues, which is usually coupled with apical extrusion of a significant amount of debris containing bacteria.

Retreatment of teeth with posttreatment disease has been found to be a risk factor for postoperative pain.29,78,84 During the removal of root canal filling material and further instrumentation, filling remnants

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1047 -->

and infected debris tend to be pushed ahead of the instruments and forced into the periradicular tissues, exacerbating inflammation and causing pain.29,84 The solvents used during filling removal are also cytotoxic and may contribute to the exacerbation of periradicular inflammation.4

Forcing bacteria and their products into the periradicular tissues can stimulate an acute inflammatory response, the intensity of which will depend on the number and/or virulence of the extruded bacteria.
Therefore, quantitative and qualitative factors are decisive in the causation of interappointment pain as a result of the apical extrusion of debris. The number of bacterial cells extruded (bacterial load) is the quantitative factor, while the virulence of the extruded bacteria is the qualitative factor. All instrumentation techniques promoted apical extrusion of debris/bacteria in
70% to
100% of the cases.1,2,10,11,17,76,96 However, factors including the preparation technique as well as instrument design and motion significantly affect the amount of extruded debris. Differences in the amount of extruded debris may be crucial for the development of postoperative pain, as instruments/techniques that extrude more debris allegedly increase the risk of exacerbation. Irrespective of whether hand or enginedriven instruments in reciprocating motion or continuous rotation motion are used, crown-down techniques usually extrude less debris and should preferentially be used for instrumentation of infected root canals, maintaining enlargement to the root canal limits. Therefore, the quantitative factor is more likely to be under the control of the clinician, whereas the qualitative factor is more difficult to control. If virulent clonal types of pathogenic bacteria are present in the root canal system and are propelled into the periradicular tissues during instrumentation, even a small amount of infected debris will have the potential to cause or exacerbate periradicular inflammation. The presence of virulent clonal types of bacteria in the canal microbiota may be why teeth with preoperative symptoms are at higher risk of developing postoperative symptoms.

### Change in the intracanal microbiota and/or

### environmental conditions

The balance between bacterial aggression and host defense achieved in an asymptomatic chronic pathologic condition may be disrupted by changes in the root canal microbiota or in the environmental conditions that favor the bacterial side of the equation.
In such cases, exacerbation can occur.

Incomplete root canal preparation may cause such changes.
Ideally, chemomechanical preparation should be completed in a single visit. The microbiota associated with endodontic infection are usually established as a mixed community, and changes in this consortium will affect both the environment and the remaining community members. Potent exogenous forces such as instruments and antimicrobial substances are needed to eradicate the bacterial community from the root canal system. However, incomplete chemomechanical preparation can disrupt the community balance by eliminating some inhibitory species and by leaving other previously inhibited species behind to grow and proliferate.74 If these overgrown species are virulent and reach sufficient numbers, they can result in increased damage to the periradicular tissues and exacerbation of the lesion (Fig 18-12).

It is salient to point out that teeth with preoperative symptoms are at higher risk of flare-ups. One of the reasons for this is the presence of virulent clonal types of some pathogenic species, which play an important role in the preoperative symptoms;71 if favored by treatment-induced environmental changes or when apically extruded, these clonal types can be pivotal in exacerbating inflammation.
Furthermore, environmental changes induced by incomplete instrumentation have the potential to induce the activation of virulence genes. As a result of this increase in the community virulence, a previously asymptomatic tooth may become symptomatic.

The entry of oxygen into the root canal is another environmental change induced by endodontic intervention. It has been suggested that this could alter the oxidation-reduction potential (Eh) in the root

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1049 -->

canal, resulting in acute exacerbation.40 This is based on the theory that an increase in Eh could induce the microbial growth pattern to change from anaerobic to aerobic, with consequent overgrowth of facultative bacteria (Fig 18-13). The overgrown facultative bacteria might then precipitate acute periradicular inflammation. This mechanism remains speculative and there is no scientific evidence supporting its validity.

### Secondary intraradicular infections

Secondary intraradicular infections are caused by microorganisms that were not present in the primary infection but which gained entry into the root canal either during treatment, between appointments, or after the conclusion of the endodontic treatment.63
New microorganisms can enter the root canal system by several pathways, the most common being a breach in the aseptic chain during treatment (see Chap 6).68 If the microorganisms that gained access to the root canal succeed in surviving and colonizing this new environment, a secondary infection will establish itself and may cause postoperative pain, provided that the newly established species populations are virulent and dense enough to induce acute periradicular inflammation (Fig 18-14). Secondary infections can cause posttreatment apical periodontitis and persistent symptoms. As flare-ups usually develop a few hours after professional intervention, a secondary infection could be the cause in cases where there is a heavy inoculation of bacteria in the canal, penetration of highly virulent species, or when concomitant to accidents such as overinstrumentation.

### Management

Management of flare-ups will depend on the type of secondary pathology established, whether it be symptomatic apical periodontitis or acute apical abscess.

### Secondary symptomatic apical periodontitis

The temporary coronal restoration is removed and the canal profusely irrigated with 2.5% NaOCl. Working length is confirmed, apical preparation is revised by using the master apical file (if possible, enlarging to one size bigger), and apical foramen patency is checked.
Intracanal medication (CHPG or CHCx paste) is placed.
A coronal seal is established and analgesics prescribed.

The presence of blood in the apical canal, as revealed by paper points, is a sign of overinstrumentation. In these cases, the working length needs to be reassessed.

### Secondary acute apical abscess

An abscess is present and should be treated accordingly (see above).

NSAIDs should be prescribed postoperatively after the management of flare-ups. Ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, or ketoprofen can be used because they are all effective in controlling pain of endodontic origin.

If the overall frequency of flare-ups in a clinician’s daily practice far exceeds 2% to 5% of cases with infected root canals (necrotic pulps or retreatment cases), something is going wrong with the professional approach, which should be carefully reviewed and revised in terms of concepts and practice.

## Post-obturation pain

Mild discomfort after obturation of root canals is not uncommon. The clinician should warn the patient about this possibility and recommend mild analgesics in the event of pain. Post-obturation pain rarely constitutes a true emergency. The discomfort usually subsides spontaneously within a few days.

Harrison et al26 reported the occurrence of post-obturation pain in
48% of the teeth that were treated. In most cases, symptoms developed in the first 24 hours, and 92% of the patients were asymptomatic within 7 days. A multicenter two-part clinical study81,82

showed that the incidence of posttreatment pain was lower after root canal obturation (6%) than after chemomechanical preparation (22%).
