## Introduction

Healing of apical periodontitis relies primarily on reducing the number of bacteria inside the root canal system and sealing portals of entry and exit; it also relies on an adequate immune system that can allow apical healing and bone remodeling.82 Incomplete obturation, which was initially thought to be responsible for treatment failure,45 is an indication of inadequate cleaning and shaping of the root canal system. Healing of apical periodontitis can be achieved in the absence of root canal obturation material, following sufficient canal disinfection and the presence of an adequate coronal restoration.57,98

For healing to take place, the root canal system has to be thoroughly disinfected. This, however, can only guarantee short-term success, since, to date, achieving a sterile canal is nearly impossible. The failure to seal portals of entry and exit can allow remaining bacteria inside the root canal system to repopulate and cause irritation to the apical area. It can also allow periradicular tissue fluid to seep into the canal space, providing nutrition to residual bacteria. These scenarios will result in reinfection of the root canal system and persistence or resurgence of periapical disease.

The objectives of root canal obturation are to:116

Seal or “entomb” the remaining bacteria inside the root canal space.
Stop periradicular tissue fluids and exudates from seeping into the canal and providing nutrition to residual bacteria.
Create a barrier between the oral cavity and the periapical tissues.

Achieving these objectives will prevent communication of bacteria with the periradicular area and will therefore sustain a long-term successful treatment (Fig 15-1).

## Time of obturation

The ideal time of obturation should be after complete cleaning and shaping of the root canal system and the achievement of a dry canal space. Technically, all clinical cases can potentially be completed in a single visit. However, the following factors may delay the obturation procedure and extend it to involve second or multiple visits:

Pulp and periapical status.
Clinical presentation.
Case difficulty, clinical experience, and time.

The pulp status of the tooth reflects the level of bacterial contamination within the root canal space. Teeth with vital pulps indicates that the level of bacterial contamination is low and the completion of treatment in a single visit would be appropriate. Teeth with necrotic pulps or previously treated teeth as well as radiographic signs of apical periodontitis reflect a high level of bacterial contamination, and thus more than one visit may be required to adequately disinfect the root canal space.51,52,71,104,109 Ricucci and
Siqueira94 showed a correlation between the size of the apical periodontitis lesion and the bacterial load inside the root canal space, suggesting that there may be more bacteria in clinical cases with large periapical lesions, and thus more time would be required to achieve adequate disinfection. A protracted debate exists regarding whether infected canals should be completed in a single visit or in multiple visits with the utilization of intracanal medicaments. This issue is discussed in detail in Chapters 11 and 20.

A key element prior to obturation is achieving a dry canal at the end of the treatment, with no fluids or exudate present. Such fluids can affect the seal, provide nutrition to remaining bacteria inside the root canal space, and compromise the treatment outcome. The cause of persistent exudate can be derived from the preoperative diagnosis of an acute or chronic apical infection that continues to drain into the canals. It can also occur in the maxillary posterior teeth with roots in,

## Obturation quality

The quality of endodontic treatment is often determined based on the quality of root canal obturation. The length and density of the obturation material are the two main parameters that can be used to assess the root canal filling quality. The root canal filling should be dense, with no voids along the entire length of the filled canal, in order to seal all portals of entry and exit and to prevent apical microleakage. The presence of voids or insufficient filling material has often been associated with poor endodontic quality and treatment failure.15,88,122

The length of obturation reflects the length of instrumentation – also known as the working length (WL) – during chemomechanical preparation. Ideally, the length of obturation should extend from the canal orifice to the dentinocemental junction (DCJ), which represents the end of the root canal space and the beginning of the periodontal ligament.58,59 Identification of the DCJ may not be clinically possible due to the complexity of the apical area. Two distinct anatomical landmarks are characteristic for the apical area: 1) the apical foramen, where the canal exits the root; and 2) the apical constriction, which represents the narrowest diameter of the canal and where instrumentation and obturation should be terminated.59,90

Clinicians often rely on the radiographic apex as a reference point to determine the position of the DCJ. It has been shown that the DCJ is located around 0.5 mm from the radiographic apex.98 However, this distance can range between 0.2 to 2.5 mm.85 It can also be laterally positioned and varies with age due to the deposition of cementum with time (Fig 15-3).85 The presence of periapical disease may also result in resorption of the apical foramen and damage to the apical constriction.27,73 Therefore, a more accurate determination of the
DCJ would be the electronic apex locator, in conjunction with radiographic and clinical assessment, to avoid the possibility of apical extrusion of root filling material.

## Overextension vs overfilling

The terms overextension and overfilling are often used interchangeably despite having different meanings. While both terms mean the extension of the root canal filling material beyond the apical foramen, overextension refers to situations in which some root canal filling material (gutta-percha and/or sealer) extends beyond the apex while the root canal space is inadequately filled. Overfilling, on the other hand, indicates adequate filling of the root canal space and extrusion of excessive material beyond the apex (Fig 15-6). While both scenarios are undesirable, the former is more likely to impact the outcome of the endodontic treatment and result in clinical failure due to an inadequate seal and the presence of communication between the root canal space and the apical area. This communication is likely to irritate the apical area, which may result in delayed healing.
Riccuci and
Langeland90 investigated the histopathologic response of the periapical tissues to endodontic procedures when performed short of, or beyond, the apical constriction. These authors found that the most favorable histologic conditions were when the instrumentation and obturation remained at the level of, or short of, the apical constriction. Extrusion of sealer and/or gutta-percha into the periapical tissue resulted in severe inflammation, despite the clinical absence of pain. This is also referred to as subclinical inflammation. Fristad et al32 and Halse and
Molven42 found that root canal filling that extended beyond the apical foramen was characterized by late signs of healing that may take more than 10 years to occur. Similarly, Azim et al7 showed that cases with gutta-percha that was extruded beyond the radiographic apex resulted in delayed healing by almost 14 months compared with

## Root canal filling material

The Guide to Clinical Endodontics of the American Association of
Endodontists and the Consensus Report of the European Society of
Endodontology state that root canal sealers are used with bioacceptable semisolid or solid obturating materials to prevent bacterial and fluid leakage through the root canal system by filling the main canal, dentinal tubules, and accessory canals.

There are two primary groups of materials used for root canal filling: core materials and sealers.

### Core materials

Core materials occupy the major volume of the prepared canal.

## Filling the root canal space

Several obturation techniques have been proposed to fill the root canal space, most of which rely on the use of a bulk filling material (gutta-percha) and a flowable sealing material (sealer) to adequately fill and seal the canals. Among the most commonly used techniques are cold lateral condensation, warm vertical compaction, carrierbased obturation, and single cone techniques. Several in vitro studies have been conducted to compare the various techniques, most of which showed that warm obturation techniques can result in an obturation with greater density than cold lateral condensation.60,74

Adequate filling of the root canal space can be achieved using any technique (Fig 15-9). The success of any technique, however, depends on the clinician’s knowledge of the technique, clinical skills, and ability to follow instructions.18 From an outcome point of view, clinical studies showed no significant difference between the various obturation techniques that utilize gutta-percha and sealer.49,84

Regardless of the obturation technique used, several pre-obturation steps are required to ensure adequate filling of the entire root canal space.

### Pre-obturation steps

### Step 1: Determining the master cone

The determination of the master cone size and length is dependent on the length and extent of instrumentation. Ideally, the master cone should extend to the DCJ, which can be clinically determined using the electronic apex locator or within 0.5 to 2 mm from the radiographic apex, as indicated above.

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 860 -->

The master cone tip size should correspond to the largest file size used during instrumentation, also known as the master apical file (MAF). The taper of the master cone may vary depending on the instrumentation technique used and the obturation technique that will be utilized. The master cone that is selected should have a proper fit at the apex. The clinician should feel some resistance when attempting to remove the cone from the canal (this is referred to as
“tug back”). If the cone appears short or if it has a distorted or
“buckling” appearance, it may indicate short instrumentation or the presence of debris in the apical third, preventing the cone from further advancement (Fig 15-10). In that case, the canal may require further irrigation to remove the debris. If the cone continues to be short of the WL, a smaller cone may be considered. If the master cone is loose or overextended, a larger cone should be considered to adequately fit in the apical area. Clinicians should be aware that the
ISO and American Dental Association ANSI standards allow a degree of tolerance regarding the size of gutta-percha cones and instruments. As a result, gutta-percha cones may vary in their tip size and taper. The canal can be dried using a paper point placed to the extent of the

Gutta-percha cones are thermolabile and should be sterilized by the manufacturer. If this is not the case, they can be disinfected prior to obturation. Contamination has been reported in 5% to 8% of guttapercha cones taken directly from the original packaging. Following the opening of the packaging, this increased to almost 20%.39,76 This can occur due to the clinical environment and improper handling.

This step should not alter or affect the structure of the gutta-percha cone. ## Obturation techniques

### Cold lateral compaction

Cold lateral compaction (CLC) or lateral condensation is among the most commonly used obturation technique in dental schools. The technique does not require specialized equipment and is inexpensive and predictable. One important limitation of the technique is that, in curved canals, the spreaders may not reach the apical area. It also requires a longer time, and the forces used by the hand or finger spreaders may generate cracks or fractures within the root.12 It may also not be suitable for obturating all canals, e.g. it may be inappropriate to fill teeth with internal resorption, an open apex, or an irregular canal using the CLC technique due to the lack of adaptability of gutta-percha.

### Procedural steps for the CLC technique (Fig 15-14)

A 0.02-mm tapered gutta-percha cone coated with sealer on its tip (2 to 4 mm) is seated to the WL. The cone tip size should correspond to the MAF and tug back should be felt, as described above. The rationale for using a 0.02-mm tapered cone is to facilitate the insertion of the hand or finger spreaders within 1 to
2 mm from the apex. Using a tapered gutta-percha cone will not allow further advancement of the spreader closer to the apex or the exertion of excessive pressure, which may generate cracks within the root surface.

### Warm vertical compaction

The warm vertical compaction (WVC) technique was originally introduced by Herbert Schilder.101 The technique relies on warming small pieces of gutta-percha to transform them to the alpha phase, making them more flowable to adapt to the canal irregularities. They are then condensed using hand or finger pluggers (Fig 15-15). The technique was later modified and is now known as the continuous wave compaction (CWC) technique, which relies on heating equipment (Fig 15-16) to cut the master cone at the apical area (3 to
5 mm), followed by the packing of flowable gutta-percha into the middle and coronal thirds of the canal (Fig 15-17). WVC, with its modification, is currently the most commonly used obturation technique among endodontic specialists.41 The reason for this is its ability to provide a denser, more homogenous three-dimensional filling of the root canal space.18 For better adaptation of the master cone in the apical third of the canal, the heating system used to cut the master cone at the apical area needs to be placed 3 to 4 mm from the WL.112 While the WVC techniques overcame the limitations of lateral condensation to fill irregular spaces, the forces generated during vertical compaction appear to be similar to those of lateral condensation.12 WVC is also technique-sensitive and requires specialized equipment and adequate training to obtain an homogenous fill with no voids. In addition, there is a higher risk of extrusion associated with this technique.84

### Procedural steps for the WVC technique

These steps can be divided into two parts (Fig 15-18):

Downpack step. In this phase, the master cone is cut and adapted to the apical third of the canal.

The apical 3 to 4 mm of the master cone is coated with the sealer and placed to the WL.

A heating system (such as touch and heat or system B) is then adjusted at around 200° C and advanced to 3 to 4 mm from the WL while activating the heat, until resistance is met.

Once it has reached the designated position within the canal, the heat application is stopped for 2 to 3 seconds, then reactivated and moved in a sweeping motion outside the canal to sear off the master cone, leaving gutta-percha only at the apical third.

Pluggers are then advanced in the canal to pack the guttapercha and adapt it to the canal walls.

2.
Backfill step. In this phase, the remainder of the canal is filled using flowable gutta-percha. In the original Schilder technique, pieces of gutta-percha would be cut, placed in the canal, and packed with a warm plugger. Today, equipment such as Obtura (Obtura Spartan, Earth City, MO, USA) or Calamus (Dentsply

### Carrier-based obturation

Carrier-based obturation is an obturation system developed to combine the advantages of warm obturation techniques without the technique sensitivity associated with them. Carrier-based obturation utilizes obturators that are composed of a core carrier surrounded by gutta-percha. Prior to obturation, a verifier is used to confirm that the obturator selected is a good fit for the canal with the use of a periapical radiograph. Verifiers are tapered hand files that correspond to the MAF used during treatment. Once confirmed, the obturator is heated in a specifically designed oven for a set time at a certain temperature. The oven will soften the gutta-percha around the carrier, allowing it to flow and fill irregular spaces while the carrier is pushed to the WL. The handle of the carrier is then removed using a round bur, and the coronal portion of the fill is condensed using a plugger (Fig 15-20).

### Single-cone technique

As the name indicates, this technique utilizes a single gutta-percha cone that corresponds to the MAF used during cleaning and shaping.
The technique relies on the use of endodontic sealers to fill all the remaining spaces and canal irregularities (Fig 15-21). Controversy existed for a long time about whether the single-cone technique would be appropriate for root canal obturation. Due to the shrinkage and dissolution associated with most endodontic sealers, obturation techniques often relied on maximizing the amount of gutta-percha and minimizing the amount of sealer usage.13 Thus, the single-cone technique has not been a popular technique in the past few decades.
With the introduction of CSBSs, the single-cone technique resurfaced as a viable obturation technique. Improvements in newer sealers (such as CSBSs) aim to increase their physical and chemical stability.128 As indicated earlier, CSBSs do not shrink; on the contrary, due to their hydrophilic nature they expand as they absorb tissue fluids. This makes them good candidates to use with the single-cone technique.106

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 876 -->

The single-cone technique has been shown to have a high success rate when used during endodontic treatment and retreatment.16 The technique is also easy to use and is less technique-sensitive compared with all the other obturation techniques.125 For the technique to be properly conducted, the clinician should select a sealer that does not shrink upon setting, making the single-cone technique a “sealer-dependent” obturation technique. In other words, a single-cone technique can only be used in conjunction with sealers that do not shrink upon setting. Currently, CSBSs are the most appropriate sealers to use with this technique.

The tip of the master cone is also coated with the sealer and advanced gently into the canal to the WL. Filling large canals with one cone and sealer may result in multiple voids. In these cases, an additional cone can be placed in the canal using lateral condensation.

### Custom cone technique (solvent technique)

The custom cone technique relies on customizing the size of the cone to match the size of the canal. It is primarily employed in canals with an open apex or apical resorption where a master cone cannot adequately fit at the apex. The technique combines more than one cone together, using chloroform to create one larger cone or a

### Apical plug

Creating an apical plug using MTA is the technique of choice to manage cases with an open apex or apical resorption. The technique relies on the placement of MTA in the apical 3 to 6 mm of the canal to establish a good apical seal. The properties of MTA and its hydrophilic nature allow better adaption to the moist environment in the apical area. The placement of an MTA apical plug can be preceded with the placement of a resorbable collagen barrier such a
CollaCote (Zimmer Dental, Carlsbad, CA, USA) or CollaPlug (Zimmer
Dental) at the apical area to prevent the material from extruding to this area (Fig 15-24a). MTA placement requires specific carriers to place the material into the canal (Fig 15-24b). It also requires adequate visibility to gently pack the material in the apical third of the

canal. All these requirements make an MTA apical plug a techniquesensitive approach. MTA can be used to fill the apical 3 to 6 mm of the canal, followed by the placement of gutta-percha in the coronal portion (Fig 15-25). It can also be placed in the entire canal space (Fig 15-26). However, care should be taken to avoid placing MTA close to the clinical crown in order to avoid tooth discoloration.

## Coronal seal

Root canal treatment is not complete until a permanent restoration is placed. Exposure of gutta-percha to the oral cavity may result in coronal leakage and possible treatment failure. Most of the data supporting coronal leakage are derived from in vitro leakage studies.66,120 It is a good practice to seal the access cavity with a permanent restoration; ideally, this should take place immediately following obturation. Delaying the placement of a coronal restoration will subsequently delay the placement of a full-coverage restoration, which may result in the fracture of the temporary restoration and/or the tooth.87 Prior to placement of a coronal restoration, gutta-percha and sealer should be removed from the clinical crown and should not extend coronally to the cementoenamel junction to avoid discoloration of the clinical crown46 (Fig 15-28).58
