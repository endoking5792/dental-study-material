## Objectives

Chemomechanical preparation is intended to promote cleaning, disinfection, and shaping of the root canal. These distinct objectives are achieved through the utilization of instruments and irrigants during preparation. For many years, it was believed that the quality of the root canal obturation was the main factor influencing the treatment outcome. This concept became popular after the Washington study,109 which reported that more than 60% of teeth with posttreatment disease were associated with inadequate root canal fillings, as determined radiographically. However, one must bear in mind that two events happening at the same time (poor fillings and poor outcome) do not necessarily translate into a cause-and-effect relationship. The huge majority of failed cases associated with inadequate fillings were also inevitably related to poor cleaning, shaping, and disinfection, predisposing the teeth to persistent infection (Fig 12-1). In these cases, obturation merely reflects the aptness of chemomechanical procedures. While the importance of root canal obturation cannot be underestimated (see Chaps 11 and
15), the best outcomes in infected teeth are observed when root canal infection is eradicated or reduced to levels compatible with periradicular healing. In this regard, chemomechanical preparation assumes a role of paramount importance.

Although it may not be correct to place particular emphasis on any single endodontic procedure, chemomechanical preparation plays a pivotal role in disinfection. Instruments and irrigants act primarily on the main canal, which is the largest area of the system and consequently harbors the highest number of bacterial populations. In addition to the mechanical effects of instruments and the flow and backflow of irrigants during preparation, the use of an antimicrobial substance for irrigation has been shown to be essential to enhance bacterial elimination from infected canals (see Chap 11 for a detailed discussion).41,43,228

The objectives of cleaning, shaping, and disinfecting the root canal are not difficult to attain in teeth with an uncomplicated anatomy (Fig
12-2). However, given the anatomical complexity of most teeth, several studies have demonstrated that, regardless of which instrumentation techniques, instruments, and irrigants are used, chemomechanical procedures alone are unable to achieve thorough cleaning of the root canal system.14,64,116,161,201,221,230,245,258,261,271

While the main canal and minor anatomical irregularities are usually incorporated into the preparation, tissues present in other areas such as recesses, isthmuses, lateral canals, and apical ramifications usually remain unaffected by preparation because of the inherent physical limitations of instruments and the short time of retention of the irrigants within the root canal (Fig 12-3). Even areas of the main root canal may remain untouched by instruments and harbor tissue remnants and residual bacterial biofilms.116,161,226,261 If infected, tissue remnants may influence treatment outcome.180

Root canal preparation involves enlargement and carving a shape that should ideally be conical, with the largest diameter at the coronal end and the smallest diameter at the apical end (Fig 12-4). The initial shape of the canal should be incorporated into this cone during enlargement. Shaping is also intended to smooth the canal walls and facilitate further filling procedures.

In curved canals, however, attainment of an adequate shape may not be an easy task. When passed along the curvature, the instrument tends to straighten to return to its original shape. This results in more pressure applied by the instrument cutting blades against the inner wall of the curvature as well as on the opposite wall in the apical segment of the canal. These effects are more pronounced with greater curvatures and with larger and stiffer instruments and make the final shape of the preparation irregular, predisposing the task to procedural errors, including ledge, perforation, deviation (zip), foramen transportation, and blockage with debris (Fig 12-5).

In the past, because of the physical limitations of stainless steel instruments, the common recommendation was that curved canals

should be enlarged to small apical diameters. The reasons to reduce the apical preparation size in curved canals were basically that a) smaller preparations result in less cutting of the canal walls with consequently less uncontrolled cutting action, and b) small instruments are more flexible and therefore less likely to cause procedural errors.183 However, the greatest problem with small preparation sizes is that this may compromise the cleaning and disinfecting efficacy of chemomechanical procedures as well as the quality of the root canal filling.

In order to avoid these procedural accidents and make shaping safer and more predictable, modified instruments and techniques have been devised. Technological advancements have made it possible to manufacture instruments from alloys other than stainless steel, for instance, nickel-titanium (NiTi).

NiTi alloy represents a major breakthrough in endodontics, particularly regarding the preparation of curved root canals (Fig 126). As NiTi alloy has a lower modulus of elasticity than stainless steel,
NiTi instruments have increased elasticity and resistance to plastic deformation. They enable the preparation of curved canals that are larger at the apical part, more centered, and contain fewer deviations than those prepared with stainless steel instruments.55,108,213,284

Further instrument modifications include innovative designs of the helical shaft and tip, a larger or variable taper, and the length of the working part.

Although there are numerous studies showing the mechanical superiority of NiTi over stainless steel instruments for root canal preparation, only a few studies have compared the outcome of treatment using these instruments. While one study failed to observe a significant difference in periradicular healing after instrumentation with either hand stainless steel files or NiTi rotary instruments,125 two other studies showed a better outcome for teeth treated using NiTi instrumentation. Pettiette et al165 reported less posttreatment disease in teeth instrumented with hand NiTi files compared with those prepared with hand stainless steel files. The use of NiTi files was five times more likely to achieve healing than that of stainless steel

NiTi rotary instruments achieved higher healing rates (77%) than stainless steel hand files (60%). ## Kinetics of endodontic instruments

Endodontic instruments are operated in three types of motion, which vary depending on the intended action in the root canal:

### Removal

Removal motion is composed of three actions: the instrument is advanced in the canal, rotated clockwise (one or two turns along its own axis), and then pulled in the coronal direction without being totally removed. This motion is used to remove vital pulps, free debris, and cotton pellets or paper points from the canal. It is also useful during retreatment for the removal of previous filling material. In this case, engine-driven instruments can also be used under continuous clockwise rotation.

### Negotiation

In negotiation movement, the instrument is gradually advanced in the apical direction with simultaneous gentle alternating rotation to the right and left. If resistance is encountered, the instrument is withdrawn a few millimeters, then guided again in the apical direction with the same watch-winding motion.

### Reaming

Reaming action consists of a cutting rotation. For the instrument to cut dentin and enlarge the canal using this motion, it must bind the root canal walls. Reaming may be achieved by using a partial (hand) or continuous (engine-driven) clockwise rotation, always followed by a pull-back motion to disengage the file from the dentin and exert a

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 579 -->

cutting force. Reaming can also be achieved through reciprocating clockwise–counterclockwise rotation along the axis of the instrument.
This is also referred to as balanced force, oscillatory motion, or alternating rotary motion. In this case, the clockwise rotation pushes the file into the canal and allows the instrument’s cutting edges to engage the dentinal walls, and the counterclockwise turn cuts away the engaged dentin. For the latter to occur, the operator should keep the instrument from going outward in the canal by maintaining slight apical pressure during the counterclockwise movement. Very light pressure should be used during reaming movements.

The cutting ability of an instrument in reaming motion is influenced by the following factors:

Helix angle. The smaller the helix angle, the higher the cutting efficiency of the instrument (Fig 12-7).
Internal angle of the cutting edge. The smaller and more acute this angle is, the higher the cutting efficiency of the instrument.
For instance, instruments with triangular cross-sections (60 degrees) have higher cutting efficiency than square instruments (90 degrees) (Fig 12-8).
Rake angle. Instruments with a positive angle exert an effective cutting action, while those with a negative angle exert a scraping action (Fig 12-9).
Instrument alloy/dentin hardness ratio.
The harder the instrument in relation to dentin, the higher the cutting efficiency.
Rotational cutting speed. The higher the cutting speed, the less time required for root canal enlargement.

During the continuous reaming action of engine-driven rotary instruments, it is important to prevent immobilization of the instrument tip, which predisposes the procedure to torsional failure by making sure that the speed of advancement of the instrument in the canal is slower than the speed of cutting. A pecking or pumping motion, which lowers the apical forces during root canal preparation, has been recommended as an effective way to prevent instrument binding and reduce cyclic fatigue. When resistance from the canal walls is felt, inward advancement of the instrument should not exceed 1 to 3 mm beyond this point, and the inward motion should be accompanied by an alternating withdrawal motion to disengage the instrument tip (pecking motion). Deeper penetration would increase the frictional force against the dentinal walls, increasing the resistance to cutting and creating a load that might exceed the limit of material resistance to fracture. In curved canals, the pecking motion also reduces the

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 586 -->

concentration of tension in a certain area of the instrument during bending rotation, increasing its useful life in relation to cyclic fatigue.

### Filing

Filing action consists of placing the instrument in the canal to the desired length and then pressing it laterally against the canal wall while withdrawing it only 1 to 3 mm, without removing the instrument from the canal. This motion is repeated circumferentially to treat all canal walls, or it may be focused on regions of interest such as canal irregularities or ledged walls. This motion is of special importance for the instrumentation of long oval and flattened canals, which usually contain recesses unaffected by instruments operated in reaming motion. It is also used in anti-curvature preparation, where the instrument is used to file canal walls opposite to thinner areas (risk zone). This anti-curvature approach is especially recommended during coronal flaring procedures to preserve the furcation wall of the canal during chemomechanical preparation of molars. Filing motion should not be used for apical preparations as it results in preparations that are irregular in cross-section and predisposes the task to procedural accidents (e.g. ledges, blockages, and deviations) in curved canals.

## Apical limit of the preparation

One of the most controversial issues in root canal treatment is the definition of the ideal apical point where chemomechanical and filling procedures should terminate.27,174,175 This is an important discussion, since the periradicular tissue response to intracanal procedures in infected teeth is obviously influenced by the length to which they are restricted. While the termination point of root canal procedures appears to influence the development of postoperative pain mostly in the event of overinstrumentation/overfilling, the most significant

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 587 -->

effects (and controversies) are related to long-term treatment outcome.

As the anatomy of each root canal system is unique, any standardization attempt is prone to error. Therefore, the pathologic conditions of the root canal should also be taken into consideration in any discussion on working length (WL). In daily practice, clinicians usually face three basic diagnostic endodontic conditions requiring endodontic intervention – vital pulps, necrotic pulps, and retreatment (see Introduction to Section 2). Recognition of the differences between these conditions and clinical decision-making based on these differences is the basis on which the outcome of root canal treatment is founded. The major difference between these conditions resides in the fact that, while infection is absent in vital pulps, necrotic pulps and retreatment cases present a microbiologic challenge that should be treated appropriately. A diseased vital pulp is irreversibly inflamed but free of bacteria colonizing the root canal. Infection is mainly restricted to the surface of the pulp exposed to the oral cavity. As long as the radicular pulp remains vital, it usually succeeds in protecting itself from bacterial invasion and colonization.

Necrotic pulps and pulpless (treated) teeth, on the other hand, lack the essential defense apparatus needed to fight infection.
Consequently, they are characterized by the presence of primary intraradicular infection or persistent/secondary intraradicular infection.
The fact that a periradicular radiolucency is not visible on a radiograph does not necessarily mean that apical periodontitis is absent,26,46 and cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) studies have clearly confirmed that fact by revealing more apical periodontitis than periapical radiographs.75,155,156 Therefore, regardless of whether disease is radiographically detectable or not, it is advisable to treat root canals containing necrotic pulps as infected canals.
Treated teeth requiring retreatment because of posttreatment disease almost always harbor a persistent or secondary intraradicular infection, which can be more difficult to eradicate in well-treated canals.80,218,220

<!-- PDF page 588 -->

Several conditions associated with the presence of microbial infection have been shown to influence treatment, including the preoperative pulpal status,106,137,159,233,234 the presence of apical periodontitis,52,82,106,142,147,150,178,233,234 and the need for retreatment due to posttreatment disease.100,137,159,233 Based on these wellrecognized differences, the presence of infection should be the most important factor to consider when discussing the apical terminus of preparation procedures.

### Noninfected (vital) teeth

Absence of inflammation in the periradicular tissues is arguably the main histologic picture achieved in successfully treated teeth. From a clinical standpoint, however, success is better characterized as the absence of radiographically detectable apical periodontitis and the absence of signs and symptoms of infection such as pain, swelling, or draining sinus tract (Fig 12-10).81,96,233,244 Clinical and radiographic healing has been shown to correlate well with histologic healing in more recent studies. Ricucci et al176 evaluated the healing process in
51 root canal-treated human teeth after long observation periods, correlating histologic and radiographic observations. They reported that complete histologic healing was observed in the majority of the cases with radiographic healing, with no signs of acute or chronic inflammatory processes in the remaining apical or periradicular tissues. They concluded that the apical and periradicular tissues of properly treated teeth with no signs of periapical changes were only rarely significantly inflamed.

In teeth with vital pulps, some authors recommend that canals should be instrumented up to 1 mm short of the canal terminus in an attempt to preserve the vitality of the apical pulp tissue (the so-called
“pulp stump”), which might play a role in periradicular tissue repair.
Preservation of a healthy and vital pulp stump has been suggested to promote the occurrence of natural healing processes, most commonly apical closure by hard tissue formation (Fig 12-11), even when

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 589 -->

dentinal chips have been packed against the pulp stump.72,73,254

However, a histologic study of human teeth revealed that, while narrowing of the apical root canal by cementum was a common finding in most healed cases, total closure was never observed.176

Debris mixed with necrotic tissues and sealer particles was a common finding in the pulp stump.176

It must be appreciated that maintaining such a small tissue fragment in a healthy condition is not predictable, particularly during the instrumentation of curved narrow root canals. Moreover, irrigation with sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution in different concentrations may lead to severe inflammation or necrosis of the pulp stump141 as a result of cytotoxicity.154 Considering that asepsis is the decisive factor in preventing the development of disease when treating vital pulps, it could be argued that NaOCl irrigation should not be used in order to reduce the potential of damaging the pulp stump. Admittedly, keeping the root canal flooded with an antimicrobial agent such as
NaOCl during chemomechanical preparation may help to maintain asepsis during treatment. Furthermore, irritation to the periradicular tissues caused by NaOCl is usually transient and restricted to a small tissue area. Also, it usually induces no substantial adverse effects, provided the irrigant is prevented from extruding through the apical foramen.

It is salient to point out that tissue repair after the treatment of vital pulps is accomplished by cells and molecules of the periodontal ligament (Fig 12-12). This is a soft, specialized connective tissue ranging in width from 0.15 to 0.38 mm, with its thinnest portion located around the middle third of the root.140 Fibroblasts are the predominant cells in the periodontal ligament, which also contains osteoblasts and osteoclasts (lining the bone surface of the ligament), cementoblasts (lining the cementum surface of the ligament), epithelial cells, macrophages, and undifferentiated mesenchymal cells. The extracellular matrix is composed primarily of collagen fiber bundles embedded in a ground substance consisting of glycosaminoglycans, glycoproteins, and glycolipids. Constituents of the periodontal ligament are constantly being synthesized, removed,

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 590 -->

and replaced. Fibroblasts, the main cells responsible for the matrix turnover, are able to simultaneously synthesize and degrade collagen in a continuous remodeling process.140 The high turnover is not exclusive to the extracellular matrix; cells are frequently renewed as well. The periodontal ligament is highly vascularized, which is fundamental to the high rate of turnover of its cellular and extracellular constituents (see Fig 12-12). The total vascular volume is about 20%, which is much higher than that in most other body tissues (3% to 4%) or the dental pulp (14%).135,269 Its main blood supply comes from the posterior superior alveolar and infraorbital arteries in the maxilla, and the inferior alveolar artery and its branches in the mandible. The ligament also possesses an efficient lymph drainage system. As the periodontal ligament has a high turnover rate, it can easily adapt to changing local conditions.23 All these features culminate in an excellent regeneration ability of the periodontal ligament in response to injury.

Maintenance of the vitality of the pulp stump during chemomechanical procedures is unpredictable207,217 and is apparently not crucial for periradicular tissue healing. Studies have demonstrated that mechanical removal of the pulp stump by instrumentation at or beyond the apical foramen does not preclude repair by hard tissue formation, particularly when calcium hydroxide is used as the intracanal medication or is present in the root canal sealer.103,118,144,235 In addition, studies in dogs25,105,238 revealed that the enlargement of the apical foramen with the consequent removal of the pulp stump was followed by periradicular tissue ingrowth into the root canals, sometimes associated with the deposition of cementumlike tissue over the canal walls (Fig 12-13). This confirms the healing potential of the periodontal ligament, which is conceivably a consequence of its intense metabolic activity. In the clinical setting, enlargement of the apical foramen is an undesirable and unnecessary procedure that can result in a lack of apical control during obturation and predisposes the patient to postoperative pain.

The apical limit of the root canal procedure does not appear to have a substantial effect on the treatment outcome of teeth with vital pulps, provided bacteria are prevented from gaining entry into the root canal before filling.239 Obviously, gross overfilling should be avoided as it usually results in postoperative pain. The recommended
WL in teeth with vital pulps is 1 to 2 mm short of the radiographic apex. The use of apical patency files has been advocated to clean the apical foramen and keep it free of debris without enlarging it. The price of using patency files in such cases may be the removal or damage of the pulp stump. The reasons for maintaining a patent apical foramen in teeth with vital pulps are almost exclusively based on mechanical factors. For example, this is said to prevent apical blockage with dentinal debris that could lead to a loss of apical control and result in procedural errors during chemomechanical preparation, particularly in curved and narrow root canals. From a biologic perspective, however, it seems that it does not matter whether or not the pulp stump is removed using small patency files, since the apical periodontal ligament is the main entity responsible for periradicular tissue healing.

### Infected teeth (necrotic pulp and retreatment cases)

Root canals containing necrotic pulp tissue associated (or not) with apical periodontitis as well as root canal-treated teeth with posttreatment disease are a different matter entirely due to infection.
In most of these cases, bacteria reach the apical part of the canal and can be located near or at the apical foramen and at the apical ramifications, in close contact with the periradicular tissues.83,179,180,224 The length of instrumentation in infected cases is critical. It is reasonable to assume that it would be preferable to clean the canal to its terminus. Nevertheless, the risks of

instrumenting the canal to this position include the possibility of overinstrumentation, which can force infected debris and previous filling materials into the periradicular tissues (Fig 12-14).

One condition for bacteria to participate in the pathogenesis of apical periodontitis is that they be spatially located in the root canal system in such a way that they or their virulence factors can gain access to the periradicular tissues.219 This region is the apical root canal. The microbiota colonizing the very apical part of the root canal can exhibit a high species diversity, dominance of anaerobes, and significant individual-to-individual variation, and may reach densities as high as 106 bacterial cells at the apical 5 mm of the canal.7,20,184,227,231 Chapter 5 provides more details on the apical endodontic microbiota.

The apical part of the root canal can be regarded as “critical territory” for pathogenic bacteria, the host, and the clinician.227 This region is critical for bacteria because it affords close contact with the periradicular tissues, from which the bacteria can obtain nutrients and to which they can inflict damage. It is also critical to the host because the host defenses must concentrate in this area and wall off the bacteria in order to prevent the infection from spreading. It is critical for the clinician because the treatment outcome will depend on how

effective treatment procedures are in eradicating the infection and promoting an effective antibacterial seal in this area.

Given its strategic anatomical position and complex anatomy, the apical portion of the root canal system can be considered the most critical element of the whole system with regard to the need for cleaning, disinfection, and sealing (Fig 12-15).216 As the number of bacterial cells remaining in the apical portion that can be managed by the host defenses is largely unknown (this will certainly vary according to community composition), the chemomechanical procedures should ideally extend no shorter than the apical level of the infection (Fig 12-16). However, the apical level of the infection may be anywhere in the apical canal9,177,246 and is impossible to

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 600 -->

determine clinically (Fig 12-17). Consequently, it seems prudent to extend the apical limit of instrumentation to the canal terminus in an attempt to eliminate or at least significantly reduce the bacterial counts before the filling is placed.

Instrumentation of infected root canals produces bacteria-laden dentinal debris that may be packed in the apical canal or extruded into the periradicular tissues. When packed in the apical canal, dentinal debris may reduce the WL and compromise tissue healing because it contains bacteria.104 Infected dentinal debris that is extruded into the periradicular tissues may also be responsible for persistent periradicular inflammation and consequent treatment failure.181,285

To effectively clean and disinfect the most apical part of root canals, the use of patency files that reach or even pass through the apical foramen has been proposed. The apical patency concept is based on the placement of a small file (size no. 10 or 15) up to 1 mm longer than the canal terminus in an attempt to remove dentinal debris from the apical portion of the canal.36 It is recommended that this small file be passively moved through the apical constriction without widening it.

Clinical experience demonstrates that patency with small files can be very useful in maintaining control of the WL. This is because the use of patency files ensures that the apical foramen remains unblocked and patent, and when used repeatedly, tends to prevent accumulation of pulpal and dentinal debris that can cause blockages and other complications. It has been shown that the use of patency files improves the delivery of irrigants to the apical canal.260

The rationale for the establishment and maintenance of apical patency during preparation of infected cases is based on biologic and mechanical factors. From a mechanical point of view, using the instrument to prepare a root canal to its terminus and repeatedly advancing it to that point theoretically reduces the risks of procedural accidents. This is because this technique prevents or reduces the accumulation of debris in this area. Such accumulation may lead to blockage of the apical root canal, resulting in loss of the WL.

Ideally, there is no apparent reason to extend the use of patency files beyond the confines of the canal. Reaching the canal terminus would appear to be sufficient to perform the effects expected of a patency file. Even so, patency files are likely to pass through the apical foramen in many cases because of the inherent difficulties in establishing the precise location of the canal terminus clinically. If small instruments are used, it is entirely unlikely that they might cause significant mechanical damage to the periradicular tissues. In infected root canals, instruments could be subject to bacterial contamination when inserted up to the patency length. Thus, patency files could theoretically carry bacteria from the canal to the periradicular tissues.
However, an in vitro study111 analyzing the effectiveness of 5.25%
NaOCl in preventing inoculation of the periradicular tissues with contaminated patency instruments demonstrated that the NaOCl present in the canal after irrigation was sufficient to kill the test bacteria used to contaminate the instrument.

Studies showed that this did not have any apparent effect on the development of postoperative pain when using patency instruments.229,251 Systematic reviews and metaanalyses revealed that apical patency procedures do not influence the incidence of postoperative pain, flare-ups, and the use of analgesics.2,282 These findings indicate that apical extrusion induced by patency files may not be of clinical relevance, provided small instruments are used gently and passively.

### Establishing the working length

In his classic study, Kuttler115 found that the root canal usually narrows toward the apex and then expands to form the apical foramen. The narrowest part of the canal comprises the apical constriction, which is located just short of the apical foramen (Fig 1221). This area is generally believed to be situated at the CDJ, and its distance to the apical foramen varies from 0.5 to 1 mm in individuals of different ages.69,115,242 In more than 60% of root canals, the apical foramen is not located at the root apex, and the distance between the apical foramen and the radiographic apex ranges from 0 to 3

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 609 -->

mm.115,276 Kuttler115 reported that the mean apex-to-foramen distance was 0.48 mm in young persons and 0.6 mm in older subjects. Dummer et al69 reported that the mean apex-to-foramen distance in anterior teeth was 0.36 mm.

Traditionally, the CDJ has been considered the ideal termination point for root canal procedures.93,166 However, the greatest problem with using this area as the terminal landmark for endodontic procedures is that the CDJ is very often impossible to detect clinically, even by experienced practitioners,206 because it is not necessarily the most constricted area of the root canal. The apical foramen could also be a useful landmark, but it is also difficult and often impossible to locate it clinically and radiographically because the position of the apical foramen frequently does not coincide with the root apex.115 To complicate matters further, it is virtually impossible to identify the apical foramen on radiographs when it exits to the side of the root in a buccal or lingual direction. Consequently, it is difficult or even impossible to determine the exact site where the root canal ends using currently available methods.27 In addition to anatomical variability, there may be sclerosis of the apical root canal, or the apical constriction may have been modified or lost due to apical root resorption resulting from the inflammatory periradicular disease process.243

The root apex is widely used as a reference for the determination of the WL because it is usually visible radiographically. However, even though it is commonly recommended to make the termination point
0.5 to 1 mm short of the radiographic apex, this remains only an estimate. It can be argued that instrumenting and filling to an arbitrary point short of the apex is not treating the root canal system in its entirety. However, if infection is absent (i.e. if the teeth are vital), this may not be necessary. On the other hand, an infected root canal should ideally be instrumented to its entirety. Thus, the astute clinician should take advantage of the advanced methods available to increase the chances of more accurately assessing where the root canal terminates.

Radiographs have been widely used for WL determination. Other methods have also been proposed such as tactile sensation and the paper point technique,37 but they are imprecise, unreliable, empirical, and fraught with limitations. Over the last two decades, electronic length measurement devices have evolved significantly and have been widely used to determine the root canal terminus with a satisfactory degree of accuracy and reliability.91,129,205 A long-term retrospective study found that the use of an electronic apex locator to determine the WL in infected root canals associated with apical periodontitis resulted in a high rate of healing.138 Commercially available electronic apex locators have been reported to be accurate to within +0.5 mm of the apical foramen in 90% to 100% of cases, irrespective of the pulpal diagnosis.57,91,127,130,168,211,267 After electronic location, a radiograph should be taken to confirm the measurements. In fact, the combined use of electronic apex locators and radiographs of a trial instrument has been shown to be more accurate than the use of radiographs alone.35,79 Due to their accuracy, apex locators reduce the number of radiographs needed to determine the WL, particularly in teeth where the apex is difficult to visualize on radiographs. ## When preparation is complete

Cleaning and disinfection of the canal cannot be measured reliably enough to establish the end point of preparation. Some criteria have been proposed such as the attainment of clean dentinal filings, clean irrigating solution when collected in a gauze, the tactile feeling of glassy and smooth walls, and canal enlargement 2 to 4 file sizes beyond the first file to bind. However, none of these indicators is reliable. In fact, canals containing tissue remnants and residual bacteria can meet virtually all these criteria. Therefore, in the absence of an accurate method, chemomechanical procedures should be considered complete when the canal is enlarged to diameters that are compatible with the root anatomy and large enough to be safe and antibacterially effective.

## Apical width of the preparation

As discussed in Chapter 11, the apical width of the preparation could be as important as the apical limit with regard to the treatment outcome of teeth with apical periodontitis. While the size of the apical preparation may not be so critical for success in vital noninfected teeth, there is sound clinical rationale as well as ex vivo and in vivo evidence indicating that larger apical preparations may improve the treatment outcome of teeth with apical periodontitis. Preparations should be sufficiently large to incorporate more anatomical irregularities and remove more infected dentin, allowing for the removal of more bacterial cells from the root canal. Pérez et al160

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 612 -->

reported that after each increase in the apical preparation size, the amount of prepared canal surfaces increased significantly (Fig 1222). Studies have shown that the larger the apical preparation of infected root canals, the greater the reduction of the bacterial populations45,59,149,186,187,223 and the cleaner the apical canal segment.4,258 Consequently, larger preparations have also been associated with an improved treatment outcome (Fig 12-23).8,197

Although there are no consistent longitudinal outcome studies on this subject, it is undeniable that better disinfection improves the prognosis. Thus, large preparation may be regarded as an important part of the overall strategy to enhance bacterial elimination from the root canal.

Traditionally, the shape of the canal at its apical terminus is said to be circular. However, in many cases, the apical terminus is irregular, oval, or flattened in shape.126,134,275 In these teeth, the canal crosssectional shape has a maximum and a minimum diameter, and the first instrument used for apical preparation will bind to the latter. For all the canal walls to be properly reached and cleaned, enlargement of the apical segment should exceed the largest diameter of the original canal. This allows for incorporation of all root canal walls in the final shape (Fig 12-24). This follows the concept of giving a round shape to canals that are irregular in cross-section. Unfortunately, this is not feasible in most teeth because it would require overenlargement,5 with increased risks of causing deviations, perforations, or weakening of the root.

Obviously, enlargement of infected canals should be compatible with the tooth anatomy to avoid procedural errors. The type of instrument used is of great relevance. In the recent past, apical instrumentation was restricted to small instrument sizes in an attempt to minimize the occurrence of accidents when using stainless steel files to prepare curved canals. This rationale is related to the physical limitations of stainless steel instruments, especially the significantly reduced flexibility of files larger than nos. 25 or 30. The recommendation to limit apical enlargement to these file sizes has nothing to do with the minimum sizes needed to promote cleaning and

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 613 -->

disinfection, but rather to do with the largest sizes beyond which the risk of accidents such as ledges and perforations would increase significantly.

With the advent of much more flexible NiTi instruments, curved root canals can be enlarged to sizes previously unimaginable for stainless steel files, with a lower risk of procedural accidents.4,18,108,110,162,165,213,236 The low modulus of elasticity of NiTi alloy used in endodontics (1/4 to 1/5 that of stainless steel) gives these instruments the property of superelasticity. The load needed to bend a no. 45 NiTi instrument is equivalent to the load required to bend a no. 25 stainless steel file of the same design.210

When planning the size of apical instrumentation, the clinician should take into account important aspects such as the type of instrument used, the root volume, the anatomy and mean initial diameter of the apical canal, and the presence of curvature.
Preparation of the root canal in the apical segment must be large enough to increase cleaning and disinfection. At the same time, it must be compatible with the root anatomy so as to avoid overpreparation and consequent ledge formation or perforation. Care must also be taken to avoid over-enlargement of the coronal segment of the canal, which would lead to unnecessary weakening of the root and consequent predisposition to fracture.

### Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl)

During World War I, Dakin58 proposed the widespread use of 0.5% to
0.6% NaOCl solution for the disinfection of open and infected wounds. Based on this report, NaOCl was recommended as an endodontic irrigant by Coolidge in 1919.56 In 1936, Walker270

introduced double-strength chlorinated soda solution (5% NaOCl) as a root canal irrigant. NaOCl has since been used in endodontic practice worldwide, and no other root canal irrigant has been found to be more effective with regard to its cleaning and disinfecting properties.289

NaOCl has broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and rapidly kills vegetative and spore-forming bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and viruses.71,195 Most oral bacteria are killed within a short time of contact with NaOCl.145,264 Enzymes containing cysteine have side chains terminating in sulfhydryl groups. Such enzymes cannot function unless the sulfhydryl groups remain free and reduced. NaOCl exerts its antibacterial effect by inducing the irreversible oxidation of sulfhydryl groups of essential bacterial enzymes, resulting in the formation of disulfide bonds and the consequent disruption of the metabolic activity of bacterial cells.34,71 NaOCl can also have deleterious effects on bacterial DNA by inducing the formation of chlorinated derivatives of nucleotide bases. NaOCl has also been reported to disrupt membrane-associated activities.107,131

Hypochlorites are salts of the hypochlorite ion (OCl-). The sodium salt produces an aqueous solution, and the calcium salt is a solid.195

In aqueous solution, NaOCl ionizes to produce Na+ and OCl-, which establishes an equilibrium with hypochlorous acid (HOCl).131 HOCl is the main active moiety responsible for the antibacterial effectiveness of hypochlorites, and the effect of undissolved HOCl is higher than that of OCl-.131 The pH of the solution determines which of the two forms will prevail. HOCl predominates between pH 4 and 7, while
OCl- predominates above pH 9. Therefore, as the pH increases, more OCl- is formed, and the antibacterial activity consequently decreases. Disinfection by hypochlorites is optimal at around pH 6 because the concentration of HOCl is optimal and its dissociation is minimal.195 The NaOCl solutions commonly used in endodontics are manufactured at pH 12 for increased chemical stability, which means that all of the chlorine is present in the form of OCl-.200,289

### Chlorhexidine (CHX)

Chlorhexidine (CHX) is widely used as an antimicrobial substance in antiseptic products, particularly in hand wash and oral rinse solutions, but also as a disinfectant and preservative.131 This cationic bisbiguanide compound is highly effective against several Grampositive and Gram-negative oral bacterial species as well as yeasts (Fig 12-27).145,241,249 CHX also has antimicrobial substantivity in dentin114,188 and causes low irritation to living tissues.247,283 Due to these properties,
CHX has emerged as an irrigant and interappointment medication used in endodontic treatment of teeth with apical periodontitis.16,136

CHX is bacteriostatic at low concentrations and bactericidal at high concentrations.66 Although it can induce damage to the outer microbial cell layers, this effect is usually insufficient to cause lysis or cell death. CHX crosses the cell wall, presumably by passive diffusion, and subsequently attacks the cytoplasmic membrane.
Damage to this delicate membrane is followed by leakage of intracellular constituents.131 At high concentrations, CHX causes precipitation of intracellular constituents, particularly phosphated entities such as adenosine triphosphate and nucleic acids.66 As a consequence, the cytoplasm becomes congealed, resulting in a decrease in leakage.131

Aqueous CHX solutions are most stable within the pH range of 5 to 8. The antimicrobial activity of CHX is pH-dependent, and its optimum range of pH 5.5 to 7 is greatly reduced or abolished in the presence of organic matter.131 Hydroxyapatite has little or no inhibitory effect on CHX,171 whereas dentin matrix170 and bovine serum albumin171 have been shown to inhibit its activity significantly.

Diluted CHX solutions can be stored at room temperature and their expected shelf life is at least 1 year in suitable packaging.
Prolonged exposure to high temperature or light should be avoided.

### Access preparation and canal negotiation

Regardless of the instrumentation technique used, preparation of an adequate access cavity determines the success of subsequent chemomechanical procedures (Fig 12-33). In conventional access preparations, the pulp chamber must be unroofed completely so as to provide the clinician with a straight-line access to the orifice of each canal. Although minimally invasive access cavities have been proposed with different shapes and terminologies (e.g. contracted,
“ninja,” and “truss” access), there is currently no scientific evidence that they are of any benefit, not even in terms of improving tooth resistance to fracture.189,215 Actually, root canal shaping and disinfection can be negatively affected when minimally invasive access cavities are prepared.15,266

There are several special burs for the preparation of access cavities. However, a common and very effective approach consists of using a round bur of an appropriate size in a high-speed handpiece to penetrate and unroof the pulp chamber, then using a tapered bur such as EndoZ (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) or diamond burs to flare the access cavity walls adjacent to the canal orifices.
Next, a stable reference point is selected, and the canal orifices are located using an endodontic explorer.

### Engine-driven rotary instrumentation

Studies assessing the diametric dimensions of apical anatomy have repeatedly demonstrated that the buccolingual diameter is usually greater than the mesiodistal diameter; most canals are predominantly ovoid throughout, not round (Fig 12-36).5,275 The most inherent technical flaw, which is the use of round files of any design conformation to clean an ovoid canal configuration, manifests as the failure to debride a substantial amount of the canal contents. Peters et al164 showed that the mean (± standard deviation) untreated areas ranged from 59.6% (± 14.9%) to 79.9% (± 10.3%) for the total canal length, and 65.2% (18.7%) to 74.7% (17.2%) for the apical canal portion, respectively.

Following the introduction of NiTi alloy for manufacturing endodontic instruments and their excellent performance in laboratory and clinical studies, a large variety of engine-driven NiTi rotary instruments with different designs have become commercially available. NiTi rotary instruments are generally designed to be used in

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 644 -->

continuous clockwise rotation obtained through air-driven or electric motors usually operated at a low speed (150 to 600 rpm) and low torque (0.1 to 5 Ncm). The newest thermal-treated NiTi files that are programed to be close to a full martensenic phase can be sped up from 1,000 to 2,500 rpm. This is the case with the HyFlex, RaCe
EVO, and XP-endo files. Low-speed handpieces on standard dental units have speeds ranging from 5,000 to 20,000 rpm and a working torque of about 2.5 Ncm, compared with speeds of 380,000 to
450,000 rpm and torques of 0.1 to 0.5 Ncm in high-speed handpieces. In most electric motors available for use in NiTi rotary instrumentation, speed and torque may be programmed by the clinician and/or preset by the manufacturer. Many electric motors are also available with programs for instruments that are not used in continuous clockwise rotation but in reciprocating motion.

NiTi alloy has indisputably led to the manufacturing of reliable rotary instruments with improved capabilities for shaping root canals.
However, rotary instruments may be prone to a higher risk of fracture during instrumentation.61,87 Before stainless steel instruments fracture, they usually become deformed, exhibiting severe bending or the unwinding of flutes. NiTi instruments, on the other hand, can fracture without any visible signs of deformation. Thus, visual inspection is not a reliable method for evaluating the physical integrity of NiTi rotary instruments.172

Fracture of NiTi rotary instruments occurs either by torsional stress or cyclic flexural fatigue.68 Torsional fracture occurs when the tip of the instrument is locked in the canal while the shaft continues to rotate. Cyclic fatigue occurs when an instrument rotates within its elastic limit in a curved canal. In the latter situation, every bent portion of the instrument is subjected to mechanical loading, represented by alternate compressive and tensile stresses. The continuous repetition of such stresses leads to the low cyclic fatigue of the instrument.

Cyclic fatigue resistance refers to the number of cycles an instrument is able to resist under specific loading conditions. Since the number of cycles to fracture is cumulative, it can be calculated by multiplying the rotational speed by the time elapsed until fatigue

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 645 -->

fracture occurs.77,151,281 In contrast to stainless steel instruments, which develop cyclic fatigue after only a few cycles, NiTi instruments can withstand several hundred flexural cycles before fracturing.

Astute clinicians must follow appropriate measures to reduce the risk of instrument fracture. In order to do so, they must be aware of the factors associated with the risk of instrument fracture. Instrumentrelated factors that affect the torsional and bending properties of rotary instruments include size, taper, and flute depth.48,257,277

Regarding size and taper, small files with reduced taper are usually more susceptible to torsional fracture but more resistant to cyclic fatigue. This contrasts with files with greater taper and larger diameters, which are generally more resistant to torsional load but more prone to cyclic fatigue. Finally, instruments with shallow flutes, evenly tapered shafts, and consistently shaped cross-sectional areas are more resistant to fracture because the torsional and bending stresses that develop during use are evenly distributed along the entire length of these instruments.

Other factors also influence the incidence of fracture. Rotary instruments used in low-torque motors (< 1 Ncm) are more resistant to fracture than those used in high-torque motors (> 3 Ncm) during preparation.84 The position of curvature also influences the risk of fracture: coronal curvature has a higher risk than apical curvature. In addition, the risk of fracture for an instrument rotating in a curved canal increases as the curvature angle increases and the curvature radius decreases.98,172

The evolution of NiTi instrumentation manufacture has persisted with a round core blank, regardless of whether it has been ground, twisted, coated, heated, or metallurgically reformulated. NiTi files are superelastic and able to self-center, avoid apical transportation and, with appropriate taper selection, prevent thinning of the coronal third and the danger of inner curvature on the middle third of the root, which might result in weakening or strip perforation. They are, however, unable to clean most of the intracanal space effectively.
Moreover, regardless of design configurations with a variable tip or variable taper on a single file, they are unable to adequately clean the

## Smear layer

The smear layer is a layer of debris composed of organic and inorganic material that is always formed on the canal walls after dentin is cut with endodontic instruments (Fig 12-44). It has an amorphous, irregular, and granular appearance under a scanning electron microscope.128,209,268 In addition to mineralized collagen particles generated during dentinal cutting, the smear layer may also contain pulp remnants and bacterial cells.250 The smear layer covering the instrumented canal walls is approximately 1- to 2-μm thick and can also be observed in some regions as plugs packed into the dentinal tubules to depths of up to 40 μm (“smear plugs”).124

The reasons for removal of the smear layer are as follows:

Enhances and expedites diffusion, thus enhancing antibacterial activity of intracanal medicaments within dentinal tubules.148

Promotes the adaptation of filling materials to the root canal dentinal walls, increasing their sealing ability.24,198,212
