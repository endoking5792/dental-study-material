## Contemporary goals and means of root canal

## treatment

Apical periodontitis (AP) is defined as an oral, biofilm-induced, inflammatory disease caused by a reaction of the immune system of the host to the presence of microorganisms or their products, close to, within, or outside the root canal system.69 Since the goal of root canal treatment is to prevent or heal AP, eradication of the microorganisms that have caused the disease in the first place has been traditionally considered to be the ultimate treatment outcome.
However, contemporary views on biofilm disinfection, together with the long-held acknowledgment that the root canal system is an anatomical niche impossible to sterilize once it becomes infected, have shifted the treatment perspective from the traditional concept of bacterial eradication toward the theory of healing-conducive bacterial load reduction.154 This mind shift has profoundly influenced the philosophy of contemporary root canal treatment, with mechanical root canal instrumentation being currently considered to be the primary means to ensure proper irrigant delivery.

### Root canal treatment based on a noninstrumentation

### technique

A noninstrumentation technique to remove microorganisms from the root canal system and prevent new (in)growth of microorganisms would be ideal. Such a technique would avoid instrumentation of the root canal, which is associated with disadvantages such as the smear layer and dentinal debris production, instrumentation failures, weakening of the root structure, and apical crack formation.64,94,126,134,148,182 This problem was recognized by Lussi et al,101 who introduced a device for root canal cleaning and filling without instrumentation. Despite the promising (in vitro and in vivo)

Establishing an alternating negative and positive pressure that would enable an effective irrigation procedure without instrumentation and extrusion of the irrigant seemed not to be feasible at that time.
Nowadays, the concept of the noninstrumentation technique has resurfaced with the launch of the GentleWave System (Sonendo,

Although the basic principles of GentleWave share similarities with the noninstrumentation technique, it needs to be stressed that it is an apparatus developed only for the delivery of root canal irrigants and not a means to perform a noninstrumentationbased root canal treatment.118

### Space for irrigation

As mentioned above, creating space by mechanically carving the root canal system in order to deliver disinfecting solutions or medicaments into it is still inevitable. When the root canal system is infected, root canal instrumentation by-products such as dentinal debris and the smear layer will also be infected. The smear layer can be defined as a mixture of dentinal debris, remnants of pulp tissue, odontoblastic processes, and microorganisms (if present). This layer is strongly attached to the root canal wall and can even penetrate up to 40 μm into the dentinal tubules.148 Dentinal debris may be defined as dentin chips, tissue remnants, and particles attached to the root canal wall or present in the root canal.126 Both dentinal debris and the smear layer will chemically inactivate the root canal medicaments and irrigants and mechanically block their access to the biofilm.68 It has been clearly shown that the production of dentinal debris and the subsequent blockage of isthmuses is a bigger problem than it was once assumed to be.125,142 After the first instrument is used in the root canal, the wall will be covered with an infected smear layer at the sites where the file touched the canal wall. At these sites, the biofilm is mechanically disrupted but also merged with the smear layer and therefore not completely removed. At the sites where files

### Mechanical and chemical aspects of irrigation

The smear layer, dentinal debris, and untouched biofilm can only be removed by irrigation. For an effective irrigation procedure, both the mechanical detachment of pulp tissue, dentinal debris, and the smear layer (instrumentation products), microorganisms (planktonic or biofilm) and their products (later referred to in this chapter as substrate) from the root canal wall and their removal from the root canal system as well as the chemical dissolution or disruption are important. Irrigant flow is the common denominator for both the mechanical and chemical aspects of irrigation. Especially for the latter, root canal irrigants, for instance, sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), are known to become chemically inactive after reacting with the dentin, microorganisms, or biofilm; tissue remnants;35,67,68,167 or irrigants themselves.144 Therefore, irrigant refreshment or mixing with fresh irrigant becomes crucial for maintaining the constancy of its chemical action. This depends on the fluid flow and thus on the mechanical aspect of the irrigation procedure. Without flow (also termed convection), the irrigant would have to be delivered throughout the root canal system by diffusion, which is the result of the random movement of individual particles (molecules/ions) in the fluid. This process is slow and depends strongly on temperature and concentration gradients. Convection is a faster and more efficient transportation mechanism, during which molecules are transported by the motion of fluid.75 This brings about an effective delivery, refreshment, and mixing of the irrigant. Convection can be described and estimated by the (second) Damköhler number, which is defined as the ratio of the typical irrigant transport time to the reaction time.a

The reaction rate of an irrigant with biofilm, pulp tissue, or the root canal wall (the dentin and smear layer) is important to predict its chemical effect. ### Confinement and anatomy

It is important to realize that root canal irrigation takes place in a confined space with a complex anatomical configuration containing isthmuses, oval extensions, and lateral canals. Furthermore, the root canal is surrounded by dentin, which has a porous structure because it contains microchannels and tubules with a diameter of 0.5 to 3.2
μm and a density of 103 to 104 tubules/mm2.129 These structures are expected to be filled with biofilms in cases of teeth associated with
AP, at least up to a certain depth.155 Ideally, proper disinfection demands the delivery of the disinfecting solution into all these anatomical areas during the irrigation process. Pragmatically, however, root canal system configuration and dentin structure dramatically hamper the effectiveness of the irrigation procedure and the efficacy of the disinfection. Consequently, complete biofilm removal will not be possible.

### Aim and objectives of root canal irrigation

The aim of root canal irrigation is to create a fluid flow dynamic to the full extent of the root canal system, which will fulfill the following objectives:

Bring the irrigant into close contact with the substrate, carry away the substrate, and provide lubrication for the instruments.
Ensure an adequate delivery throughout the root canal system as well as refreshment and mixing of the irrigant; this will compensate for the rapid inactivation of the irrigant by keeping stable an effective concentration of the active chemical component(s).
Ensure a force on the root canal wall (wall shear stress) sufficient enough to detach/disrupt the substrate.
Prevent irrigant extrusion toward the periapical tissues.

During the irrigation procedure, two phases can be distinguished: a flow phase, during which the irrigant is delivered and flows in and out of the root canal; and a rest phase, where the irrigant is at (relative) rest in the root canal. For the flow phase to occur, the irrigant needs an energy boost. For example, during syringe irrigation the plunger will be pushed downward, causing movement of the irrigant fluid, while during laser activated irrigation (LAI), cavitation will provide the energy boost needed. Each irrigation system works on its own principles, thereby creating its unique energy boost. Irrigant activation systems introduce an additional activation phase, enhancing the streaming of the irrigant by an energy source.

This chapter aims to:

Present the basic principles of the fluid dynamics developed during irrigant flow.
Discuss how biofilm structure and viscoelastic behavior affect irrigant flow.
Present the operational characteristics of the different irrigation systems available, discuss the fluid flow dynamics developed, and provide clinical recommendations associated with the respective irrigation systems.

Research investigating smear layer removal during irrigation is not reviewed in this chapter as the authors are of the opinion that the reliability of the methodology applied to address this topic is highly debatable.105

## Fluid dynamics and irrigant flow

The mechanical efficacy of root canal irrigation is inextricably linked to the characteristics of the irrigant flow developed during irrigation.
Consequently, the in-depth study of the physics of irrigant flow and the associated phenomena generated during irrigation is of the

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 695 -->

utmost importance. Fluid dynamics is the study of fluids in motion, that is to say, the study of liquids and gases that have the ability to flow. It is a branch of fluid mechanics that is applied in a wide range of scientific disciplines such as in weather forecasting and traffic engineering. Particularly in endodontics, it helps to describe and analyze the flow characteristics of a flowing irrigant.

### Laminar and turbulent flow

Flow is used to describe the physical properties of the movement of a liquid. During a steady flow, the movement of a fluid does not change over time. A smooth flow, i.e. a flow of liquid wherein the liquid particles follow regular trajectories is known as laminar flow. On the contrary, during an unsteady flow, the flow properties change over time. A flow of liquid wherein the liquid particles follow irregular trajectories is known as turbulent flow. Turbulent flow contains a chaotic, nonlinear motion. The Reynolds number (Re) is used to make a distinction between laminar or turbulent flow and is calculated based on the fluid density (ρ), viscosity (μ), velocity scale (ν), and length scale (D).b

A low Re is related to laminar flow, whereas a high Re indicates a more turbulent flow, including eddies and vortices. An eddy is a fluid current with a flow direction that differs from that of the general flow, and a vortex shows a circular movement pattern. The irrigant flow during root canal irrigation will be influenced greatly by the root canal system because its boundaries will ultimately confine the flow.
Viscous flow is also considered to be laminar flow. For viscous flow to occur, a force must be exerted on the substrate (e.g. a fluid) in order to maintain the motion of the fluid particles, a motion originally impeded by the internal frictional resistance to flow. This resistance is called viscosity. Biofilm can show the behavior of viscous flow, which is discussed later in this chapter.

Syringe irrigation using a 30 G needle and with low flow rates (volume of irrigant delivered over time; in endodontics, this is best expressed in mLs), i.e. in the order of 0.01 mL/s, creates a steady

### Acoustic streaming

Ultrasonic and laser activation of the irrigant induce a special kind of flow called acoustic streaming, which is a phenomenon that was already introduced in 1884 by Lord Rayleigh.95 The induction of acoustic streaming by an oscillating cylinder to remove biomaterial from a surface was first described by Williams and Nyborg in
1970.180,181

Acoustic streaming, when induced by an ultrasonically oscillating instrument, consists of two flows superimposed on one another (Fig
13-1). One part of the streaming is oscillatory and the other is steady. The strength of both flows depends on the oscillation amplitude of the file. Ahmad et al4 used the theory of acoustic streaming to describe ultrasonic activation in endodontics, taking into account only the steady part of the flow. Nonetheless, it has been demonstrated that the oscillatory flow also contributes significantly to the overall streaming.171

The oscillatory part of acoustic streaming makes the flow oscillate back and forth together with the file, with a velocity equal to that of the oscillating file. However, the velocity decreases quadratically as the distance from the file increases. This renders the oscillatory flow important only at the vicinity of the file. The oscillating fluid exerts an alternating pressure and shear stress on (the biomaterial on) the root canal wall, reaching 100 kPa and 1 kPa, respectively.171 The values for the pressure and the shear stress are similar to those reported for syringe irrigation at a very high flow rate (0.26 mL/s) and near the outlet of the needle.18 For ultrasonic activation, these forces are markedly present around every antinode of the instrument (see section below entitled “Ultrasonic activation” for further details).

### Cavitation

Transient cavitation is defined as the growth and collapse of vaporfilled cavities (bubbles) due to local fluctuations in the fluid pressure.22,139 It is associated with surface cleaning,53,83,109 medical therapy, surface erosion,52,164 and other mechanical effects.109

During stable cavitation, the gas-filled bubbles may be stable for a long time. Indisputably, cavitation can play an important role during the irrigation procedure, but unfortunately it is still poorly understood and often misused in endodontics. Cavitation can be induced by a dynamic flow of the irrigant (e.g. during ultrasonic activation) or it can induce the flow of the irrigant itself, which occurs during LAI.

### Stable cavitation

A pressure field induced by an ultrasonically oscillating file or shock waves produced by laser energy can cause the stable cavitation bubbles to oscillate or implode.170

The largest oscillation of these bubbles can be achieved when they are driven at resonance with the ultrasound. The approximation in water is provided in footnote ‘d’.22

The behavior of the stable bubbles is time-dependent, as stable bubbles dissolve slowly over time. That is why the resting time between ultrasonic activations or laser pulsations may be important.81

Stable cavitation bubbles do not automatically participate in the cleaning process. Notably, they may also block the entrance of lateral anatomical features of the root canal system, as is reported for ultrasonic activation (Fig 13-2).

### Transient cavitation

The surface cleaning potential of transient cavitation makes cavitation interesting for root canal cleaning. In non-pure water (tap water, distilled water), there are often tiny pockets of entrapped gas (cavitation nuclei) within the bulk volume or on the surfaces of particles in the liquid. These are initiation points from which bubbles can grow, followed by a fast collapse (a process called heterogeneous cavitation).

For ultrasonic activation of the irrigant, the life cycle of a bubble is determined by the oscillating pressure of the ultrasound, and for LAI by the length, energy, and frequency of the pulse.97,98 The bubbles can grow when the pressure drops from ambient pressure to below the vapor pressure of the liquid (103 Pa for water)e.22

In water, the velocity threshold is around 15 m/s, which can be achieved with the current endodontic ultrasonic and laser devices, but not with sonic devices.79,102,103,151 Small bubbles always collapse against a nearby solid, hard wall. Interestingly, when bubble collapse occurs next to a soft substrate (such as a biofilm covering a wall), the soft material might be pulled from the wall toward the bubble.25 Highvelocity jets (hundreds of meters per second) and shock waves22,174

have been reported in the literature during the bubble collapse, and these jets lead locally to pressures in the order of 1 GPa and shear stresses of 1 MPa.52 These small bubbles may therefore further enhance cleaning of the root canal walls.

The structural backbone of a biofilm is the self-produced extracellular matrix (extracellular polymeric substance [EPS]), consisting of several polymers purposefully secreted by the bacteria. Biofilm EPSs are highly heterogeneous and are comprised of mainly polysaccharides, proteins, and extracellular DNA (eDNA); they effectively protect the microorganisms present in the biofilm.54 The EPS composition may account for more than 80% of the biofilm volume. This imparts viscoelastic properties to the biofilm, similar to a viscoelastic fluid,179

which implies that biofilms exhibit both elastic and viscous behavior in a time-dependent fashion89,135 (Fig 13-3). Biofilm viscoelasticity is acknowledged as a virulence factor that contributes significantly to biofilm persistence and recalcitrance.135 Therefore, for biofilm disinfection to be effective, disruption of the EPS is essential.

The EPS composition of biofilms strongly depends on the type of microbial species comprising the biofilm as well as the environmental conditions that prevail during biofilm growth such as nutrition and the substances that are present.108 It is important to realize that a root canal may harbor up to 600 bacterial taxa.124 An example of environmental conditions is the presence of simple metal ions such as
Ca++. Ca++ can be incorporated into the matrix, causing cross-linking of the negative binding sides of polysaccharides and thus reinforcing the matrix.175 Furthermore, resistant spots have been demonstrated within biofilms that are more difficult to remove. Their recalcitrance can be attributed to a higher cell and/or EPS density present in these localized structures or the presence of cell subpopulations and/or an
EPS with higher reducing capacity. Notably, resistant spots show a rapid regrowth capacity after treatment with potent biocides.35

Forces on the biofilm exerted by irrigant flow could disrupt its top layers or its EPS matrix (cohesive failure) or could completely remove the biofilm (adhesive failure). Disruption of the top layers or EPS matrix facilitates irrigant penetration in the biofilm and could therefore enhance the chemical effect of irrigants. Although much is known about the effect of fluid flow on biofilm development,60 not much information is available on the effect of fluid flow on a developed

biofilm.154 With regard to the latter, a methodological obstacle that warrants attention concerns the assessment of the mechanical properties of developed biofilms. Measurements should take place on a short time scale (within minutes), because, as the biofilm is a living organism, it will adapt to its environment.55 Critical loads necessary to disrupt biofilms and the techniques available to investigate this parameter have been reviewed.12 It was found that the sensitivity to certain loading modes such as normal or shear stresses varies extensively among biofilms. Weak forces (low pressures and shear stresses) applied on a biofilm will result only in a temporary elastic deformation of the biofilm. As soon as the force is removed, the biofilm will recover. Elastic biofilm deformation may also be the result of forces applied on biofilms showing high (EPS matrix-driven) structural stability. Repeated loading of the biofilm structure with a periodic force, as in the case of sonic, ultrasonic, and laser activation, may result in biofilm fatigue.55

The threshold (force and number of loading cycles) for damage due to fatigue is nonetheless known.

Stronger forces applied on a biofilm or forces applied on biofilms showing low (EPS matrix-driven) structural stability will result in the viscous deformation of the biofilm. As biofilm displacement occurs, the biofilm rearranges its structure in an attempt to dissipate the stress developed.87 When a steady force is applied, e.g. in the case of a steady flow, the biofilm will acquire a steady state and no further deformation or removal will take place. Therefore, it may be advantageous to generate a non-steady flow, e.g. by unsteady oscillations of the ultrasonic file, or by generating pulsations with ultrasound81 or a laser.38

At stresses exceeding the cohesive or adhesive strength of the biofilm, parts of the biofilm will detach from the bulk biofilm (mechanical failure of the biofilm, a process called sloughing138,156,157) or from the substrate, respectively. Detached biofilm parts may reattach at a different location where the mechanical and chemical conditions are more favorable, in a process called (passive) biofilm dispersion.55

## Irrigation systems

Syringe irrigation is still the most common irrigation system used, either alone or combined with several irrigant activation techniques.49

However, its efficacy and safety have been questioned.1 This has brought new or existing irrigation systems to the forefront in the pursuit of improving the efficacy of syringe irrigation or preventing irrigant extrusion.65,77

Irrigant activation is an effective way to augment syringe irrigation.
An easily applicable irrigant activation protocol is the “intermittent flush technique” first described by Cameron.28 The irrigant is first delivered into the root canal by syringe irrigation. Then, the irrigant is activated inside the root canal. This activation brings about a significant mechanical stress on the substrate attached on the root canal wall, leading to partial or complete substrate detachment. After activation, the root canal needs to be rinsed using syringe irrigation in order to remove the substrate loosened from the root canal wall by the irrigant activation.

An alternative protocol applies a continuous flow of irrigant into the pulp chamber, through or alongside the instrument employed. The irrigant flows from the pulp chamber or coronal root canal toward the

### Syringe irrigation (positive pressure irrigation)

During syringe irrigation, the irrigant is typically delivered by a needle connected to a syringe.121 The flow through the needle is generated by applying pressure on the plunger of the attached syringe.17

Syringes are available at volumes ranging from 1 to 50 mL, with it being easier to achieve high flow rates with smaller syringes. The various needle types available on the market can be categorized as open-ended (sharp or blunt end) or closed-ended (one or more side outlets) designs (Fig 13-6).20 Various needle sizes are available, of which the 27 and 30 G needles (0.4- and 0.3-mm outer diameter, respectively) are customarily used. Needles are made from stainless steel, NiTi, or flexible material such as polyimide or polypropylene.
For apical cleaning, the needle tip is preferably positioned as close to the working length (WL) as possible.121 Importantly, the needle should remain loose in the root canal while irrigating. To prevent the needle tip from getting stuck in the root canal, it is advisable to move the needle up and down in short vertical strokes while irrigating. This clinical procedure is recommended for two reasons: Firstly, to minimize the risk of periapical irrigant extrusion. Secondly, to enhance irrigant replacement apically to the needle and improve irrigant streaming in the entire length of the root canal. Optimal control of flow rate as well as needle placement in the root canal are attainable, which have a positive impact on the cleaning efficacy.

Negative pressure irrigation systems make use of a microcannula that is placed close to the WL or more coronally in the middle third of the root canal.65 Fresh irrigant is provided in the pulp chamber using a larger needle and is aspirated through the microcannula.

### Sonic activation

Sonic activated irrigation can be performed using sonic handpieces that drive instruments at sonic frequencies. The instruments are driven into vibration at one end (at the handpiece). The other (free)

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 715 -->

end of the instrument is inserted into the root canal near the WL. The oscillations of the instruments agitate the irrigant inside the root canal in order to enhance the mixing of the irrigant and the cleaning by the fluid flow.

Sonic devices operate at audible frequencies below 20 kHz. The sonically driven instruments exhibit a simple bending pattern, consisting of a large amplitude at the tip (antinode) and a small amplitude at the driven end (node).100 The amplitude at the antinode may be as large as 1 mm, which is larger than the diameter of a root canal. Therefore, frequent contact with the root canal dentin is highly likely to occur, which reduces the effectiveness of the technique.79

There are different sonic activation systems available on the market such as EndoActivator (Dentsply Tulsa Dental Specialties,
Tulsa, OK, USA) or EDDY (VDW, Munich, Germany). Both of these systems make use of polymer or polyamide tips that will not cut in the root canal wall and can be inserted into curved root canals.
EndoActivator tips are powered at low frequencies in the range of 2 to 3 kHz, whereas EDDY tips are powered at frequencies of 5 to 6 kHz. For both systems, it is recommended to deliver the irrigant into the root canal by syringe irrigation after a “fully tapered shape” has been created. The irrigant can then be activated for a certain amount of time and the tip can be moved up and down in short, 2 to 3 mm vertical strokes (pumping action). Significant temperature rise of the irrigant during sonic activation has not been reported in the literature.46

### Ultrasonic activation

For ultrasonic activation, most of the ultrasonic devices already on the market can be used, combined with a variety of instruments.
Sometimes, special chucks or irrigation systems are needed. Like sonic activation, the instruments used during ultrasonic activation have an enforced vibration at one end (at the handpiece) and are allowed to vibrate freely at the other end. Non-cutting instruments are

recommended to be inserted 1 to 4 mm from the WL, depending on the type of instrument used, or just before a root canal curvature in order to prevent contact with the root canal walls during oscillation.21,106 Needles that can be ultrasonically activated can be used with a continuous flush of irrigant in the root canal with positive or negative pressure.65 For the intermittent flush technique, a sequence of three activation cycles, with a 10-second irrigant activation per cycle and intermittent flushing/irrigant refreshment, has been shown to favor dentinal debris removal.16 For a continuous flush, 1 minute is advised. For optimal cleaning efficacy of oval extensions as well as isthmuses and lateral canals of which the position is known, the instrument should be placed in a position that allows for its oscillation toward these areas, if possible.80

Ultrasonic devices operate at high frequencies (typically 20 to 200 kHz) and have amplitudes of less than 100 μm.92,93,172 Higher frequency employed by ultrasonic activation leads to a more complex pattern of several nodes and antinodes compared with those generated by sonic devices. The currently available ultrasonic devices operate at 30 kHz, leading to an oscillation pattern of approximately three wavelengths, or six nodes and antinodes spaced approximately

The geometry and material properties of the instrument determine the exact oscillation pattern. The oscillation amplitude of the tip of the current ultrasonic devices and instruments is in the order of 10 to 100
μm in the direction of oscillation. There is also a small oscillation perpendicular to the main oscillation direction.93,172

The cross-section of instruments available for ultrasonic activation is circular (non-cutting) or square (cutting). In 1980, Weller177

proposed intentional contact of the oscillated instrument with the root canal wall during ultrasonic activation. Later, it was shown that irrigant streaming is better in the absence of intentional contact.166

### Laser activation

For LAI, the lasers typically used are the Er:YAG or ErCr:YSGG types, both with a wavelength within the infrared region (2,796 to
2,940 nm) that is absorbed well in water and NaOCl.111 The laser devices generate laser pulses at rates of 1 to 25 Hz, with a typical energy of 50 to 500 mJ per pulse. The laser light is coupled into an optical fiber with a flat or conical tip, suitable for insertion into the root canal. The intermittent flush technique or a continuous flow into the pulp chamber can be used. The laser fiber is typically inserted 1 to 2 mm short of the WL and moved vertically within the apical third.38

Two other techniques employing an Er:YAG laser have been introduced using sub-ablative energy (20 mJ, 15 Hz) and ultra-short pulses (50 μs), namely, photon-initiated photoacoustic streaming (PIPS) and shock wave-enhanced emission photoacoustic streaming (SWEEPS).44,159 Dedicated cylindrical tips are used that have a tapered end for PIPS and a conical-shaped end for SWEEPS. The difference in tip design influences the cavitation process and consequently the flow characteristics (addressed later in this chapter).

The commercially available laser devices are available in a variety of sizes and types of optical fibers, pulse repetition frequency (PRF),

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 718 -->

pulsation energy, and pulse length. De Groot et al38 reported as optimal settings a combination of a low power per pulse (80 mJ) and a PRF of 15 Hz. Significant loss of irrigant from the pulp chamber was reported for energy settings higher than 120 mJ per pulse, reducing the efficacy of the irrigation procedure.

For the PIPS and SWEEPS techniques, the recommended energy setting is 10 to 20 mJ. Laser settings often reported for both PIPS and SWEEPS are 20 mJ, 15 Hz, and 0.30 W. The PIPS or SWEEPS optical fiber is placed in the access cavity, close to the coronal opening of the root canal, after the pulp cavity has been filled with irrigant. Due to the lack of standardized protocols, it is not possible to provide clinical guidelines at present.

Irrigant activation by laser does not heat the root canal wall more than 5° C, depending on the pulse frequency and power.130 Using the low pulse duration and energy that is recommended for PIPS, a temperature rise of 1.2° C after 20 seconds and 1.5° C after 40 seconds of activation were recorded in the radicular apical third.44

### Syringe irrigation

The flow characteristics in the root canal during syringe irrigation depend primarily on the type of needle used (Fig 13-7).20,31,173 Two needle groups can be distinguished; namely, closed-ended and openended needles. This distinction is based on the needle outlet type and the resulting irrigant flow developed.

Open-ended needles such as those with a flat, beveled, or notched outlet generate a jet that extends along the longitudinal axis of the root canal, apically to their tip, depending on the flow rate. The irrigant then returns to the coronal opening, flowing along the exterior

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 719 -->

of the needle, as the apex is considered closed.128,163 As the jet is directed at the apical foramen, high pressure is developed there, increasing the likelihood of irrigant extrusion.140,141 The jet may become unstable and break up at high flow rates or large distances from the apex,173 leading to unsteady (but not turbulent) flow, with enhanced mixing and reduced pressure at the apex.

In the case of closed-ended needles (side-vented, double sidevented), the irrigant leaves the needle from the side outlets and is directed toward the apex at an angle of approximately 30 degrees.
The flow then curves around the tip of the needle before it returns to the coronal opening.18,20,31 A series of counter-rotating vortices (flow structures where the fluid is rotating) is formed apically to the tip, up to the apex. The size, position, and number of vortices are determined by the needle insertion depth, root canal size and taper, and flow rate. The irrigant velocity inside each consecutive vortex drops rapidly toward the apex, resulting in a very low irrigant refreshment near the apex.173 The irrigant exchange of the closedended needles is therefore limited to 1 mm beyond the needle tip14,15,18,20,31,173 at clinically realistic timescales. A low velocity at the apex is associated with a small pressure, making closed-ended needles safer in terms of the risk of irrigant extrusion.140,141

### Negative pressure irrigation

The aspiration of irrigant by an apically placed microcannula creates an inverse flow pattern compared with the positive pressure systems.
The irrigant is sucked along the cannula toward the apex, which results in the development of shear stress on the walls of the root canal.31 As the microcannula can be placed up to the apex, irrigant mixing up to the apex can be achieved.31,96,153

Various negative-pressure irrigation systems are available.65 As most available data derive from studies on EndoVac, this system is used in this chapter as a reference for an estimation of the flow velocities and shear stresses developed. The maximum possible flow rate through the EndoVac microcannula (30 G) is determined by the negative pressure and is, under ideal conditions, 0.05 mL/s for an aspiration pressure of 25.4 kPa (see Table 13-1).26

Assuming a root canal of size 40/0.04 taper, which is recommended for an optimal flow rate under clinical conditions,26 an average irrigant velocity of 1.1 m/s can be calculated for the root canal near the tip of the microcannula.145 At these flow rates, the flow

will remain laminar.145 As the flow velocities produced during positive pressure irrigation (syringe irrigation) are typically higher than this in areas near the needle outlets, negative pressure systems are expected to generate lower shear stress on the root canal walls31,96

and therefore be less effective than syringe irrigation regarding the removal of biomaterial from the root canal wall. ### Sonic and ultrasonic activation

The sonically or ultrasonically oscillating instruments act as a mixing device when agitating the irrigant, inducing an oscillating streaming of the fluid around them. This results in the generation of alternating pressures and shear stresses on biomaterial attached to the root canal wall.171

The flow along the file in the axial direction is determined by the pattern of nodes and antinodes that the instrument exhibits along its length (Fig 13-8). Sonic instruments typically have a single antinode at the tip and a node at the driven end, which leads to streaming from the tip to the driven end. The multitude of antinodes and nodes on an ultrasonically driven instrument lead to a more complex pattern of microstreaming along the instrument. In both cases, a strong lateral flow is generated, which is advantageous for cleaning extensions and lateral anatomical features of the root canal (lateral canals, isthmuses, oval extensions).6,36,56,80 By increasing the oscillation

<!-- PDF page 724 -->

amplitude (e.g. a higher ultrasound intensity), a more effective cleaning is brought about.78

For the sonic instruments, the flow will be influenced by the frequency of the oscillating tip – the higher the frequency, the more dynamic the streaming. This is expected to have an impact on the cleaning action, which was recently confirmed in an in vitro investigation where EDDY (6 kHz) outperformed EndoActivator (2 kHz) in cleaning efficacy.161

The flow generated in the apical third is weak, for both sonic and ultrasonic activation.123 Notably, effective mixing and refreshment of the irrigant and dentinal debris removal occur only within 3 mm beyond the tip of an ultrasonically activated file.106 Consequently, sonic or ultrasonic instruments should be placed near the WL, considering also that the risk of irrigant extrusion is minor due to the absence of a strong flow toward the apical foramen. ### Acoustic streaming

Acoustic streaming can be induced by ultrasonic but not sonic devices, as the energy output of the latter is too low.171 As described earlier in this chapter, acoustic streaming has an oscillatory and steady component. Whereas the oscillatory component oscillates the fluid back and forth, it is the steady component of the flow (jets) that brings about the actual transport and mixing of the fluid. The jets also exert a pressure (1 kPa) and shear stress (10 Pa) on (the biomaterial on) the root canal wall (see Table 13-1).171 These forces are developed even at relatively large distances from the instrument, as the decrease in the velocity of the jets with increasing distance from the file is much less compared with the decrease the oscillatory flow undergoes as the distance increases. Near the file, the steady pressure and shear stress values associated with the jets can be one or two orders of magnitude lower than those developed as a result of the oscillatory flow, while at a distance from the file the forces generated may be of similar values. For the jets, the pressure generated is highest in the center. The highest shear stress values, however, are recorded off-center and at a distance of 0.1 times the distance between the oscillating file and the wall.43,137

Acoustic streaming is induced by all instruments that are currently available, irrespective of their cross-sectional designs. The streaming, however, may change,86 especially near sharp corners. The confined space of a root canal may also affect the streaming.

### Cavitation

In water, the velocity threshold for cavitation is around 15 m/s. This can be achieved with the current endodontic ultrasonic devices, but not with sonic devices.79,102,103 An oscillating pressure field induced by an ultrasonically oscillating file can lead to the formation of stable bubbles that oscillate with large amplitude. This may significantly enhance the streaming and thereby the cleaning in the vicinity of the bubbles.22,75,107 This effect is especially useful when bubbles are

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 727 -->

located in an area that is otherwise difficult to access such as lateral canals and isthmuses. This mechanism has been observed near an ultrasonically oscillating endodontic file, where the generation of stable bubbles was very effective in removing a layer of viscoelastic hydrogel (see Fig 13-5).170 In another study, the synergistic action of a microbubble emulsion and ultrasonic agitation was shown to improve biofilm removal.70

There are three ways to introduce stable bubbles into the root canal during ultrasonic activation. Firstly, through entrainment, which happens when file-induced instabilities at the irrigant–air interface lead to gas being entrapped in the irrigant.102 Secondly, bubbles may grow during rectified diffusion,33 which is a process occurring when, during each ultrasound-induced oscillation of the bubble, a small amount of gas diffuses into the bubble. After several seconds (or hundreds of thousands of oscillations), the bubble can grow up to the resonance size of 100 μm in radius. Thirdly, stable bubble formation may occur as a result of gas generation when the irrigant reacts with organic material.61,170

Several studies have demonstrated the occurrence of cavitation on or very close to endodontic instruments.3,51,78,99 In ultrasonically oscillating files, the bubbles tend to form near the sharp edges of files with a square cross-section (cutting files), where the velocity and pressure gradients are highest. Although cavitation is known to promote cleaning,53,75,109 it remains debatable whether the cavitation developed in the confined root canal space by ultrasonically oscillating endodontic instruments contributes to cleaning the root canal wall.3,99

## Physical properties of irrigants

The flow of irrigants is directly influenced by their physical properties, especially density, viscosity, and surface tension.178 The properties of endodontic irrigants are very similar to those of distilled water,66,167

as irrigants are mainly aqueous solutions. The surface tension of endodontic irrigants has been studied more extensively than the other two properties, under the assumption that this particular property may significantly affect the irrigant penetration in dentinal tubules and accessory root canals,1,162 with repercussions for the disinfection and dissolution of pulp tissue.155 Addition of wetting agents (surfactants) to commonly used irrigation solutions has also been suggested as a means of reducing their surface tension.1,162 Typical caveats in studies investigating the effect of surfactants to irrigating solutions are the frequent omission of reporting the exact concentration of the added surfactants in the solutions as well as the fact that eventual interactions occurring by mixing NaOCl with other chemicals are grossly overlooked.34 While the density and viscosity of an irrigant always affect the flow, the effect of surface tension acquires importance only when two immiscible substances are present in the root canal (e.g. irrigant and air).90,178 However, dentin is hydrophilic, and the dentinal tubules are likely to contain dentinal fluid with fluidic properties similar to water.10 The dentinal fluid will probably soak the root canal wall, thereby limiting the effect of surfactants, as the two fluids are miscible. Recent studies have also reported that surfactants did not enhance the ability of NaOCl to dissolve pulp tissue,82 nor the ability of common chelators to remove calcium or the smear layer from the dentin.40,82,183 On the contrary, it appears that the use of

## Vapor lock and apical penetration or irrigant

An air bubble may be trapped in the apical part of the root canal, also called a “vapor lock.” The presence of an air bubble occupying the apical part of the root canal has been demonstrated ex vivo31,165 and in vivo.168,169 Such a bubble could be entrapped during irrigant delivery in an empty root canal by the advancing irrigant front65 or due to the coalescence of gas bubbles produced in the root canal during the reaction between NaOCl and organic material.65,169 A vapor lock could potentially obstruct irrigant penetration into the apical area.

Air entrapment has been demonstrated and studied in top-filled conical capillaries,47,112,113 where it has also been shown that the bubble disappears spontaneously, albeit after a relatively long time.133 However, in the case of root canal irrigation, the method of

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 740 -->

introducing the irrigant is different because root canals are filled from the inside out and not from the top. Nevertheless, a bubble may still disappear spontaneously. The results of a published experimental and
CFD study show that the occurrence of a vapor lock decreases by using an open-ended needle, increasing the apical size, positioning the needle closer to the WL, and delivering the irrigant at a higher flow rate (0.26 mL/s). Also, the brief insertion of the needle to the WL could effectively remove an established vapor lock (Fig 13-13).16

Negative pressure irrigation, ultrasonic activation, or laser activation of the irrigant can prevent or remove the vapor lock.30,31,168,169 An in vivo study demonstrated that both ultrasonic activation and negative pressure were equally effective in apical irrigant delivery in root canals of mesial roots of mandibular molars.122

## Comparison between irrigation systems

In patients undergoing a root canal treatment, do any of the currently existing irrigation systems result in superior treatment outcomes (primary outcome of interest)? A meta-analysis of clinical trials would be the most appropriate study design to properly address this issue.
However, designing clinical studies (superiority or noninferiority) to assess the effect of irrigation systems on the healing of AP is difficult and unfortunately sparse in the field of endodontics. Healing of AP depends on many factors, which are difficult to control in a clinical trial such as (root canal) filling and instrumentation techniques, immune response of the host, biofilm (intra- and extraradicular), anatomy of the root canal system, imaging techniques (2D vs 3D), definition of healing, and healing over time. Furthermore, in order for the comparison to be meaningful, information on the costeffectiveness of the irrigation systems is also important. Again, there is a paucity of studies in the endodontic literature that perform a costeffectiveness analysis of the currently existing irrigation systems.

Consequently, a choice can only be made based on ex vivo and in vitro studies assessing surrogate outcomes (e.g. cleaning efficacy of irrigation systems). Here another problem arises, as proper comparison is difficult due to the different methodologies applied among the studies. Recently published systematic literature reviews on activated/nonactivated irrigation29,160 as well as a narrative review on LAI give a good overview of the different irrigation systems, the level of evidence, and the heterogeneity of the results.45 The conclusions drawn from these reviews are generally aligned and can be summarized as follows:

Ultrasonic and LAI lead to superior surrogate outcomes compared with non-activated irrigation, but no superiority in the primary outcome of interest (i.e. the healing of AP) can be corroborated.
The level of evidence is low and the risk of bias is moderate to high.
Due to the great variation in the methodological designs of the included studies, any clinical recommendations seem precarious at present. Within the same context, it is the opinion of the authors of this chapter that well-designed and methodologically similar clinical (superiority or noninferiority) trials are required before any conclusions can be drawn about the added value of the different irrigation systems on patient- and clinician-centered outcomes.
