## Introduction

In 1928, Alexander Fleming discovered the first antibiotic, penicillin, and Dr Florey introduced the use of antibiotics in clinical practice.
Antibiotics have been helpful in treating a large number of infectious diseases that were once a major cause of death. Moreover, over the past few decades, antibiotics have been used to support modern medical care, including the ability to perform surgeries and organ replacements as well as to treat cancer.27 Undoubtedly, the lengthening of life expectancy and the decrease in infant mortality that have occurred in the world since the middle of the 20th century are largely attributable to antibiotics. Like physicians use systemic antibiotics to treat various infections in the human body, so dentists use them in the treatment of endodontic infections.57 Systemic antibiotics may be a complement to endodontic clinical treatment when the immune response of the host needs help in order to control the infection.44 However, to avoid overprescription, which increases the appearance of resistant bacterial strains, the prescription of antibiotics must always be based on scientific evidence. On the other hand, antibiotics may be indicated in certain patients as a prophylactic treatment prior to endodontic therapy.58 The prescription of antibiotics as a prophylaxis must also be based on scientific evidence; that is, the risk to the patient must be serious enough to justify antibiotic prophylaxis in terms of risk-benefit and costeffectiveness.60 This chapter analyses the main aspects of the use of antibiotics in the management of endodontic patients.

## Principles of systemic antibiotic therapy

Antibiotics are naturally occurring substances of microbial origin, or similar synthetic (or semi-synthetic) substances, which have

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1065 -->

antimicrobial activity in low concentrations that inhibit microbial growth or selectively kill microorganisms. Antibiotics are specific drugs for the treatment of bacterial infections. For antibiotics to be effective in treating an infection, some basic principles must be known and followed (Table 19-1). If these principles are not adhered to, the treatment will not work and undesirable side effects may occur.

### Mechanisms of antibacterial action

Some antibiotics kill bacteria (bactericidal) while others inhibit bacterial growth (bacteriostatic). Each type of antibiotic performs its effect through a specific mechanism of action (Table 19-2).
Bactericidal antibiotics kill bacteria, inhibiting cell wall synthesis (βlactams, vancomycin, bacitracin, etc), disrupting the cytoplasmic membrane (polymyxins, polyenes, etc), or acting on
DNA (metronidazole, quinolones). Bacteriostatic antibiotics, on the other hand, inhibit bacterial growth, inhibiting protein synthesis (aminoglycosides, chloramphenicol, macrolides, tetracyclines, lincosamides, etc) or exerting antimetabolite activity, e.g. by inhibiting folic acid synthesis (sulfonamides, trimethoprim, etc). While the distinction between bactericidal and bacteriostatic agents appears to be clear according to the in vitro definition, this only applies under strict laboratory conditions and is inconsistent for a particular agent against all bacteria.42 Bacteriostatic antibiotics can exhibit bactericidal effects at high concentrations. Although bacteriostatic/bactericidal data may provide valuable information on the potential action of antibacterial agents in vitro, it is necessary to combine this information with pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic data to provide a more meaningful prediction of efficacy in vivo. The ultimate guide to treatment of any infection must be clinical outcome.45

β-lactam antibiotics (penicillins and cephalosporins), which are the most widely used antibiotics in the treatment of endodontic infections, kill bacteria by inhibiting cell wall synthesis. The action of β-lactams especially occurs during the active growth of the bacteria, at which time the bacterial cell wall is synthesized. β-lactams inhibit

Peptidoglycan crosslinking is essential for the bacterial cell wall to be rigid, and to protect the bacterium from osmotic lysis. β-lactams competitively inhibit transpeptidases by binding to these enzymes as alternative substrates. Transpeptidases act as penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs). In addition, the β-lactams–

Tetracyclines are contraindicated in children younger than 9 years of age because they can be irreversibly incorporated into bone and teeth during mineralization, thus leading to malformation or irreversible tooth crown pigmentation. Macrolides are antibiotics used in the treatment of endodontic infections in patients who are allergic to penicillin. Resistance can develop by modification of the target (23S rRNA methylation), enzymatic cleavage, and active efflux.

Licosamides (clindamycin and lincomycin) are also used in endodontics in the treatment of patients allergic to penicillins. They also bind to the 50S subunit of the bacterial ribosome, inhibiting protein synthesis. In this case, the inhibited enzyme is peptidyl transferase, so the elongation of the peptide is blocked.
Lincosamides do not alter protein synthesis in human cells because prokaryotic and eukaryotic ribosomes are structurally different.
Lincosamides are used against Gram-positive bacteria since they are unable to pass through the porins of Gram-negative bacteria.
Resistance may occur due to methylation of the 23S rRNA.

Metronidazole is also a bactericidal antibiotic used in the treatment of endodontic infections. It is an imidazole derivative, active against anaerobic bacteria. ### Broad- and narrow-spectrum antibiotics

Each antibiotic is effective for a specific group or spectrum of microorganisms. A broad-spectrum antibiotic is one that has an effect on Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria or on a wide range of pathogenic bacteria. These antibiotics (such as ampicillin) are indicated in the treatment of infections caused by unknown bacteria (empirical therapy) or when an infection with multiple groups of bacteria is suspected. On the contrary, narrow-spectrum antibiotics act against a small number of susceptible species.

Endodontic infections that require antibiotic treatment usually start abruptly and with severe pain. Therefore, the treatment must be immediate, which makes it impossible to carry out a culture of the bacteria and an antibiogram (antibiotic susceptibility testing). The decision regarding which antibiotic to administer should be based on knowledge about the efficacy of antibiotics against the bacterial species most frequently implicated in the etiology of endodontic infections. Considering that it is most usual to find mixed communities with a predominance of anaerobic bacteria, a broad-spectrum antibiotic should be chosen.

### True and opportunistic pathogens

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1072 -->

Some microorganisms, called true pathogens, have antigens, toxins, and virulence factors that cause disease to develop when they invade the human body (Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Treponema pallidum, and Neisseria meningitidis cause tuberculosis, syphilis, and meningitis, respectively). In contrast, other microorganisms colonize human cavities and surfaces without causing disease. The human microbiota is the assemblage of microorganisms that reside on or within human tissues and biofluids.35 The human cell-to-bacteria ratio is estimated to be around 3:1, or even 1:1.59. Nonpathogenic microbes play an important role in host homeostasis and in the functioning of body systems, especially the digestive system. In addition, by stimulating the innate and adaptive immune response, and by occupying space and consuming nutrients, they help to defend humans against true pathogens. Some of these microorganisms are commensal, which means they coexist without harming humans;
others have a mutualistic relationship with their human hosts.
However, if commensal bacteria invade normally sterile places such as dental pulp, they can become pathogens and cause disease.
These microorganisms are called opportunistic pathogens.

### Side effects of antibiotics

The administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics always entails the alteration of the normal microbiota and the breakdown of the ecological balance between the host and the microorganisms, which can cause systemic alterations, especially digestive ones. There are no antibiotics that respect the normal microbiota, so side effects are inevitable. The most common side effects of antibiotics affect the digestive system (vomiting, nausea, diarrhea, bloating, indigestion, abdominal pain, loss of appetite) and occur in around 10% of people.
Side effects are usually mild and disappear as soon as treatment is completed or stopped. In endodontics, the dentist does not know what microorganisms are responsible for the endodontic infection in a specific patient. Therefore, a broad-spectrum type of antibiotic is chosen empirically, based on clinical and epidemiologic studies.47

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1073 -->

Therefore, when antibiotics are prescribed in an endodontic situation, the patient should always be warned that side effects may possibly occur.

### Antibiotics are not curative drugs

The action of antibiotics on bacteria occurs by altering some structural element or by interrupting any metabolic pathway of the microorganism. Depending on the degree of structural alteration and interruption of the metabolic pathway, the effect will be inhibitory or killing. The antibiotic should affect only the bacteria, respecting the host cells (selective toxicity). Antibiotics are not curative drugs;
infections are cured by the human immune system. Antibiotics are given to a patient suffering from an infection because that individual’s immune system requires help to fight the infection. The antibiotic collaborates with the host’s defenses, helping them to control the infection and kill the causative bacteria. Most human infections are cured thanks to the activation of the immune response, without the need for antibiotics.44 Antibiotics should only be prescribed when there are clinical signs and symptoms indicating that the immune system needs help.

### Abuse of antibiotics: bacterial resistance

The abuse of antibiotics is causing bacteria to develop resistance to them. Some strains of Porphyromonas spp and Prevotella spp,10

initially sensitive to antibiotics, have developed resistance that makes them immune.31 The prescription of antibiotics to treat minor endodontic infections (such as irreversible pulpitis, or symptomatic apical periodontitis without swelling or systemic involvement) is widespread throughout the world.52,55
The inappropriate administration of antibiotics when they are not indicated, or for an excessive period of time, contributes to the appearance of resistant bacterial strains among the saprophytic and commensal bacteria that

inhabit the digestive system, the skin, and the mucous membranes, and may be contributing significantly to the problem of bacterial resistance.57

### Antibiotics should be used only when indicated

Therefore, is very important that clinicians know the correct indications for the prescription of antibiotics for endodontic infections.
Antibiotics should be reserved primarily for the short-term treatment of infectious diseases with acute symptoms or as a prophylactic measure. On the contrary, they are not usually indicated in chronic infections because their prolonged use could induce the selection and predominance of resistant bacterial species that would produce secondary infections, even in other parts of the body.

### Adequate doses and loading dose

For the antibiotic to have an effect on the bacteria, it must reach an adequate concentration in the blood. Moreover, antibiotics typically need to reach a site of action outside the plasma such as the periapical tissues. This requires the drug to pass through the capillary membranes. If the adequate concentration is not reached due to the administration of an inappropriate dose, the antibiotic treatment will fail.28 Inadequate antibiotic dosing is strongly associated with unfavorable outcomes in the treatment of infection.

In addition, for the antibiotic to begin working as soon as possible, it is necessary that its concentration in the blood reaches the bactericidal level quickly. In order to rapidly achieve high peak plasma concentrations, the first dose of antibiotic (loading dose) must be higher than the maintenance dose. Loading dose is a single dose or a multiple set of doses given to a patient to attain the desired drug levels more rapidly. Most antibiotics have time-dependent activity and exert their optimal activity when drug concentrations are maintained above the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), which is the lowest

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1075 -->

concentration of an antibiotic that inhibits the visible growth of a microorganism. To achieve this, the first dose of antibiotic must be twice the maintenance dose.12 Furthermore, a percentage of antibiotics bind to serum proteins (25% moxicillin; 40% to 80% penicillin V, erythromycin, or azithromycin; and > 80% to 90% clindamycin).7 Since only the portion of the antibiotic not bound to serum proteins can diffuse through blood vessels and tissue barriers, the loading dose also compensates for the serum protein binding effect of these antibiotics.

### Duration of antibiotic treatment

In some lung or kidney infections, clinical guidelines recommend a specific regimen for the administration of antibiotics. However, in oral infections, including endodontic infections, the duration of treatment should be determined by the patient’s symptoms.43 As soon as the dentist detects that the patient’s immune system has the infection under control, the antibiotic treatment should be stopped.58

Obviously, this requires the dentist to monitor the patient who is taking antibiotics on a daily basis. The only guide for determining the effectiveness and duration of antibiotic treatment should be the clinical improvement in the patient’s symptoms and signs such as the absence of pain and fever, the absence or reduction of swelling, and the disappearance of other signs or symptoms of infection.58 The patient should stop taking antibiotics when the symptoms have resolved and there is clinical evidence of healing.19 Endodontic infections tend to have a rapid onset, and, if proper operative treatment is performed to eliminate the source of infection, they have a relatively short duration (2 to 5 days). Therefore, most cases of infection require only 3 to 4 days of antibiotic therapy. The majority of the patients were reported to be asymptomatic after 2 days;36

therefore, patients should be seen after 2 or 3 days to determine whether the antibiotics should be continued or stopped.

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1076 -->

The idea that prolonged antibiotic treatment is necessary for resistant bacteria to die is completely wrong.37 On the contrary, the opposite is true. The longer the duration of antibiotic treatment, the greater the chance of developing new resistant strains. In addition, antibiotic-resistant bacteria will not be killed no matter how long the treatment lasts.

Another common misconception is that prolonging the antibiotic treatment once the symptoms have disappeared prevents recurrences of the periapical inflammatory process.61 If the source of periapical infection has been properly eradicated by complete debridement, irrigation, and disinfection of the root canal system, endodontic infections do not rebound.

In short, maintaining antibiotic treatment beyond the disappearance of symptoms is unnecessary, contributes to the development of resistant strains, and increases the risk of side effects such as allergic sensitization or secondary infection.32 As far as duration is concerned: the shorter the better.61

### Selection of the best antibiotic

The susceptibility test (antibiogram) is the best way to select the antibiotic to use in a specific infection. In severe oral abscesses, this should be the method of selection of the antibiotic to ensure maximum effectiveness of the treatment. The problem is that bacterial culture is a slow procedure (7 to 14 days), and, when indicated, antibiotic treatment should be started as soon as possible to control the infection, which may otherwise progress very quickly. For this reason, in endodontics, the antibiotic must be selected empirically, and an antibiotic should be chosen that has a spectrum of action that covers the most frequent microbial species in periapical abscesses.28

### Interactions of antibiotics with food and drugs

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1077 -->

Antibiotics can interact with food, alcohol, and some drugs. Food can alter the bioavailability of some antibiotics (tetracycline, azithromycin, and quinolones) when administered orally. If any of these antibiotics are prescribed, patients should be clearly advised to take them 1 hour before or 2 hours after food intake.53

Some antibiotics can interact with alcohol. Alcohol consumption can reduce erythromycin efficacy, and doxycycline may have reduced efficacy in chronic alcoholism.38 The classically supposed disulfiramlike interaction between metronidazole and alcohol occurs with uncertain frequency and with varied severity.67 Cephalosporins also have an increased risk of a disulfiram-like reaction.49 Dentists should advise patients not to drink alcoholic beverages during treatment with these antibiotics. Furthermore, cephalosporins should not be prescribed to patients with alcoholism. On the contrary, clinical evidence supports that other antibiotics such as penicillins, fluoroquinolones, azithromycin, tetracycline, and fluconazole can be safely used with concomitant alcohol consumption.38

Although there is no evidence that antibiotic treatment interferes with oral contraceptives, it is prudent to warn the patient of the potential risk that the antibiotic might reduce the effectiveness of oral contraceptives.1

### Failure of antibiotic treatment

When antibiotic treatment is ineffective and the infection persists or progresses, this may be due to the host itself, the antibiotic, or the bacteria that are causing the infection. In such cases, all three factors must be taken into account.

The first cause to consider when antibiotic treatment fails is noncompliance with treatment by the patient. The poor immune status of the patient and the lack of penetration of the antibiotic to the infected site due, for instance, to the presence of purulent exudate, necrotic tissue, or biofilms, may also be involved.43 Alternatively, the antibiotic itself may not be appropriate because its spectrum does not cover the bacteria that caused the infection, or where the antibiotic is in fact appropriate, it may be that insufficient dosage has been administered. Finally, the cause may be that the bacteria causing the infection have been able to develop resistance to the antibiotic, or even if they have been killed by the antibiotic, the infection may be maintained by an active source that was not removed.

## The antibiotic resistance crisis

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1079 -->

Since their discovery a century ago, antibiotics have played a main role in extending expected human lifespans by reducing mortality from infections. In 1920, people in the USA and Europe were expected to live to be only 50 to 60 years old. Today, the average lifespan is over
80 years. Throughout the world, the beneficial effect of antibiotics has been similar. However, many decades after the first patients were treated with antibiotics, bacterial infections have again become a threat. Microbial evolutionary responses to the selective pressure exerted by antibiotics have resulted in microbial species becoming resistant to virtually every known antibiotic agent.65 Natural selection controls the growth and development of bacterial colonies. When the colony is exposed to the antibiotic, the high rate of division of the bacteria facilitates numerous mutations (Fig 19-1).

Epidemiologic studies have demonstrated a direct relationship between antibiotic consumption and the emergence and dissemination of resistant bacterial strains. Even for companies that are optimistic about pursuing the discovery of new antibiotics, obtaining regulatory approval is often an obstacle.65

### Resistance of antibiotics to oral bacteria

Resistance is often conferred by the presence of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs). Among the ARGs, “bla genes” encode for broadspectrum lactamases, e.g. blaTEM, blaSHV, blaCTX-M, blaCfxA, blaCepA, blaCblA, and blaampC. These genes are found in the oral microbiome.4

Genetic analyses of the oral microbiome using metagenomic techniques reveal a broad distribution of ARGs in individuals not recently exposed to antibiotics. This has resulted in a paradigm shift, from a focus on the carriage of antibiotic resistance in pathogenic bacteria to a broader concept of an “oral resistome,” which includes all resistance genes in the microbiome.62

## Antibiotics of choice and dosage in endodontic

## infections

As previously mentioned, the choice of an antibiotic for the treatment of a specific endodontic infection must be based on the knowledge of the endodontic microbiota normally associated with that type of periapical infection. The antibiotic whose spectrum of action includes the most commonly detected bacteria in that case should be selected. The selection will have an empirical basis, with the choice being based on what previous studies have shown to be the most effective antibiotic against the bacteria that are more likely to be responsible for that particular clinical condition. On the other hand, the percentages of antibiotic-resistant strains usually found in the microbiota of the specific type of endodontic infection to be treated should also be taken into account. Both primary and posttreatment apical periodontitis as well as apical abscess are associated with a highly diverse microbiota. Although some taxa appear differentially abundant in each one, no evident clustering of the microbiota by infection type is observed, with

Most of the bacterial species associated with endodontic infections, including abscesses, are susceptible to penicillins.26,56

Penicillins are the drugs of first choice for the treatment of infections of endodontic origin. Among the penicillins, the semi-synthetic penicillin, amoxicillin, has the fastest rate of absorption, the longest half-life, and the widest spectrum of action. In addition, it also achieves more sustained serum levels. Therefore, amoxicillin, alone or in combination with clavulanic acid, is the antibiotic of choice for the treatment of endodontic infections.58 Amoxicillin belongs to the group of β-lactam antibiotics, which have a bacteriolytic action. Compared with penicillin VK, it has several advantages, being a broader spectrum antibiotic against Gram-negative bacteria; fewer resistant bacterial strains; better oral absorption (can be taken with food since it resists gastric acidity); and a more satisfactory pharmacokinetic profile, tolerance, and dosage, maintaining adequate blood levels for a longer period of time.33,58

The major disadvantage of amoxicillin is that it can be degraded by the β-lactamase enzymes that are produced by some bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, and, as mentioned above, some anaerobic bacteria implicated in endodontic infections. To solve this problem, clavulanic acid (Co-amoxiclav), which is a β-lactamase inhibitor, is associated with amoxicillin. However, the combination of amoxicillin/clavulanic acid more easily induces bacterial resistance and is associated with a higher risk of infection by Clostridium difficile.
Therefore, it should be used with caution in immunocompetent patients, in whom amoxicillin alone is the antibiotic of choice in endodontics. Amoxicillin/clavulanic acid should be reserved for immunosuppressed patients or for cases in which there has been no response to amoxicillin alone.58

Although penicillin V exhibits antimicrobial activity against the majority of bacteria isolated from acute endodontic infections, when it failed to be effective, the combination of penicillin V with metronidazole or replacement by amoxicillin with clavulanic acid proved to be the best options.23 Furthermore, amoxicillin/clavulanate are the most effective agents for Enterococcus faecalis isolates from teeth in which root canal treatment has failed.5 In short, amoxicillin can be considered a suitable first-line antibiotic for the treatment of endodontic abscesses, and amoxicillin/clavulanate, clindamycin, and metronidazole are useful alternatives.23,25,33

Metronidazole is a good supplemental medication for amoxicillin in the treatment of endodontic abscesses due to its excellent activity against anaerobes. Where clinical improvement is still not evident in cases 48 hours following conservative endodontic treatment and the start of antibiotic treatment with amoxicillin, supplemental medication with metronidazole should be considered. In addition, the source of infection should be reassessed to determine whether additional intervention is necessary.

In cases of severe purulent odontogenic inflammations, the use of a combination of antibiotics such as amoxicillin with ciprofloxacin or clindamycin with cefuroxime is recommended.8

A major drawback of β-lactam antibiotics is that they can cause allergies. In patients with a demonstrated allergy to β-lactams, the alternatives of choice are clindamycin, clarithromycin, or azithromycin.
Clindamycin, belonging to the group of lincosamides, has strong antimicrobial activity against oral anaerobes.25 Clindamycin is associated with an increased risk of Clostridioides difficile infection, characterized by a wide range of symptoms, from mild or moderate diarrhea to severe disease with pseudomembranous colitis, colonic ileus, toxic megacolon, sepsis, or death.40 Patients taking clindamycin should be advised to stop taking the antibiotic immediately if they

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1085 -->

experience diarrhea. Macrolides, such as clarithromycin and azithromycin, are also effective against most Gram-positive bacteria and have much better Gram-negative activity than erythromycin, with a longer duration of action. They can be used in allergic patients, but have not been shown to be better than amoxicillin or clindamycin against the anaerobic bacteria commonly found in abscesses.25 On the other hand, considering that 20% of patients allergic to penicillins are also allergic to cephalosporins, these antibiotics are not a good alternative for allergic patients.

Regarding the dosages, amoxicillin should be taken three times a day, with or without clavulanic acid. The recommended oral dosage being a 1,000 mg loading dose, followed by 500 mg every 8 hours.
Clarithromycin can be administered every 12 hours, and azithromycin every 24 hours. Table 19-3 shows the European Society of
Endodontology (ESE) recommendations regarding the types, dosages, and duration of antibiotics in the treatment of endodontic infections.58

## Indications for systemic antibiotics in the treatment

## of endodontic infections

The use of systemic antibiotics in endodontics may be indicated in two clinical situations: 1) as adjuvants to the treatment of an endodontic infection; and 2) before the endodontic treatment, to prevent an infection caused by the bacteraemia following the endodontic treatment. In addition, topical antibiotics can be used for root canal disinfection or for the treatment of traumatic injuries of the teeth.

As a rule, most endodontic infections are confined within the tooth and do not require treatment with systemic antibiotics. Even periapical abscess with localized swelling can be managed successfully with local operative treatment and normal endodontic procedures such as drainage of the abscess or tooth extraction. On the other hand, the systemic administration of antibiotics in cases of pulp necrosis is ineffective, since the lack of blood circulation in the necrotic root canals prevents the antibiotic from reaching the pulp tissue.

In immunocompetent patients with apical abscesses in which exudate spreads through the interstitial spaces, the drainage incision is usually enough to eliminate purulent secretion and relieve pain.
Nevertheless, there are situations in which the endodontic infection spreads rapidly and extensively invades the adjacent tissues, acquiring a serious character. At this point, antibiotics may be indicated because they are a very useful therapeutic complement for local treatment and help to stop the spread of the infection.58

Therefore, systemic antibiotics should be reserved for patients with

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 1087 -->

symptomatic apical periodontitis with systemic compromise or for medically challenged or immunocompromised patients.58

The efficacy of the antibiotic decreases if the bacterial density at the infected site is high (inoculum effect).29 Especially in biofilms with slow bacterial growth and the accumulation of resistance factors, the ideal circumstances exist for the bacteria to be less susceptible to antibiotics. Therefore, delaying the start of antibiotic therapy can reduce its effectiveness by increasing the inoculum effect. Similarly, establishing adequate drainage of the apical abscess before starting antibiotic treatment is essential to reduce the bacterial load and facilitate the penetration and antibacterial action of the antibiotic.43

Antibiotics can also be used topically to disinfect the root canal during regenerative endodontic procedures in immature teeth with necrotic pulp and apical periodontitis.39 The mixture of ciprofloxacin, metronidazole, and minocycline as an intracanal dressing has been reported to attain complete disinfection of immature teeth with necrotic pulps. Likewise, the topical application of antibiotics could have positive effects on periodontal and pulp healing in some cases of dental trauma such as avulsion.58

## Indications for antibiotic prophylaxis in

## endodontics

The indication of antibiotics prophylactically before endodontic treatment does not have a solid scientific basis.30 There are no studies demonstrating the implication of root canal treatment in distant infections. If bacteraemia occurs during endodontic treatment in a healthy subject, it is rapidly scavenged from the bloodstream within minutes to 1 hour and does not cause complications.46

However, when there is a remote theoretical possibility of a serious distant infection occurring in susceptible patients, prophylactic administration of antibiotics is indicated. Thus, prophylaxis with little supporting evidence is sometimes recommended when the potential infection to be prevented involves a high risk for the patient. This would be the case for medically compromised patients, whose immune system is altered, or patients who have an organ in the body with some alteration that makes it more susceptible to infection.58 In cases of doubt, patient management should be discussed with a physician prior to endodontic treatment.56

Taking into account the risk of sensitizing the patient, the patient suffering an adverse reaction to the antibiotics, and the development of bacterial strains resistant to antibiotics, antibiotic prophylaxis should only be indicated when there is scientific evidence that the advantages outweigh the drawbacks.58

The ESE position statement recommends antibiotic prophylaxis in two clinical situations: immunocompromised patients, and in cases involving locus minoris resistentiae58 (Table 19-5).

In cases of impaired immune response, as with immunocompromised patients suffering from systemic diseases such as leukemia,

## Conclusions

Nowadays, bacterial resistance to antibiotics is a serious global problem, which is largely explained by the misuse of antibiotics. The prescription of antibiotics in endodontic infections must be based on the scientific evidence. Dentists must not contribute to the turning back of the medical clock and the beginning of the post-antibiotic era.

The fundamental principles to be followed regarding the prescription of systemic antibiotics in endodontic infections that are discussed in this chapter are summarized below:

Most endodontic infections do not require antibiotic treatment and can be managed only with conservative operative procedures.
Antibiotics are only necessary in very special cases of endodontic infections, being in acute apical abscess with systemic compromise or immunosuppression as well as in progressive or persistent infections.
Amoxicillin is the antibiotic of choice, with the use of clindamycin for patients who are allergic to penicillin.
The first dose should be a loading dose in order to quickly reach the serum peak.
Treatment should be stopped when there is clinical evidence of healing: the shorter the duration of the antibiotic treatment, the better.
Prolonging antibiotic treatment beyond the disappearance of symptoms contributes to the development of resistant bacterial strains.
Antibiotic prophylaxis in endodontics should only be carried out when indicated by clinical guidelines.
