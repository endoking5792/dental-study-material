The infected root canal is a reservoir of bacterial cells, bacterial virulence products, and bacterial antigens involved in the induction and maintenance of apical periodontitis. Essentially, endodontic treatment or retreatment of infected root canals consists of eliminating the existing infection and preventing further reinfection. In this regard, chemomechanical preparation supplemented by an interappointment intracanal medication serves to control endodontic infection.
Prevention of reinfection of the disinfected canal is accomplished by root canal obturation and definitive coronal restoration.

Clinicians frequently ask, “Is it really necessary to sterilize the root canal?” and “Can we predictably sterilize root canals with the current technology and substances available?” Based on current knowledge, the response to both questions is “No.” So, what are the microbiologic goals of endodontic treatment?

To better understand the goals to be reached during treatment or retreatment of teeth with apical periodontitis, it is important to address the classical observation by Theobald Smith that an infectious disease is the result of the interplay between microbial virulence and number and the host defenses.197 This concept, combined with recent data on microbial community ecology, can be applied to understand the pathogenesis of apical periodontitis as an infectious disease and, consequently, can serve as a rationale for setting the goals clinicians should pursue for optimal treatment outcome.188

A minimum load of any given pathogen is required to cause harm and initiate disease. To accomplish this, bacteria would have to reach a population density (cell count) that is conducive to tissue damage caused either by the bacteria themselves or by the host defense mechanisms in response to them.175 No clinical signs and symptoms of the disease will be apparent until this quorum of bacterial cells has been reached (Fig 11-1). The higher the virulence of the species composing the community, the lower the number of bacterial cells needed to cause disease. Endodontic infections are characterized by a mixed community of about 10 to 30 species with varying levels of virulence. Thus, it may be virtually impossible to define a threshold

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 493 -->

number of cells needed to induce disease. Host resistance is another important factor that has an impact on disease pathogenesis. The same combination and number of bacterial species may give rise to different responses in different individuals.

Ideally, the goal of endodontic treatment should be to sterilize the root canal system. Nevertheless, the very complex anatomy of the system makes it virtually impossible to meet this goal using the currently available instruments, substances, and techniques.
Therefore, the more realistic goal of antimicrobial treatment must be to reduce bacterial populations to counts below those needed to cause or maintain disease (see Fig 11-1). Low numbers of bacteria may not influence healing. For instance, in skin wounds and periodontal disease, the tissues are not sterilized and still heal in the presence of low bacterial numbers and/or a beneficial microbiota.

The great challenge is to define bacterial levels that are compatible with healing. Given the bacterial diversity of the infected canal and the large interindividual variability in species composition, it is difficult to conceive that a precise threshold value could ever be established. However, a study showed that canals with total bacterial counts below 3 × 103 cells at the time of filling had a satisfactory treatment outcome represented by the healing of apical periodontitis.231 Moreover, qualitative data from culture45,193,204,223

and molecular studies231 used to establish a correlation between persistent bacteria and treatment outcome have shown that the occurrence of negative results for bacteria before filling usually predicts a good prognosis (Fig 11-2).

Therefore, these are good parameters to define the best protocols to treat endodontic infections. In the real world, the goal of endodontic treatment is thus to reduce bacterial populations to levels below the limits detected by microbiologic procedures or at least below 103 bacterial cells as detected by highly sensitive molecular quantitative methods.

This does not mean that the clinician should go back to using chairside bacteriologic tests to determine the timing of obturation.
Reliable anaerobic culture or molecular microbiology techniques are

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 494 -->

not available for chairside testing, and this would unnecessarily delay filling in the majority of cases. Chairside tests for rapid bacterial detection have been proposed in endodontics,61,152,205 but they have not yet been introduced into clinical routine and their results still need to be evaluated for associations with long-term treatment outcome.
Consequently, clinicians are encouraged to rely on the literature and adhere to treatment protocols shown to predictably render root canals free of detectable bacteria in qualitative analyses or reduce the counts to levels of < 103 cells in quantitative analyses.

Well-controlled clinical trials and prospective studies evaluating the long-term treatment outcome represent the top level of scientific evidence and should influence the decision-making process for the best treatment approach. With the lack of such high-level studies comparing the long-term outcome, clinical microbiologic studies can be used as a surrogate endpoint to treatment outcome. This is because there is a strong correlation between bacterial elimination and the healing of apical periodontitis.23,192,223,231

Due to the high interindividual variability in species composition of endodontic bacterial communities,80,149,184 endodontic treatment should consist of a broad-spectrum, non-specific antimicrobial strategy with the potential to reach all the different community members. This chapter discusses evidence-based strategies to accomplish the microbiologic goals of endodontic treatment.

## Why should we treat or retreat teeth with

## asymptomatic disease?

Before any discussion here on how to combat endodontic infections, the question of why the clinician must be aware of the reasons for the importance of controlling infections and promoting the healing of apical periodontitis is addressed. The answer to this question might seem obvious to many readers, but it is not. This is because some authors and clinicians, based on a tooth survival criterion, have considered as successful treated teeth that remain functional and asymptomatic, even if they have an associated apical periodontitis lesion. This is a lenient criterion to categorize the treatment outcome and is against the view and definition of endodontics as the clinical specialty involved in the prevention and treatment of apical periodontitis. Based on this concept, a successful treatment is that which effectively prevented apical periodontitis when the disease was absent or created conditions for complete healing when it was present. In other words, using a stricter criterion, success of an endodontic treatment is related to the absence of apical periodontitis after an adequate follow-up period.

If one accepts the concept that an asymptomatic lesion in a treated tooth is not a reason for reintervention, then one should also assume that this very same concept might be applied to an untreated tooth. Most people, however, would probably agree that not treating a tooth with necrotic pulp and apical periodontitis only because it is asymptomatic is absurd. However, primary and posttreatment apical periodontitis are virtually the same disease with the same etiology, the major difference being the treatment status of the root canal. If a lesion persists to characterize a posttreatment disease, this is because there is infection inside or outside of the root canal system, and reintervention is needed.

Actually, most teeth with apical periodontitis are asymptomatic.209

## Treating infected root canals

Root canal infections possess some peculiarities that are distinct from infections in most other human sites. Entrenched in the root canal system, bacteria are beyond the reach of host defenses and systemic antibiotics. The absence of an active blood supply in necrotic pulp tissues or pulpless (treated) canals impedes the transport of defense cells/molecules and systemic antibiotics to the infected site. Thus, apart from tooth extraction, the only way to treat endodontic infections is by means of professional intervention using

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 499 -->

procedures with mechanical, chemical, and ecological effects that kill microorganisms.
Chemomechanical preparation and intracanal medication are the main endodontic treatment measures for infection control.

### Role of chemomechanical preparation

Chemomechanical preparation is of paramount importance for root canal disinfection since instruments and irrigants act primarily in the main canal, which is the largest area of the canal system and consequently harbors the largest number of bacteria. The mechanical action of instruments and irrigation as well as the chemical (antibacterial) action of irrigants serve to eliminate bacteria from the root canal.

### Mechanical action

Studies in which no antibacterial irrigants were used have shown that the mechanical action of instrumentation and irrigation alone is effective in eliminating more than 90% of the cultivable bacteria in a root canal.27,38,144,171 Thus, mechanical instrumentation and irrigation play a significant part in achieving the goal of maximal bacterial removal. However, other studies have demonstrated that clinical attainment of negative cultures is not predictable, even after several appointments.27,38,104

In an early study by Ingle and Zeldow,67 which was performed without using anaerobic culturing techniques, 80% of the initially infected root canals yielded positive cultures immediately after instrumentation and irrigation with sterile water, and this number increased to 95% by the beginning of the second appointment 48 hours later. Using approaches to recover and cultivate anaerobic bacteria, Byström and Sundqvist27 measured bacterial counts after instrumentation of root canals of teeth with apical periodontitis using stainless steel hand instruments and saline for irrigation. Root canals

In another clinical study of teeth with apical periodontitis, Dalton et al38 compared intracanal bacterial reduction after instrumentation with either 0.04 tapered nickel-titanium (NiTi) rotary instruments or stainless steel K-type instruments using saline as the irrigant. Both

### Chemical action

As discussed above, mechanical preparation can achieve considerable bacterial reduction. Nevertheless, the outcome of treatment without chemical disinfection would be quite unpredictable due to the number of residual bacteria remaining in the canal when saline is the irrigant. The residual bacterial counts can exceed the entombment ability of root canal obturation (see discussion below) and may be large enough to jeopardize the outcome of root canal treatment. Therefore, irrigants with antibacterial properties must be used to achieve maximal bacterial elimination during root canal preparation.

In early clinical studies, investigators found negative cultures in more than 70% of the initially infected root canals treated by chemomechanical preparation with antibacterial irrigation.14,199

However, the fact that they did not use stringent protocols for sample collection or any methods for detection of anaerobic bacteria somewhat diminishes the impact of their results.

Following the concept of apical periodontitis as an infectious disease, and based on the studies reported in this section, the most important property of a substance used as an irrigant must be its antibacterial effectiveness. While tissue-dissolving capacity (NaOCl) and antimicrobial substantivity (CHX) are very interesting properties, these and other properties should be subordinate to antibacterial efficacy. While NaOCl should remain the irrigant of choice in root canal therapy, CHX represents a potential substitute with similar antibacterial potency. Regardless of concentration, large volumes and frequent exchange of irrigant solutions are required for optimal antibacterial activity.

A higher irrigant volume is used as the canal is enlarged to greater sizes.
The irrigant remains in the canal longer as the canal is enlarged.
The larger the preparation, the higher the volume of irrigant filling the canal, increasing the chances of improved chemical effects.

### Single-file instrumentation systems

The use of a single-instrument approach to prepare root canals was proposed about 10 years ago.229 Since then, numerous systems have been introduced that have, on the whole, been greatly accepted by clinicians. Examples include the Reciproc (VDW, Munich, Germany),
WaveOne (Dentsply Sirona, Tulsa, OK, USA), and One Shape (Micro
Méga, Besançon, France) systems. Many of these instruments are operated in reciprocating motion. Other single-file systems such as the self-adjusting file (SAF, ReDent Nova, Ra’anana, Israel) and XPendo Shaper (FKG Dentaire, La Chaux-de-Fonds, Switzerland) have a special design and features and are discussed in the next section.

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 514 -->

One reason for concern with the use of single-file systems for root canal preparation is that there is a theoretical risk of reducing antibacterial efficacy. Once the preparation time is substantially reduced using these systems, the antibacterial irrigant will consequently remain for a shorter period of time in the canal. In addition, as there are no frequent instrument changes and the single instrument is removed from the canal only a few times during its advance in the apical direction, the volume and renewal of the irrigant can also be reduced. Retention time and irrigation volume are important factors that influence the antibacterial activity of NaOCl.
However, ex vivo8,17,81,84,97,187 and in vivo31,86,87,98 studies showed that single-file systems can show antibacterial performance similar to multifile systems (Fig 11-10). A common condition in all studies that showed similarity in antibacterial efficacy between single-file and multifile preparations was that high and equal volumes of irrigants were used. This was clearly demonstrated in an ex vivo study by
Gazzaneo et al,56 who evaluated the bacterial reduction promoted by chemomechanical preparation using the
Reciproc single-file technique, varying the volume, concentration, and retention time of
NaOCl irrigation in comparison with the BioRaCe multifile system. The teeth were anatomically matched based on micro-CT and the canals contaminated with a fresh mixed bacterial culture. The only significant difference observed was between the multifile group and the
Reciproc group with lower irrigant volume. They concluded that the concerted effects of multiple instruments, the high volume of irrigation, and the long retention time of NaOCl had a positive influence on intracanal disinfection.

### Minimally invasive endodontics

Recently, the concept of minimally invasive endodontics (MIE) was introduced. The main purpose of MIE is to promote minimal change to the dental hard tissues during root canal treatment in order to improve the long-term survival and function of treated teeth.34 The two most important aspects of MIE refer to the size of apical preparation and the size and design of endodontic access cavities.
The effects of a small apical preparation on bacterial reduction have already been discussed in the previous sections, with evidence showing that the best disinfecting effects and treatment outcomes are attained with large preparation sizes.

As for the size and design of coronal access preparations, contracted cavities have been recommended to preserve hard tissue structure and reduce the risks of tooth fracture and loss following root canal treatment. Results from studies evaluating the influence of minimally invasive access cavities on the fracture resistance of root canal-treated teeth and the shaping ability of instrumentation are rather inconclusive.2,15,37,73,94,105,126,146,222 A systematic review of ex vivo studies concluded that contracted access cavities do not significantly increase tooth fracture resistance.158

There are no outcome studies that support the alleged benefits of using contracted cavities and only a few studies evaluating their effects on root canal disinfection. A laboratory study by Barbosa et

### Role of intracanal medication

Although NaOCl is a potent and rapidly acting disinfectant, its in vivo performance is still somewhat disappointing in view of the significant potential it has shown in vitro. Chemomechanical preparation using
NaOCl at different concentrations does not suffice to predictably render root canals free of detectable bacteria. Culture studies have demonstrated that 20% to 60% of the root canals still contain detectable levels of bacteria after preparation using NaOCl as the irrigant.25,64,74,88,141,157,170,173,189,193,223 Studies using more sensitive molecular biology methods, which can also detect difficult-to-grow and as-yet-uncultivated bacteria, show bacterial persistence in 30% to 70% of the cases.13,98,106-108,142-144,232 CHX has been proposed as an alternative irrigant, but clinical studies showed that it is not superior to

### Role of root canal obturation

Endodontic treatment serves not only to control infection but also to prevent further reinfection of the root canal after treatment. Herein

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 533 -->

resides the importance of root canal obturation and coronal restoration. Obturation serves to entomb bacteria remaining in some specific areas such as dentinal tubules and untouched canal walls, cut off substrate delivery to residual bacteria, and eliminate empty spaces that would be highly conducive to bacterial reestablishment (e.g. in the event of leakage through a coronal restoration). A properly placed and adapted coronal restoration provides a tight antibacterial seal that helps to prevent bacteria in the saliva from recolonizing the root canal. Therefore, both root canal obturation and coronal restoration play an important role in preventing reinfection (or infection of vital cases).

Although subject to speculation, it is highly questionable whether root canal obturation alone plays a relevant role in combating infection attributable to the ecological (entombment) or chemical effects (antibacterial effects of gutta-percha and sealer). One of the goals of obturation is certainly to ensure entombment of bacteria in the canals by the root canal filling. It has been argued that a technically wellperformed root canal filling can entomb bacteria in the canal, denying them access to the periradicular tissues. This is especially applicable to bacteria remaining on the root canal walls or within dentinal tubules (Fig 11-21). However, bacteria can sustain long-term infections if they remain in the very apical part of the root canal, in apical ramifications, or in lateral canals. In these areas, bacteria cannot be entombed by obturation as they are in direct contact with the periradicular tissues, with access to a sustainable source of nutrients, and can maintain periradicular inflammation and impair healing (Fig 11-22). The fact that bacteria-positive root canals are associated with a significantly worse outcome45,49,60,193,204,223,231 also indicates that entombment is unpredictable and unreliable. This is further confirmed by the findings from a myriad of studies showing that no filling material or technique can predictably seal the root canal.19,32,41,59,71,82,129,155,168,180,182,186,208

## One-visit versus two-visit treatment

One-visit endodontic treatment offers some advantages to both the clinician and patient. In addition to being faster and very well accepted by patients, it may prevent the risk of infection or reinfection of the root canal system between appointments. If the pulp is noninfected (vital), the treatment should ideally be completed in one session if certain conditions are met (adequate time available, sufficient operator skills, favorable anatomical conditions, etc). On the other hand, treatment or retreatment of infected teeth with apical

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 541 -->

periodontitis in one session is one of the most controversial issues in endodontics.18,79,212

Despite anecdotal rationale for accomplishing endodontic therapy in a single visit, two factors must be taken into account before recommending one-visit treatment of teeth with necrotic pulp: the incidence of postoperative pain and the long-term treatment outcome.
Numerous investigators found no difference in the incidence of postoperative pain between one-visit and multiple-visit endodontics.43,44,51,66,211 However, a systematic review showed that patients undergoing a single-visit treatment may experience a slightly higher frequency of swelling and are significatively more likely to take painkillers in the first week following treatment.52 Consequently, the long-term outcome of endodontic treatment should be the main factor taken into account when deciding the number of treatment sessions.
The ability of treatment to create conditions conducive to healing is the determining factor of optimal outcome. The absence of microorganisms in the root canal is the best environmental condition conducive to periradicular healing. This condition resembles that found in teeth with vital pulp and has a very high potential for success.
Therefore, clinicians should pursue treatment protocols that predictably control root canal infection and establish an environment favorable to healing.

The answer to which approach is better can only be given by longterm outcome studies represented by randomized trials or prospective cohort studies. However, there is a lack of well-controlled clinical studies comparing the success rates of one-visit versus multiple-visit endodontic treatment of teeth with apical periodontitis.
Most available studies are plagued by a small sample size and/or other potential biases such as operator skills, proper randomization, evaluation time, apical enlargement, apical periodontitis lesion size, criteria for outcome categorization, lack of detailed information, etc.

## Ecological considerations

The concept of biofilm communities as the unit of pathogenicity for apical periodontitis allows for better understanding of the effects of antimicrobial treatment and opens new perspectives for ecology driven treatment.176
In addition to the direct effects of chemomechanical preparation and interappointment medication, ecological effects may also play an important role in infection control.
Ecological interference is any event that significantly disturbs an ecosystem, affects biologic communities, and compromises their survival. Eradication of key elements in a community represents an ecological disaster for the community, which could ultimately result in death of the remaining members. Key components include species involved in cross-feeding and acquisition of essential nutrients for less competent species, species that modify the environment and promote the establishment of others, species producing protective enzymes that degrade antibiotics and host defense molecules, etc. Apart from eradicating bacteria, endodontic treatment affects nutrient availability.
Chemomechanical preparation and intracanal medication serve to eliminate existing nutrient sources (necrotic pulp tissue and bacterial partners), and obturation prevents the further supply of nutrients (by sealing the canal and preventing protein-rich tissue fluids and/or saliva from seeping into it).

On the other hand, eliminating certain members of the community may make some niches available to even more virulent or resistant species that may previously have been absent or present in low numbers. Depending on numerous factors, this change in species composition and/or proportion could alter the overall properties of the community, including species dominance, opening the door to a more robust and/or more virulent species. This may predispose the tooth to an unfavorable treatment outcome or to flare-ups.

Thus, partial elimination of the community could lead to death (good outcome) or reorganization of the community (bad outcome).

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 546 -->

To avoid community reorganization, chemomechanical preparation (preferably completed in a single visit) supplemented by intracanal antimicrobial medication is recommended for maximal and broadrange elimination of the endodontic bacterial community.64,88,107,157,173,174,194 Obturation serves to eliminate or reduce space for reorganization of the community and to prevent further nutrient supply to the surviving bacteria. Above a certain bacterial threshold, and depending on the location of residual infection, obturation cannot successfully entomb the bacteria that escaped the previous procedures.49,93,188,193,204,223,231 Therefore, the effects on the community have to be maximized so as to reduce bacterial populations significantly before a root canal filling is placed.188

Essentially, the current treatment paradigm is to eliminate as many members of the bacterial community as possible and to deny substrate to those that remain. Endodontic researchers have tirelessly looked for protocols and techniques that promote total bacterial eradication or a perfect apical or coronal antibacterial seal, but no plausible conceptual alternative has yet been devised or proposed. Although the success rate of endodontic treatment is satisfactorily high following the existing paradigm under controlled conditions,54,70,100,136,190,195,200 the overall success rate in the general population is alarmingly low.42,72,96,109,153,179,203,206,226 This may be due mainly to technical difficulties, since the high failure rates are related to substandard treatment conditions.96,101,179,210 The ecology and virulence of endodontic communities, host responses, or possible disease modifiers may be potential therapeutic targets for interference strategies in future research, especially if associated with less complex treatment technologies.176

After completion of access preparation under rubber dam isolation, copiously irrigate the pulp chamber with 2.5% NaOCl solution.
Perform chemomechanical preparation using a crown-down technique, hand and/or rotary instruments, and abundant irrigation with 2.5% NaOCl after each instrument size (at least 2 ml of solution following each instrument used). NiTi instruments should be used in curved root canals. The root canal should be enlarged to within 1 mm short of the apical foramen with instrument sizes sufficiently large enough to maximize disinfection but which, at the same time, do not weaken the root.
Overinstrumentation is undesirable as it can predispose the tooth to postoperative problems and treatment failure. However, the 1mm apical segment should ideally be cleaned and maintained free of debris by using small-size patency instruments.
To improve irrigant delivery and exchange in the apical canal, use small needles that reach the working length (if side-vented needles are used) or 2 to 3 mm short of the working length (if open-ended needles are used).
Remove the smear layer by using EDTA or citric acid, followed by NaOCl. The smear layer can harbor bacteria, prevent or delay the antibacterial effects of medicaments deep within the dentin and in ramifications, and interfere with the sealing provided by root canal obturation.
Next, perform an intravisit supplementary disinfection step using one of the following: ultrasonic activation of NaOCl by a freeoscillating instrument, mechanical activation of NaOCl using a finishing instrument, or final rinse with 2% CHX. If the last approach is used, it is important to irrigate the canal with a substance such as absolute alcohol, EDTA, or saline between the use of NaOCl and CHX to avoid pigment formation.
