## Bacterial identification in endodontic infections

Identification of the microorganisms associated with any infectious disease is of paramount importance to establish proper strategies for prevention and treatment. It is important to point out that virtually all studies of the endodontic microbiota in humans were cross-sectional in nature; as a consequence, the results can infer association of certain species with disease, but not causation. Therefore, the microbial species/groups detected in high prevalence in endodontic infections are commonly regarded as candidate or putative endodontic pathogens. This category is further strengthened when the species is also involved with infections in other body sites and is shown to be pathogenic in animal studies.

Endodontic microbiology studies that performed microbial identification have been divided into five generations on the basis of chronology and the methods used.124,125 (Fig 5-1):

### 1st generation (culture methods)

Studies of this generation were based on open-ended culture methods, which permitted the isolation and identification of the cultivable microbial species occurring in the root canal. Findings from these studies revealed many cultivable species highly associated with apical periodontitis, including Fusobacterium nucleatum, Prevotella species,
Porphyromonas species,
Parvimonas micra,
Pseudoramibacter alactolyticus, and
Streptococcus species.
Enterococcus faecalis is also an example, but is found in persistent/secondary infections (see Chap 6).

Studies from the 2nd generation started the era of molecular microbiology methods in endodontic research. All the other generations are based on these technologies. The studies of this generation used closed-ended species- or group-specific DNA-based molecular methods, targeting cultivable bacterial species. The methods commonly used were the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and its derivatives as well as the conventional checkerboard hybridization approach.
Findings from
2nd-generation studies strengthened the association of some cultivable species with endodontic infections because they were detected in even higher prevalences due to the higher sensitivity of the molecular methods when compared with culture. Moreover, several difficult-to-cultivate species that are important periodontal pathogens such as Tannerella forsythia, Dialister species, Filifactor alocis, and Treponema species were detected for the first time in endodontic infections and in high frequencies, allowing them to be included in the list of candidate pathogens of apical periodontitis.

### 3rd generation (open-ended molecular methods)

Studies of this phase involved open-ended DNA-based molecular studies to detect virtually all species in the sample, including not only cultivable named species but also as-yet-uncultivated phylotypes. The most commonly used technologies included broad-range PCR followed by cloning and Sanger sequencing, terminal-restriction fragment length polymorphism (T-RFLP) or denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis (DGGE). As these molecular methods are laborious, time consuming, and expensive, most studies from this generation were restricted to a few samples. Nonetheless, findings lent a substantial contribution to the knowledge of endodontic infections because they allowed researchers to set a catalogue of the species and phylotypes present in endodontic infections. This, along with information of the bacterial diversity in other oral sites, set the stage for the next phase.

### Acute forms of apical periodontitis

Symptomatic (acute) apical periodontitis and acute apical abscesses are the typical acute forms of apical periodontitis. While an acute abscess is usually preceded by symptomatic apical periodontitis, the latter does not necessarily evolve into the former. Therefore, the acute abscess can be regarded as an advanced stage of the acute disease. The transition from acute apical periodontitis to abscess can make it difficult to clinically distinguish these two conditions, but, in later stages of the disease, the diagnosis of acute abscess is usually

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 273 -->

not a difficult task, mostly because of swelling. In symptomatic cases, the infection is located in the root canal but may also have reached the periradicular tissues; in abscessed cases, it has the potential to spread to other anatomical spaces of the head and neck to form cellulitis (Fig 5-9).

The microbiota involved with abscess formation is mixed and dominated by anaerobic bacteria.11,41,44,61,84,92,109,121,125 Bacterial counts have been reported to range from 104 to 109 colony forming units per abscess.41,47,149 The mean number of species is higher in abscesses than in canals of teeth with asymptomatic (chronic) apical periodontitis.92,95,121,132 As-yet-uncultivated phylotypes encompass approximately 40% of the taxa found in abscesses and collectively represent more than 30% of the clones sequenced.92

Thus far, no strong evidence of the specific involvement of a single species with any particular sign or symptom of apical periodontitis has been disclosed. Some Gram-negative anaerobic bacteria have been suggested to be involved in symptomatic lesions,27,28,81,92,109,132,139,150 but the same species may also be present in somewhat similar frequencies in asymptomatic cases.5,23,29,38,80,122,123 Therefore, factors other than the mere presence of a given putative pathogenic species may play a role in the etiology of symptomatic endodontic infections.99,102,125 These factors possibly include (Fig 5-10):

The factors that lead to differences in the composition of the endodontic microbiota remain elusive. As for therapeutic implications, because the main goal for endodontic treatment is to use nonspecific antimicrobial strategies to eliminate all the bacterial populations inside the canal system, regardless of the type of species present, the geographic differences may have no impact on the treatment outcome. However, with regard to the treatment of some cases of abscesses that require systemic antibiotics with a spectrum of antibacterial specificity, the existence of geographic differences in bacterial diversity may result in some antibiotics being effective in some countries but not in others. There is a need for further studies to elucidate this issue.

### The apical root canal microbiota

Bacteria located in the apical root canal are in a strategic position to induce damage to the periradicular tissues. The bacterial species occurring in this region are potentially the most important ones participating in the causation of apical periodontitis. Products released from the bacterial biofilm in the canal accumulate and reach the periradicular tissues, giving rise to an inflammatory response that ultimately leads to the destruction of the periodontal ligament and bone. As early as 1894, Miller emphasized that the morphology of the apical microbiota is different from that of the most coronal microbiota.53 Indeed, the apical microbiota has been demonstrated to differ significantly from that in the more coronal aspects of the canal (intraindividual analysis) in terms of predominant morphotypes,136

bacterial community profile,1,62 and anaerobe:facultative ratio.17 The apical root canal microbiota is predominantly anaerobic and the time of infection can influence this dominance. For example, anaerobes comprise the large majority of isolates in the late stages of infection.2,17

## Anatomy of infection – spatial distribution

### Patterns of bacterial colonization and biofilms

Like caries and marginal periodontitis, apical periodontitis is a biofilminduced disease. Morphologic studies have shown that the root canal microbiota in primary infections is dominated by bacterial morphotypes, including cocci, rods, filaments, and spirilla (spirochetes) (Figs 5-16 and 5-17).57,97,117 Bacteria colonizing the root canal system usually grow in sessile biofilm communities adhered to the dentinal walls, but also occur as aggregates (cells with the same morphology), coaggregates (cells with distinct

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 282 -->

morphologies), and planktonic cells suspended in the fluid phase of the main canal (Figs 5-18 and 5-19).57,117 Apical ramifications, lateral canals, isthmuses, and recesses of the canal system can be clogged with bacteria primarily organized in biofilms (Fig 5-20).8,73,75

Occasionally, they can also reach the apical foramen/foramina and extend to the outer root surface, forming an extraradicular biofilm.21,72,137

The strong association of biofilms with apical periodontitis was only revealed by Ricucci and Siqueira74 in a comprehensive histobacteriologic study in untreated and treated teeth with apical periodontitis. Intraradicular biofilms were detected in the apical canal of 80% of untreated teeth and 74% of treated teeth. Intraradicular biofilms were usually thick and multilayered, and the amount of the extracellular matrix in relation to the cell content varied substantially from case to case.

These authors also reported that teeth with large lesions and histopathologically diagnosed cysts had a higher prevalence of biofilms in the apical canal.74 This can be explained by the fact that large and cystic lesions are usually long-standing pathologic processes caused by a long-standing infection that had sufficient time to organize itself as a mature biofilm. Some studies have observed that the endodontic treatment outcome can be influenced by the size of apical periodontitis.59,130,148 This may be justified by the difficulty to properly control a complex infection with more bacterial cells and species and usually organized in biofilms in the apical canal segment.74,80,132

## Bacterial ecology and the root canal ecosystem

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 294 -->

A root canal containing necrotic pulp tissue provides space for bacterial colonization and affords bacteria a moist, warm, nutritious, and anaerobic environment, which is by and large protected from the host defenses because of the lack of active blood circulation in the necrotic tissue. Moreover, root canal walls are non-shedding surfaces conducive to persistent colonization and the formation of complex communities. Intuitively, the necrotic root canal might be considered a rather fertile environment for bacterial growth, and it might be realized that colonization should not be a difficult task for virtually any oral bacterial species. Although a large number of bacterial species (about 100 to 200) can be found in the oral cavity of a given individual,65 only a few of these species (about 10 to 30) are consistently selected for growth and survival within a necrotic root canal in the same individual. This indicates that ecological determinants operate in the necrotic canal and dictate which species will succeed in colonizing this previously sterile environment. The major ecological factors that determine the composition of the root canal microbiota include oxygen tension, the type and amount of available nutrients, and bacterial interactions. Other factors such as temperature, pH, and receptors for adhesins may also be involved.

Root canal infection is a dynamic process, and different bacterial species apparently dominate at different stages of the infectious process. Shifts in the composition of the microbiota are largely due to changes in environmental conditions, particularly with regard to oxygen tension and nutrient availability. In the very early phases of the pulpal infectious process, facultative bacteria predominate.17 After a few days or weeks, oxygen within the root canal is depleted as a result of pulp necrosis and consumption by facultative bacteria.
Further oxygen supply is interrupted because of a loss of blood circulation in the necrotic pulp. Therefore, an anaerobic milieu highly conducive to the survival and growth of obligate anaerobic bacteria develops. With the passage of time, anaerobic conditions become even more pronounced, particularly in the apical third of the root canal. Consequently, anaerobes begin to dominate the microbiota, outnumbering facultative bacteria (Fig 5-23).

The main sources of nutrients for bacteria colonizing the root canal system include:

necrotic pulp tissue and cells;
proteins and glycoproteins from tissue fluids and exudates that seep into the root canal system via apical and lateral foramina;
saliva components that may coronally enter the root canal;
products of the metabolism of other bacteria.

The largest supply of nutrients is available in the main canal, which is the most voluminous part of the root canal system. Therefore, most of the infecting microbiota, particularly fastidious anaerobic species, are expected to be located in this region. Bacterial species that can best utilize and compete for nutrients in the root canal system will succeed in colonization.

In addition to being influenced by variations in oxygen levels, population shifts in the microbiota colonizing the root canal system can also be dependent upon the dynamics of nutrient utilization.
Saccharolytic species dominate in the very early stages of the infectious process but are later outnumbered by asaccharolytic and proteolytic species, which dominate in the more advanced stages.133,135 Necrotic pulp tissue can be regarded as a finite source of nutrients to bacteria because the small volume of tissue is gradually degraded and consumed.
However, induction of periradicular inflammation guarantees a sustainable source of nutrients, particularly in the form of proteins and glycoproteins present in the exudates that seep into the canal. At this stage of the infectious process, bacteria that have a proteolytic capacity or cooperative interaction with those that can utilize this substrate in the metabolism start to dominate. Therefore, as the infectious process reaches the stage of induction of periradicular inflammation, proteins become the principal source of nitrogen and carbon, particularly in the apical part of the canal, favoring the establishment of anaerobic species that utilize peptides and/or amino acids in their metabolism (see Fig 5-23).

As primary endodontic infections are usually characterized by mixed biofilm communities, different bacterial species are in close proximity with one another. Therefore, interactions are inevitable.
Thus, the establishment of certain species in the root canal is clearly influenced by interactions with other species (Fig 5-24). In this regard, early colonizers play an important role by dictating which species will live alongside them in the community. Bacterial interactions can be positive or negative. Positive interactions enhance the survival capacity of the interacting bacteria and enable different species to coexist in habitats where neither could exist alone. They include:

interbacterial nutritional interactions such as food chains/webs and concerted action to break down complex substrates (Figs 525 and 5-26);
local environment modification;
collective protection against external threats;
intrageneric and intergeneric coaggregations;
cell-cell signaling (quorum-sensing systems);
horizontal gene transfer.

Interbacterial nutritional interactions are of special interest as they represent important ecological determinants leading to a high metabolic diversity and efficiency of the whole community. Nutritional interactions are mainly associated with food chains/webs, including utilization of metabolic end-products from one species by another (see Fig 5-25) and bacterial cooperation for the breakdown of complex host-derived substrates (see Fig 5-26).

In addition, one species can create growth conditions favorable to another by reducing the oxygen tension in the environment, thus favoring the establishment of anaerobes (environment modification), or by releasing proteinases or antibiotic-inactivating enzymes that protect the entire community from host defenses and antibiotics, etc.

Many species adhere directly to host surfaces while others adhere to other bacteria already attached to the surface. The latter process, called coaggregation, is highly specific with regard to the partners involved.42 Coaggregation differs from aggregation in that the latter occurs between genetically identical cells.43 A given pair of species can attach to each other by means of specific receptoradhesin interactions, which are usually lectin-like interactions (attachment of a specific protein on the surface of one species to a specific carbohydrate on the surface of the other). Coaggregation can favor colonization of host surfaces and facilitate metabolic interactions between the partners. Coaggregation has been demonstrated in several pairs of bacterial taxa found in endodontic infections.40

Inversely, negative interactions act as feedback mechanisms that limit population densities. Examples include competition (for nutrients and space) and amensalism (when one species produces a substance that inhibits another species). Inhibitory substances include bacteriocins, hydrogen peroxide, enzymes, and short-chain fatty acids.

## Other microorganisms in endodontic infections

### Fungi

Fungi, especially Candida species, are eukaryotic microorganisms that may colonize the oral cavity. While the large majority of endodontic microbiology studies have failed to detect fungi in samples from primary intraradicular infections, there are some identification16,45,56,119 and morphologic117 studies that have occasionally detected them in low prevalence. However, a couple of molecular studies have found fungi in more primarily infected cases than previously reported,6,54,67 suggesting that they may be contaminants or low-abundance members of the intracanal microbial community that are inhibited by bacteria. Occurrence of fungi in persistent/secondary infections is discussed in Chapter 6.

### Archaea

Archaea represent one of the three primary evolutionary domains of life, distinct in their phylogenetic relations to members of the domains
Bacteria and Eucarya. Archaea comprise a highly diverse group of prokaryotes, distinct from bacteria. Members of this domain have been traditionally recognized as extremophiles, but some of these microorganisms were also recently found to thrive in nonextreme environments, including the human body. To date, no member of the
Archaea domain has been described as a human pathogen.
Methanogenic archaea have been detected in samples from subgingival plaque associated with periodontal disease.46

Several studies have failed to detect archaea in primary endodontic infections,83,85,114 while others have detected methanogenic archaea62,63,143 or archaeal proteins69 in a low prevalence. The only exception is a study that reported the occurrence of archaea in 1⁄4 of the primarily infected canals.141 The role of archaea in endodontic infections, if any, requires further clarification.

### Viruses

Viruses are not cells but inanimate particles composed of a nucleic acid molecule (DNA or RNA) and a protein coat. As they have no metabolism of their own, they need to infect living cells in order to use the machinery of the cells to replicate their viral genome. As viruses require viable host cells to achieve viral infection and replication, they cannot thrive in a root canal with necrotic pulp. Viruses have been reported to occur in the root canal only in noninflamed vital pulps of patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection.25 On the other hand, herpesviruses have been detected in apical periodontitis lesions where living host cells are present.19,20,87-89

It has been hypothesized that herpesviruses, especially human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), and possibly others, may be involved in the pathogenesis of apical periodontitis as a direct result of virus infection and replication or as a result of virally induced impairment of local host defenses, which might give rise to overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria in the very apical part of the root canal (Fig 5-27).129 Bacterial challenge emanating from the canals may cause an influx of virus-infected cells into the periradicular tissues. Reactivation of herpesviruses by bacterially induced tissue injury may evoke impairment of host immune response in the periradicular microenvironment, changing the potential of local defense cells to mount an adequate response to infectious agents.
Viruses may also stimulate a TH1 immune response, the arm of such a response mostly involved with defense against intracellular pathogens, which may negatively regulate the TH2 response against extracellular pathogens (most oral bacteria). Moreover, herpesvirusinfected inflammatory cells are stimulated to release proinflammatory cytokines.55,147

Occurrence of herpesvirus infection has been more frequently observed in symptomatic apical periodontitis lesions (Fig 5-28),87,89

including abscesses,9,19,20 large lesions,88,89 and lesions from HIVpositive patients.90 One study48 reported that EBV was associated with irreversible pulpitis and apical periodontitis lesions, but no associations with these clinical conditions were observed for HCMV, herpes simplex virus (HSV), or Varicella zoster virus (VZV) (Fig 5-29).
One should be aware that the mere presence of viruses in clinical samples does not necessarily mean that they play a role in pathogenesis. Several herpesviruses can be chronically present in the body, continuously infecting leukocytes. As these leukocytes accumulate in inflamed areas, the infecting viruses will also be present. Herpesvirus participation in disease etiology is suspected when high viral titers and/or viral RNA transcripts or proteins are detected in samples, or if a clinical condition improves after antiviral treatment.
