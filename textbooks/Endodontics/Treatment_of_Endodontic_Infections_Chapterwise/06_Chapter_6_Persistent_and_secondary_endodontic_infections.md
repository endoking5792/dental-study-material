According to the classification proposed by Siqueira in 2002,53 a persistent endodontic infection is caused by microorganisms that participated in a primary or secondary infection and somehow managed to resist intracanal antimicrobial procedures and endure periods of nutrient deprivation in treated canals. A secondary endodontic infection, in turn, should not be confused with a persistent endodontic infection as it is caused by microorganisms that were not present in the primary infection but were introduced in the root canal at some time after professional intervention (hence secondary to treatment).53

For the most part, persistent and secondary infections are clinically indistinguishable. Exceptions include infectious complications (e.g. apical abscess) arising after the treatment of noninfected vital pulps and cases in which apical periodontitis was absent at the time of treatment but emerged and was detected at a later follow-up visit.
Both situations are typical examples of secondary infections.

Both persistent and secondary infections are responsible for several clinical problems, including persistent exudation, persistent symptoms, interappointment flare-ups, and failure of endodontic treatment characterized by the occurrence of posttreatment apical periodontitis (Fig 6-1). No studies have consistently identified microorganisms associated with these clinical conditions, except for cases of treatment failure. Some reports suggest that non-oral bacteria may be involved in secondary infections and cause persistent exudation and/or symptoms.17,34,56,65 As for flare-ups, the evidence of a specific cause at the bacterial species level is weak, although some reports suggest that
Gram-negative anaerobes such as
Fusobacterium nucleatum and black-pigmented anaerobic rods (Porphyromonas and Prevotella species) may be involved (Fig 62).7,67

Secondary infections are caused by microorganisms that were not present in the canal before clinical intervention. They are thus secondary invaders that may have gained access to the canal during treatment, between appointments, or after obturation of the root canal. The main cause of microbial introduction in the canal during treatment is a breach in the aseptic chain, which can be caused by the following factors:

Remnants of dental plaque, calculus, or caries on the tooth crown;
Leaking rubber dam;
Contamination of the working parts of endodontic instruments, e.g. by touching them with the fingers;
Contamination of irrigants or other solutions for intracanal use (saline solution, distilled water, citric acid, etc).

Microorganisms can enter the root canal system between appointments via the following pathways:

Leakage through temporary restorative material;
Breakdown, fracture, or loss of a temporary restoration;
Fractured tooth structure;
Tooth left open for drainage.

Microorganisms can penetrate the root canal system after obturation of the root canal through the following mechanisms:

Leakage through temporary or permanent restorative material;
Breakdown, fracture, or loss of a temporary or permanent restoration;
Fractured tooth structure;
Recurrent caries exposing the root canal filling material;
Delayed placement of permanent restorations.

Oral or non-oral microorganisms may be involved, depending on the source of contamination. If contamination comes from saliva leakage into the canal, the microorganisms involved are normal oral inhabitants. On the other hand, if environmental microorganisms or bacteria from the intestinal or skin microbiota are the source of contamination, non-oral bacteria will be found. The presence of
Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus species, Escherichia coli, other enteric rods, Candida species, and Enterococcus faecalis within the canal is highly suggestive of a secondary infection because none of them is normally found in primary infections.17,33,34,46,56,65,73

In the clinical setting, it is commonly impossible to distinguish persistent from secondary infections. Exceptions include infectious complications arising after the treatment of noninfected vital pulps or cases in which apical periodontitis was absent at the time of treatment but developed later, to be detected on follow-up radiographs (Fig 6-3). These are typical examples of secondary infections.

Persistent or secondary intraradicular infections are the major causes of endodontic treatment failure (see Chap 7 for more details). This statement is supported by two strong evidence-based arguments:

There is an increased risk of adverse treatment outcome when bacteria are present in the canal at the time of filling.14,66,70,76

Most (if not all) root canal-treated teeth with posttreatment apical periodontitis lesions have been demonstrated to harbor an intraradicular infection.2,21,22,32,35,36,38,42,46,59,63,68

Based on these arguments, studies investigating bacteria persisting in root canals at the filling stage aim to identify species that have the potential to influence the treatment outcome (i.e. putting the outcome into perspective). On the other hand, studies dealing with the microbiota of root canal-treated teeth with apical periodontitis aim to demonstrate the association of specific species with treatment failure, because the microbial species detected are the cause of the persistent disease (outcome already established) (Fig 6-4). This chapter focuses on studies evaluating the microbiota associated with persistent/secondary infections and detected at the time of filling or during retreatment. Chapter 7 deals with these infections as the cause of treatment failure.

### Bacteria at the root canal-filling stage

Root canal samples taken at the time of treatment serve to assess the antimicrobial efficacy of treatment protocols and check the bacteriologic conditions of the canal before filling. Numerous clinical studies evaluating post-instrumentation or post-medication samples have shown that even diligent antimicrobial treatment may fail to completely eradicate bacteria from the infected root canal.3,5,6,9,10,15,26,27,39,43,45,52,55,57,58,66 This is because persisting bacteria are either resistant or inaccessible to treatment procedures.
Whatever the cause of persistence, bacterial diversity and density are substantially reduced after treatment. Root canal samples positive for bacteria after chemomechanical treatment with or without subsequent intracanal medication may contain a few bacterial species

As with primary infections, as-yet-uncultivated bacteria are also found in treated canals with posttreatment disease. In terms of richness, uncultivated phylotypes make up 55% of the taxa detected in treated canals. Uncultivated phylotypes can also be abundant, corresponding to about one-half of the 16S rRNA gene sequences retrieved in clone libraries.46 Some uncultivated phylotypes such as
Bacteroidaceae sp. HOT-272 (Bacteroidetes oral clone X083) can be among the most prevalent taxa in treated canals.46 As-yetuncultivated bacteria can also dominate the community, helping to explain why culture studies fail to detect bacteria in some treated root canals.

The bacterial community profiles in treated cases vary from individual to individual, indicating that distinct bacterial combinations can play a role in treatment failure.40,46 Like primary infections, persistent/secondary infections associated with treatment failure are also characterized by a mixed community, although it is much less diverse than in primary infections. Thus, bacterial mixed communities arranged in biofilms are a major cause of persistent/emergent disease (Fig 6-11).8,36,37,40,46,54

Candida species have been detected in root canal-treated teeth in up to 18% of cases.11,12,23,24,29,32,59,68 Fungi gain access to root canals via contamination during endodontic therapy (secondary infection) or overgrowth after inefficient intracanal antimicrobial procedures that cause an imbalance in the primary endodontic microbiota.62 Candida albicans is by far the most commonly detected fungal species in root canal-treated teeth. ## Microbiota in the apical part of teeth with

## posttreatment disease

Bacteria present in the apical part of the root canal system of treated teeth with persistent disease are unarguably involved with the treatment failure.36 There are not many studies evaluating the microbial species found exclusively in the apical root canal of teeth with posttreatment disease. To obtain samples exclusively from the apical canal is virtually impossible during nonsurgical retreatment, so the best approach to get such samples is to use extracted teeth or specimens obtained by root-end resection. Cryopulverizing the root apex specimens is the best way to detect bacteria, not only in the main canal but also in all areas of the apical root canal system, including dentinal tubules, recesses, isthmuses, and ramifications.
The first study to use the cryogenic grinding approach to sample the root canal system was published by Alves et al in 2009.1

Molecular microbiology analysis of cryopulverized apical root specimens from treated teeth with apical periodontitis disclosed highly complex bacterial communities.54 The composition of the bacterial communities varied significantly from one individual to another, i.e. there is a high interindividual variability. A molecular study evaluating cryopulverized apices of treated teeth revealed that the dominant bacteria belonged to four phyla, which accounted for approximately 80% of the species/phylotypes found: Proteobacteria,
Firmicutes, Fusobacteria, and Actinobacteria (Fig 6-13).54 The genera Fusobacterium and Pseudomonas were the most dominant, while Enterococcus was found in a few cases, always in a relatively low abundance. Another study using cryopulverization revealed that
Streptococcus species were highly prevalent (76%) in the apical canal of treated teeth, followed by members of the Actinobacteria phylum (52%) and P. alactolyticus (19%) (Fig 6-14).2 Another study confirmed the high prevalence and dominance of Actinobacteria and streptococci in the apical canal of teeth with posttreatment apical periodontitis, and demonstrated that the higher the volume of nonfilled canals, the higher the counts of these bacteria (Fig 6-15).63
