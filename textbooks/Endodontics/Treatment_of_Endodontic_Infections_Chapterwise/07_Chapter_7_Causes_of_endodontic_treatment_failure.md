According to most dictionaries, successful means “achieving your aims or what was intended.” If the main aims of endodontic treatment are to prevent apical periodontitis when it is absent or treat apical periodontitis when it is present, the detection of signs (radiolucency, sinus tract, swelling) and/or symptoms (pain) of the disease in root canal-treated teeth means that apical periodontitis has emerged, persisted, or recurred. This constitutes endodontic treatment failure, and the disease is referred to as posttreatment apical periodontitis.
Posttreatment disease can be emergent (developing after treatment), persistent (persisting despite treatment), or recurrent (redeveloping after having healed).

Endodontic treatment failure usually occurs when treatment procedures have not met satisfactory standards for prevention and control of endodontic infection, which is the cause of apical periodontitis (Fig 7-1). Numerous studies have demonstrated that most patients with teeth with posttreatment disease present with inadequate root canal treatment.11,24,56,66,80,103,118,128,132,141,144

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 349 -->

Therefore, it is easy to comprehend that the cause of persistent or emergent disease in these cases is inadequate prevention or management of root canal infection because of unsatisfactory treatment. Indeed, it has been shown that the larger the volume of unfilled (and possibly unprepared) areas in the apical segment of root canal-treated teeth with apical periodontitis, the higher the bacterial counts in that region (Fig 7-2).122 The clinician is often misled by the notion that procedural errors such as fractured instruments, perforations, overfilling, underfilling, ledges, etc are the direct causes of endodontic failure. However, procedural errors normally do not jeopardize the outcome of endodontic treatment unless a concomitant infection is present (Figs 7-3 and 7-4).49,107 Procedural errors often impede or make it difficult to properly accomplish the procedures required to achieve adequate control of endodontic infections. Thus, the potential for failure of endodontic treatment is significantly higher when a procedural error occurs during the treatment of infected teeth. For instance, a fractured instrument or a ledge may prevent instruments and irrigants from reaching the most apical part of the canal, allowing bacteria located in these areas to persist and apical periodontitis to be sustained (Fig 7-5).

Nevertheless, even well-treated teeth can fail (Figs 7-6 and 7-7).
Posttreatment disease has been reported to occur in 5% to 15% of teeth with preoperative apical periodontitis even when the treatment met acceptable standards.39,125,128,134 Irrespective of the quality of root canal treatment, the causes of failure are basically the same – bacteria usually involved in a persistent/secondary intraradicular infection and sometimes in an associated extraradicular infection.
Obviously, a poorly treated tooth has a much greater chance of treatment failure than a well-treated tooth because the chances of persistent or secondary infections are infinitely higher.

This chapter focuses on the microbial factors involved in the etiology of endodontic treatment failure, particularly in well-treated teeth. The best evidence shows that posttreatment apical periodontitis is primarily caused by microbial infection. In the past, nonmicrobial factors have also been suggested as potential causes of posttreatment disease, but assumptions were based on a few case reports using low-sensitivity diagnostic techniques.61,62 As this represents very low-level evidence, the concept of nonmicrobial factors as etiologic agents of treatment failure is questioned and will only be briefly discussed in this book.

If learning from our mistakes and those of others is helpful, then this chapter could be considered one of the most important in this book.

## Microbial causes of endodontic failure

Endodontic treatment failure is primarily a microbiologic problem.
Infection is present in virtually all cases of posttreatment disease.
Persistence of an apical periodontitis lesion after initial treatment of a tooth with preoperative disease can be caused by a persistent intraradicular infection, a secondary intraradicular infection, or an extraradicular infection. It is highly likely that in the large majority of cases with an extraradicular infection, the process is sustained by a concomitant intraradicular infection. An apical periodontitis lesion that emerges after the initial treatment of a tooth without preoperative disease is very likely to be caused by a secondary infection (Fig 7-8).

### Posttreatment apical periodontitis as an infectious

### problem

The reasons for the failure of an inadequate treatment are not difficult to understand. In most cases, bacterial infection persisted because the treatment was clearly ineffective (e.g. small and/or too short preparations, accidents, poor filling, etc). On the other hand, the failure of well-treated teeth is often intriguing. As mentioned above, the causes of failure are the same for poorly treated and well-treated teeth. Since microorganisms play an essential role in the pathogenesis of apical periodontitis, the reason for the failure of poorly treated teeth is obvious – they fail because the endodontic infection was not properly eradicated or controlled. However, if the treatment followed the highest acceptable standards, why do some cases still fail?

Most studies evaluating teeth with posttreatment apical periodontitis have demonstrated a very high association with the presence of bacteria in the canal.26,46,50,55,79,96,97,117,139 Many morphologic studies88,92,93 and case reports3,9,84 that examined root canal-treated teeth evincing apical periodontitis lesions reported the occurrence of residual bacterial infection in the apical part of the root canal system. Specifically, the infection was found on the main canal walls and/or within apical ramifications, lateral canals, isthmuses,

### Microbial persistence strategies

Endodontic infection control is achieved by mechanical, chemical, and ecological effects of endodontic treatment procedures.115 For bacteria to survive treatment they must a) resist intracanal disinfection procedures, and b) adapt to an environment that had its ecology drastically changed. Table 7-1 describes how bacteria specifically avoid the different effects of antimicrobial treatment.

Bacteria use several strategies to resist treatment. The organization in biofilm communities confers important mechanisms for bacterial resistance and persistence after intracanal antimicrobial procedures.16 Moreover, bacteria settled in recesses, ramifications, isthmuses, and other irregularities are more likely to escape the effects of instruments (because of physical limitations) and irrigants (because of time constraints) used during chemomechanical procedures (see Figs 7-9 and 7-10).148 Some bacteria penetrate deep into dentinal tubules, enabling them to survive the action of instruments and substances used in the main root canal during treatment (Fig
7-12).31,111,148
Antimicrobial agents used in

In addition to evading treatment procedures, residual bacteria must successfully adapt to the new environment in order to cause persistent disease. A major environmental change induced by treatment is a dramatic reduction in nutrient availability. The fact that the huge majority of root canal-treated teeth with posttreatment apical periodontitis harbor an intraradicular infection indicates that microorganisms can in some way acquire nutrients within filled root canals.26,46,50,55,79,96,97,117,139

Virtually all microleakage studies have demonstrated that no root canal filling technique or material succeeds in achieving a completely fluid-tight coronal and apical seal of the root canal.29 Therefore, residual bacteria in the canal can derive nutrients from saliva (coronal leakage into the root canal) or from periradicular tissue fluids and inflammatory exudate (apical or lateral leakage into the root canal).107

### When residual bacteria influence treatment outcome

Although the presence of bacteria in the canal at the time of filling is a risk factor for posttreatment disease, some apical periodontitis lesions can heal even if the root canals have detectable bacteria before filling.20,127,157 The following explanations for healing in these cases have been proposed:123,157

Residual bacteria may die after filling because of the toxic effects of the filling material, lack of access to nutrients, or disruption of bacterial ecology.
The quantities and virulence of the residual bacteria may be below the critical threshold needed to sustain periradicular inflammation.
The residual bacteria may remain in a location with no access to periradicular tissues.

### Extraradicular infections

Conceivably, extraradicular infections can be either dependent on or independent of intraradicular infection (Fig 7-19).108 The dependent infection is maintained by intracanal bacterial populations that grow and are constantly leaving the root canal and moving to the periradicular tissues. If the clinician successfully manages the intraradicular infection, the extraradicular component can be controlled/eliminated by the host immune defenses. Independent extraradicular infections in turn are those that are no longer fostered by an intraradicular infection. As such, they do not respond to nonsurgical root canal treatment. ### Failure associated with overfilling

As discussed at the beginning of this chapter, procedural accidents are usually associated with posttreatment disease when there is concomitant infection. Overfilling warrants a little more discussion because there has been some controversy as to how apically extruded filling materials influence treatment outcome. The toxicity of root filling materials has been claimed to play an important role in posttreatment disease associated with overfilling.57 However, it has also been reported that the apical extent of root canal filling seems to have no correlation with treatment failure, provided infection is absent.8,46,50,87,127 Apart from paraformaldehyde-containing sealers or pastes, most of the materials used for root canal obturation are either biocompatible or have cytotoxicity only prior to setting.4,30,64,130,131 Therefore, it is highly unlikely that any of the contemporary endodontic materials alone would have the potential to sustain periradicular inflammation when overfilled in the absence of a concomitant endodontic infection (Fig 7-23). ## Nonmicrobial causes of endodontic failure

Sophisticated molecular biology techniques have demonstrated that most, if not all, cases of root canal treatment failure are associated with intraradicular or extraradicular infection. However, a theory based on a few case reports has suggested that some lesions may fail to heal because of endogenous or exogenous nonmicrobial factors.59,60 Endogenous nonmicrobial causes of posttreatment apical periodontitis purportedly include cholesterol crystals and true cysts, and the exogenous nonmicrobial causes are presumably foreign body reactions caused by extruded endodontic filling materials59,60,93 and cellulose-containing particles from paper points or food.41,106 This theory of a nonmicrobial etiology for posttreatment apical periodontitis is consistently questioned nowadays, especially due to the lack of solid scientific support.

### Exogenous factors

A foreign body reaction to the cellulose component of paper points, cotton wool, and some food materials of vegetable origin has been suggested as a possible cause of posttreatment apical periodontitis.41,106 Cellulose, a stable polysaccharide of plant cell walls, is neither digested by humans nor degraded by their defense cells. Therefore, when introduced in human tissues, cellulose may remain there long enough to elicit a foreign body reaction. Paper points or particles thereof can be dislodged or pushed into the periradicular tissues, inducing a foreign body giant cell response or sustaining an existing apical periodontitis lesion. The same can occur with cotton wool. Food particles of vegetable origin (containing cellulose) may be introduced and packed in the root canals of teeth with grossly damaged crowns, teeth that have been left open for drainage, or teeth that had their root canals exposed due to the loss

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 398 -->

of a temporary restoration. During root canal instrumentation of these teeth, the food particles may be inadvertently pushed into the periradicular tissues. In all these circumstances, it is virtually impossible to preclude the possibility of a concomitant infection influencing the outcome.

### Endogenous factors

### Cholesterol crystals

It has been suggested that cholesterol crystals can evoke foreign body reactions and contribute to the development of non-resolving chronic inflammation (Fig 7-26).61 Cholesterol crystals are believed to precipitate and accumulate as they are released from disintegrating host cells. They may also originate from circulating plasma lipids. If multinucleated giant cells are ineffective in removing the crystals, they continue to accumulate – cholesterol crystals have been suggested to maintain apical periodontitis by this mechanism. Evidence for the direct involvement of cholesterol crystals in endodontic failure is

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 402 -->

lacking, and such an involvement is rather speculative. No association of cholesterol crystals with posttreatment apical periodontitis was observed in a study evaluating several cases of endodontic treatment failure.93 It is remarkable that numerous reviews and large parts of book chapters have been devoted to discussing the involvement of cholesterol crystals in treatment failure based on only a few case reports and very low-level evidence.

### True apical cysts

Apical cysts can be classified as “true” or “bay” (also “pocket”) depending on the relationship of the epithelium-lined cavity with the apical foramen/foramina.62,105 The true cyst has a completely independent cavity that does not communicate with the apical foramen. The bay cyst, in turn, has a cavity in direct communication with the root canal system via the apical foramen (Fig 7-27).

The true cyst has been considered to be a self-sustaining lesion that does not respond to nonsurgical endodontic treatment. This assumption was based on a prevalence study of untreated teeth105

and was further “supported” by a single case report.60 However, this belief has never been supported by solid scientific evidence and lacks biologic plausibility.45,47,88

The only case report used to “support” this theory was based on findings from culture and a correlative light and electron microscopic approach.60 In that study, no bacteria were found in the biopsy specimen by using those methodologies, which led the authors to conclude that the cause of lesion persistence was the fact that it was a true cyst. However, it is highly likely that bacteria may have passed unnoticed when using such low-sensitive methods. It is widely known that a negative culture does not mean that the root canal is sterile.
This is because the conventional sampling approach using paper points may not detect bacteria located in ramifications, recesses, isthmuses, and dentinal tubules,100 and culture methods may fail to detect bacteria occurring in low counts and/or those that are culture-

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 403 -->

difficult or uncultivated.116 In addition, while the correlated light and electron microscopic approach can provide detailed information from some selected areas of the biopsy specimen, it has a very low sensitivity to detect bacteria because of the small area that can be examined and the loss of tissue during processing. This was recognized by the authors themselves: “The extremely limited area that still can be covered by this means of investigation makes it rather easy for bacteria to go undetected.”58

In a comprehensive histopathologic and histobacteriologic study,
Ricucci et al88 compared the prevalence as well as several features of true and bay cysts in both untreated and root canal-treated teeth.
These lesions were diagnosed histopathologically using a meticulous research protocol as follows: All specimens of apical periodontitis lesions were obtained attached to the tooth apex and therefore maintaining their natural relationship with the root, and the whole of the lesions were serially sectioned. This protocol is crucial for an accurate diagnosis of cysts and their classification as true or bay types. The main clinical, radiographic, and histologic features of true and bay apical cysts were compared.

The study showed that intraradicular bacterial infection occurred in all specimens.88 Planktonic bacterial cells were detected in the main canal in all teeth, except for one bay cyst specimen in which bacteria were found in ramifications and in the extraradicular space. Biofilms were seen on the walls of the main apical canal in the large majority of specimens. Both types of cysts showed severe inflammation in response to bacteria located within the root canal and sometimes inside the lesion. The presence of a concurrent infection in all specimens questions the assumption that true cysts are selfsustainable pathologic entities.88

The fact that the cavity of true cysts has no direct communication with the root canal space does not indicate that true cysts will behave differently from bay cysts. The etiology of true and bay cysts is the same, i.e. intraradicular bacterial infection that causes periradicular inflammation and generates stimuli to epithelium proliferation and cyst formation. To be independent, the epithelium lining of the true cyst

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 404 -->

should behave like neoplastic cells, but there is no plausible reason or suggestive evidence for this to occur.45,47 In addition, findings from the study by Ricucci et al88 indicated that the cystic lesions associated with posttreatment apical periodontitis were always linked to concomitant bacterial infection and inflammation, confirming the infectious etiology of the disease.

The epithelial cells of apical cysts proliferate as a result of inflammatory hyperplasia that has ceased as long as inflammation has been resolved after effective bacterial control during root canal treatment. Without the proper signals for proliferation and survival, the epithelial lining of the cyst (true or bay) can be destroyed by the immune system142 or undergo apoptosis, with resolution of the cyst.47,48 There is no reason or evidence to believe that true cysts will act differently.

The study by Ricucci et al88 reported the occurrence of extraradicular bacteria in many cysts, either located within the cyst

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 406 -->

lumen or as a biofilm attached to the outer root surface near the apical foramina. In some cases, extraradicular bacteria were found within the cavity of true cysts. The presence of bacteria within the cavity of bay cysts can be explained by the direct advance of the infection from the main root canal. The explanation for bacterial presence in the cavity of true cysts can be a previous acute abscess episode, which, after resolution, left persistent bacteria in the periradicular tissues to maintain a chronic infectious process. This would be in line with the “abscess” theory for cyst formation.

Bacteria located within the cyst cavity are beyond the reach of nonsurgical antimicrobial treatment and may be difficult for the host defense responses to eliminate. Within the cyst cavity, bacteria are combated by host defense molecules and polymorphonuclear neutrophils that transmigrate through the epithelium into the cyst lumen. Due to the physicochemical conditions therein, the host defense mechanisms may not be effective in eliminating bacteria.
Persisting bacterial cells and their products within the cyst lumen may sustain periradicular inflammation even in teeth with well-treated root canals and be the cause of posttreatment disease.94

The study by Ricucci et al88 concluded that, except for the morphologic relationship of the cyst cavity with the root canal space, true and bay cysts have no significant differences in terms of clinical, radiographic, histopathologic, and histobacteriologic findings.
Therefore, is there a purpose for differentiating between these two cyst types? The findings of that study88 do not support the assumption that true cysts are self-sustainable entities not maintained by infection. Thus, regardless of type, it is reasonable to assume that cysts can heal provided that the inflammatory source of epithelial proliferation, i.e. the root canal infection, is properly controlled by diligent nonsurgical antimicrobial treatment.

Persistent disease following retreatment has essentially the same etiology as disease emerging or persisting after initial treatment. As for surgical treatment, persistent disease is usually related to the inability to eliminate or at least seal residual bacteria in the root canal system so as to deny them access to the periradicular tissues. Frank communication between residual bacteria and periradicular tissues may occur by the following mechanisms:21,90

Bacteria in apical ramifications and isthmuses may be in direct contact with the periradicular tissues, from which they may acquire nutrients to survive, proliferate, and maintain tissue aggression;
Infected dentinal tubules may be exposed after root-end resection and/or made patent by cementum resorption;
Poor sealing ability of the root-end filling material may allow leakage to occur, providing nutrients for remaining bacteria and creating a pathway through which bacteria and/or their products may reach the periradicular tissues and maintain inflammation (Fig 7-28);
If a root crack or fracture is present and communicates with the root canal or the gingival sulcus, it may become colonized by bacteria that cause or maintain inflammation, even in cases where the canal is apparently well sealed by the root-end filling.

As one can see, persistent intraradicular infection is also the main cause of postsurgical apical periodontitis.
