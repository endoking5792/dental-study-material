## Nature of the problem

Many epidemiologic studies have shown that a high percentage of root canal treatments show technical defects (e.g. incorrect length, ledges, straightening, perforations, inhomogeneity of obturation, defect coronal seal, remaining fractured instruments). In these studies, 40% to 65% of root canal-filled teeth were associated with radiographic signs of periradicular inflammation.35,40,61

On the other hand, controlled clinical studies have demonstrated that significantly higher success rates of up to 90% can be achieved (at least for vital cases with noninfected pulps).131 Obviously, there is a large discrepancy between the real potential and the clinical reality of root canal treatment.

## Terminology and definitions

There is still some controversy regarding the related terminology, reflecting different views of this pathology. Some of the terms found in the endodontic literature are treatment failure, non-healing, persistent pathology, refractory apical periodontitis, unfavorable outcome, and posttreatment disease. The authors of this chapter prefer to use the term “posttreatment disease,” which includes both persisting or secondary infection, whereas the term “treatment failure” may be interpreted (by colleagues or patients) as a failure of the dentist to choose and perform adequate (and successful) treatment of the primary infection.

Another debate is still ongoing regarding the definitions of success and failure. One of the earliest and most prominent classifications of the periapical status, known as the periapical index (PAI), was described by Ørstavik et al.95 Based on a histologic study on maxillary incisor teeth by Brynolf,16 the PAI includes five stages of

## Etiology of post-endodontic disease

Post-endodontic disease may have several different causes92,128,130

(this topic is discussed in detail in Chap 6). Probably, the majority of posttreatment disease is due to or associated with the persistence or development of a bacterial biofilm inside or even outside the root canal system. Nevertheless, clinically exact differential diagnosis can be challenging or even impossible.

So-called “technical problems” such as ledges, perforations, or instrument fractures per se do not cause inflammation or treatment failure, but these issues are nearly always associated with remaining biofilm and faulty disinfection. Consequently, these technical problems need to be addressed during retreatment in order to achieve improved disinfection.

## Persistent infection

Looking at the complexity of the root canal system, it is not really surprising that parts of the root canal wall – in some studies estimated to be 30% or more – are not touched by any instrument during root canal preparation, and that certain intricacies such as isthmuses, anastomoses between root canals, lateral canals, ramifications, or lateral extensions cannot be enlarged by instruments or properly disinfected by endodontic irrigants.60,129

The microbial community in such cases undergoes several modifications compared with pre-endodontic infection (as described in detail in Chap 7). A high number of microorganisms certainly will not have survived primary preparation and disinfection, which – when properly executed – has been shown in many studies to eradicate far

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 944 -->

more than 90% of microorganisms inside the root canal. Secondly, the prepared and obturated root canal presents a harsh environment, making survival impossible for a number of bacterial species. Fewer nutrients and less oxygen are available, and a less vulnerable and more resistant microbial community will establish.33,39,138 These microorganisms should be regarded as difficult to eradicate as they may be hidden in areas not accessible to instruments or irrigants. In addition, they would already have survived a first mechanical and/or chemical attempt at root canal disinfection, which will frequently be the use of highly concentrated solutions of sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), and they would have survived even with few nutrients and through phases of starvation. Therefore, finally, the composition of the microbiome in a root canal-treated tooth will be markedly different from that in untreated primary infections, and will consequently require a separate and modified treatment strategy and disinfection concept that is adapted to the specific situation (Fig 17-3).

## Secondary infection

Secondary infection may occur even in teeth with an optimally disinfected and obturated root canal system as a result of coronal leakage. It has repeatedly and consistently been shown that the quality of the coronal restoration is one of the most important and reliable predictors for success or failure of root canal treatment.94

To date, no study has compared the microbial biofilms in cases with persistent or secondary infections, but the low prevalence of
Enterococcus faecalis, Propionibacterium species, and Candida species in primary infections may point to the important role of these species in secondary infections. From a clinical point of view, it seems impossible to exactly distinguish between persistent and secondary infections for treatment failure; also, both could be present simultaneously.

In summary, it can be stated that:

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 945 -->

The persistence or development of apical periodontitis in root canal-filled teeth is due to the persistence of microorganisms after primary treatment or due to secondary infection via coronal leakage.
The microbial spectrum differs from that of primary endodontic infections, with fewer bacterial strains but probably a higher resistance against common endodontic disinfection protocols.
Consequently, nonsurgical retreatment should not merely involve exchanging root canal obturation materials and producing betterquality radiographs; it should rather comprise a repetition of the primary treatment, correcting the errors and shortcomings of that treatment.
The main goal of retreatment is regaining access to the apical foramen, preparation of previously uninstrumented areas, and disinfection of the endodontic system, which has either remained contaminated or has become recontaminated.

## Treatment decision making

Decision making in cases with post-endodontic disease has been claimed to be a rather complex process.1,79,80 Periapical health and disease should not be looked at as two different situations, but rather as a health continuum with different, individually defined cut-off points for the selection of one of the feasible treatment options, which stresses the important role of the patient in the decision-making process. A number of additional factors such as costs, experience and equipment of the operator, expectations of the patient, risk and expected benefit of the treatment, condition of the tooth (coronal, intraradicular, periapical, restorative, periodontal), prognosis, and personal values, among others, will also contribute to the final decision to some extent. It should be borne in mind that the final

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 946 -->

diagnosis is frequently influenced by, among other factors, the resulting treatment option, with its difficulties, potential problems, and prognosis. Thus, dentists may come to different, sometimes controversial, treatment decisions for the same case.5,63,106 In addition, the patient may be considering quite different, nonmedical personal factors and come to a completely different decision. Due to the lack of data, an evidence-based decision approach is not possible.79

Several treatment options exist for teeth with posttreatment disease:

Wait and see: This option is frequently preferred in clinically asymptomatic cases, but it has been shown that in 51% of such cases the lesion increases in size, in 28.6% it remains unchanged, and in some 20% of cases it decreases in size.145 As long as a connection between apical infection or inflammation and systemic health cannot be definitely ruled out,121 a “wait and see” approach should be adopted with caution. This option is not indicated in patients with severe systemic disease (e.g. kidney insufficiency); before chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or treatment with bisphosphonates; or before organ transplantation.

Nonsurgical retreatment: This option should be discussed mainly for patients with teeth that have an unaltered internal root canal anatomy, whereas for teeth with a severe violation of the original anatomy (i.e. severe straightening, perforations, ledging), surgical intervention can be considered as well as nonsurgical treatment.160

Surgical retreatment (apicoectomy): Surgical treatment, mainly apicoectomy, is recommended for root canal-filled teeth with a severely altered anatomy, when the coronal restoration cannot or will not be removed (massive posts, inacceptable costs for a new restoration, risks higher than expected benefits) (Fig 17-4), teeth or roots with inaccessible contaminated spaces, or when the reason for disease obviously (or presumably) is located outside the root in the periapical tissues (e.g. extraradicular infection). Nevertheless, even

these extraradicular infections will be associated with intraradicular bacterial contamination.

An interval of up to 4 years has been recommended before making a final decision regarding the success or failure of a primary treatment.
This is based on studies demonstrating that further healing occurs only infrequently after this time interval.96 More recently, a shorter observation period has been suggested.162 If a lesion has not healed or has not reduced in size after 1 year, retreatment should be considered. If a lesion has reduced in size, a further year of observation is advised, thus limiting the timeframe to 2 years until a final decision should be made regarding further management.

## Retreatment

When a decision is made for a nonsurgical retreatment approach, the prerequisites for success are complete removal of the previous obturation material down to the apical constriction in order to gain access to previously undetected, unprepared, and still infected areas of the root canal system; and to (re)-disinfect the complete root canal system using a protocol adapted to the specific microbiome of a nonhealing endodontic disease.

Major problems to be addressed are:

A complex root canal anatomy with probably unprepared and difficult-to-access spaces, including previously undetected root canals.
A probably altered root canal anatomy.
A bacterial community with a different (but unknown) composition compared with primary infections and the high probability of technical problems resulting from the primary treatment, which may prevent access to the most apical part of the root canal and challenge optimal (re-)disinfection.

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 950 -->

The main goals of retreatment are healing (or sometimes prevention) of apical periodontitis and maintaining the long-term functionality of the tooth. In view of the main reasons for posttreatment disease, retreatment should consequently aim at complete removal of the filling material so as to expose the maximal amount of infected root dentin to access as much of the previously non-accessed spaces inside the root canal system as possible in order to correct the preparation errors and improve the elimination of bacteria and biofilm. Thereafter, a bacteria-tight re-obturation and restoration is applied to prevent reinfection.

The primary goal of nonsurgical retreatment is not the exchange of obturation material but rather the repetition of the primary treatment, with the elimination of previous shortcomings as well as repeated and improved disinfection of the (re-)infected root canal system.

### Case selection

A thorough and strict case selection is the basis for a good retreatment success rate.41 The basic questions to be addressed are:

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 952 -->

1. Can the tooth be saved by nonsurgical retreatment and retained in the long term as a functioning unit or are the treatment alternatives (surgical treatment, extraction, placement of an implant) more promising?

Severe alterations of the original internal anatomy of the root (perforations, ledging, stripping, over-enlargement, straightening) (Fig
17-5) as well as expensive restorations (which might be damaged) or massive posts presenting a high risk of much dentin loss, perforation, or development of cracks can count against the decision for nonsurgical orthograde retreatment. On the other hand, it has been shown that the presence of a sufficient root canal filling is a major prerequisite for successful surgical treatment.112,168 The answer to this question is always subjective as there is little evidence on which to rely.

2. Does the respective dentist have the qualification, routine, knowledge, and equipment (microscope!) to handle the case adequately or would it be better for the patient to be referred to a specialist?

Except for a few “simple” retreatment cases where there are straight roots in single-rooted teeth, the use of a dental operating microscope and fine ultrasonic tips seems to be mandatory for the majority of contemporary retreatments. In certain selected cases,
CBCT will be necessary for treatment planning. The answer to this question also is based on individual assessment, and mainly requires a self-critical attitude. Again, sufficient evidence is lacking.

The American Association of Endodontists (AAE) has developed a case difficulty assessment form that allows the classification of cases into three different grades of expected difficulty.4 All teeth with previous root canal treatments at least fall into the group of moderate difficulty. The majority of retreatment cases presenting problems such as broken instruments, perforations, undetected root canals, or ledges probably can be regarded as treatments with a severe degree of difficulty, meaning that to achieve a predictably successful outcome will be “challenging for even the most experienced practitioner with an extensive history of favorable outcomes.” Referral to an

As it is not likely that infection will be restricted to only one root canal, retreatment of only one root with a clearly defined and limited apical lesion in a multirooted tooth is therefore not indicated. The radiographic absence of a lesion does not necessarily indicate the absence of an infection or inflammation. Consequently, a failed root canal treatment generally should be handled by retreatment of the entire root canal system. Nevertheless, in a strictly limited number of cases (high risk of worsening the situation by perforation, straightening, overpreparation, difficult-to-remove post, etc) even retreatment of a single root canal in a multirooted teeth may be advisable as a compromise (Fig 17-8). In such a case, the root canal orifices should be thoroughly inspected (intracoronal diagnosis) and probed for the quality of the root canal filling.

### Removal of intraradicular posts

Thorough analysis of the preoperative radiograph(s) is important for treatment planning. The size and location of the coronal access cavity must allow circumferential work around the post(s). Customized metal

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 958 -->

posts or screws can usually be removed using ultrasonic vibration.
Long, individually cast posts are more difficult to remove than prefabricated ones, mainly because of superior friction and the fact that softer metals do not transfer ultrasonic energy as well as prefabricated hard metal posts.58,62 Following removal of all filling material from the post down to the root canal orifice, an ultrasonic tip is worked with no more than 30% to 40% of maximal intensity around the post, in case of screws in a counterclockwise direction. Effective cooling, intermittent contact between ultrasonic tip and post, and a small amount of pressure are mandatory. Post removal can be a rather time-consuming procedure, with no loosening noticeable or visible for a long time (Fig 17-9). A self-defined time limit can help to avoid the removal of too much dentin and to timeously stop unsuccessful removal attempts. If a post has been loosened once, the procedure should be continued until complete removal; forceful attempts to remove a loosened post with pliers or forceps may result in the development of cracks.

Fiber posts have to be drilled out carefully without deviating from the axis of root and post, and without excessive removal of the coronal and radicular dentin.44,50 Post removal procedures are associated with some risk of deviation from the original axis of the root, heat transfer to the lateral periodontal tissues by ultrasonics, or lateral perforation. Additional intraoperative radiographs to control the axial orientation and get a rough impression of the remaining dentin thickness as well as careful use of ultrasonic tips may help to prevent or manage these problems. It should be borne in mind that apically of a removed post, it frequently occurs that a hard layer of luting materials such as dental (phosphate) cement or composite prevents further apical penetration. Small ultrasonic tips or long-shank burs can be helpful to remove these materials.

### Removal of root canal fillings

Microorganisms can be located on the gutta-percha, between the gutta-percha and sealer, between the sealer and the dentin, inside

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 959 -->

the dentinal tubules, and in previously uninstrumented areas.91

Therefore, the complete removal of the obturation material, both the gutta-percha and sealer, and the exposure of the dentin to irrigants and disinfectants should be strived for. Basically, gutta-percha can be removed using a number of different techniques and devices, including heat, solvents, hand instruments, and engine-driven NiTi instruments. Different proceedings are recommended for the removal of a single-cone obturation or compacted gutta-percha fillings. Singlecone obturations can often be removed quickly and effectively using a
Hedstrom file, which is worked carefully into the cone, which is then slowly pulled out in one piece (corkscrew technique). For compacted gutta-percha fillings, rotary or reciprocating NiTi instruments (especially those with a Hedstrom-file design) have repeatedly been shown to be effective and safe to use.32,56,113 For the straight, coronal part of the root canal, a Gates-Glidden drill is very effective and efficient to soften and penetrate the gutta-percha. Specially designed NiTi retreatment files can be used that frequently allow rapid progression to the apical part of the root canal. Such retreatment files should be used with the utmost caution, as most of them have a cutting tip, which may easily create ledges and also push the softened gutta-percha in an apical direction, or even through the apical foramen. Most NiTi retreatment systems are limited in size (25 or 30) and are not able to remove the gutta-percha completely, but rather create a “glide-path” through the bulk of the filling material.

### Untreated root canals

The number of root canals previously untreated and only detected during retreatment has been calculated in various studies to be 42%,
17%, and 23%.53,68,70 An additional root canal, mostly MB2, has been found in 81% of all root canal-filled maxillary molars,66 which, in case of a maxillary molar, may be associated with 20% to 25% of previously untreated and undisinfected root canal volume. As far as untreated root canals are concerned, 39% to 80% are associated with an apical lesion.11,18,66 During retreatment of maxillary molars,
10% more MB2 root canals were detected than during primary treatment of the same teeth.159 Therefore, an extensive search for additional root canals is mandatory in all retreatment cases following the law of exclusion: a tooth has only three root canals, unless a fourth can be detected by thorough inspection. Apical lesions located only at one single root tip and an eccentric position of the root canal filling inside the root may underline the need for an intensive search for a missed root canal (Fig 17-11).

### Determination of working length

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 964 -->

Ideally, endodontic working length, which should be defined as the most apical point of disinfection rather than of preparation or obturation, should extend to the most apically located microorganisms or piece of biofilm, but at least to the most apically located obturation material. In cases with overextension of the root canal filling, an attempt should be undertaken to completely remove even the overextended and probably contaminated gutta-percha (corkscrew technique using Hedstrom files). In most cases, overextended sealer can only be removed surgically. When the obturation is confined to the root canal, the apical constriction (minor foramen) is mostly recommended as the end point of preparation. Depending on the type of irrigation and activation of the irrigant, an “irrigation length” should be determined, i.e. 1 to 2 mm shorter than instrumentation for manual syringe irrigation or 2 mm for sonic or ultrasonic activation.

According to the literature, electronic determination of the endodontic working length in root canal-filled teeth is approximately
10% less exact and reliable than during primary treatment. Only 70% to 90% of measurements were within the apical 0 to 0.5 mm of the root, with a clear tendency for overextension of the file.2 This is probably because previously treated root canals no longer have a regular apical constriction due to previous preparation or apical resorption. The same holds true for endodontic motors with integrated electronic length measurement with up to
90% overinstrumentation.149 The electronic device can additionally be used for monitoring instrumentation: as long as no signal appears, the instrument will not have passed the gutta-percha, which initially serves as an isolator. When a signal appears, the gutta-percha has been passed, thus closing the electric circuit. A penetrant signal at mid-root or coronal root level can indicate a perforation or a root fracture.

### Management of ledges

Basically, ledges are the result of a deviation of the instrumentation from the original axis of the root canal. As ledges prevent regular preparation and disinfection of the apical part of the root canal, an attempt should be made to regain access to the apical constriction.
Ledges generally are located at the outer side of a curvature, whereas the inner side of the curvature remains anatomically unaltered, thus presenting a path to the apical foramen. Bypassing a ledge can sometimes be achieved using a small instrument with a carefully pre-curved tip, gliding along the root canal wall at the inner side of the curve (Fig 17-12). As ledges are frequently associated with (or are the consequence of) blockages, this attempt is not successful in all cases.81 If the working length cannot be reached, proper disinfection of the coronal part of the root canal is performed and development of the lesion controlled. Alternatively, surgical treatment will be necessary, also in the case of nonhealing.

### Management of perforations

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 967 -->

Perforations represent iatrogenic or resorptive interruptions of the integrity of the root, thereby creating communication between the root canal and the surrounding tissues. Intraradicular microorganisms can initiate inflammation and infection of the bone and periodontal ligament, and tissue fluids can enter the root canal and serve as nutrients for bacteria.

When accessible, perforations can be restored with a good prognosis using bioactive materials, preferably hydraulic calciumsilicate cements such as MTA (Dentsply/Maillefer, Ballaigues,
Switzerland), Endosequence (Brasseler, Savannah, GA, USA), or
Biodentine (Septodont, Niederkassel, Germany). Success rates for perforation repair have been reported to be up to 90%.77,89,102

Perforations with communication to the oral environment, old perforations, or perforations with a radiographically visible lesion have a poorer prognosis than fresh perforations or those completely surrounded by bone.125

### Re-preparation

Re-preparation of the root canal aims at accessing previously uninstrumented and undisinfected spaces and at the improvement of disinfection. It has been shown that, in many root canals, enlargement of the root canal using two, three, or even four instrument sizes will not remove the filling material completely.32,56 On the other hand, overpreparation and weakening of the roots should be avoided. The remaining dentin thickness as well as the final size of the primary preparation are unknown, implying a high risk of strip perforation and additional straightening in curved roots during re-instrumentation. As mistakes during primary preparation such as straightening, ledging, blockage, enlargement of the apical foramen, or loss of working length in many cases cannot be sufficiently corrected, the importance of thorough disinfection cannot be overstressed.

## Risks and problems in retreatment

As mentioned before, retreatment should be a repetition of the primary treatment with the utmost endeavor to eliminate the shortcomings of that treatment. However, such attempts also comprise further risks of worsening the situation. Distinctive risks and problems of retreatment procedures are possible flare-ups of previously asymptomatic teeth as well as the generation of dentinal defects, perforations, and excessive hard tissue removal in the case of overpreparation of the root canals, which is often associated with the overextension of filling material. Only limited evidence is currently available regarding these potential risks of retreatment.

### Flare-ups

A retrospective analysis of 915 root canal treatments revealed an overall prevalence of flare-ups of 2.3%.8 No flare-ups occurred after treatment of vital teeth, but in the nonvital group the prevalence was
5.3%, and 4.4% in the retreatment group. Thus, based on these very limited data, it seems that retreatment is not per se associated with an increased risk of flare-ups.

### Cracks

There is still an ongoing debate regarding the incidence of dentinal cracks during endodontic retreatment.22,25,142,148,163 Root canaltreated teeth that have undergone retreatment showed significantly more dentinal defects than primary root canal-treated teeth.124,141

However, in another study, no differences were found regarding the

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 973 -->

incidence of dentinal defects or vertical root fractures between initial treatment and retreatment.29 Thus, the currently available evidence does not allow any final conclusion to be drawn regarding whether or not root canal-retreated teeth display more dentinal defects such as cracks or craze lines than root canal-treated teeth after primary treatment.

### Perforations

A recent systematic review on root perforations117 analyzed 22 relevant studies on different types of perforations such as strip, furcal, coronal, and apical. Factors associated with perforations were the experience of the practitioner as well as the type of tooth and its morphology. Retreatment procedures were not mentioned as a variable that might be associated with perforations. Thus, to date, no evidence is available regarding the link between retreatment and an increased risk of perforation when compared with primary treatment.

### Ledging

No data are available on the incidence of ledges during removal of filling material and re-preparation. The use of instruments with noncutting tips is recommended to avoid the creation of new ledges or the enlargement of those that are already present.81 It should be noted that some NiTi retreatment instruments show actively cutting tips.

### Straightening and transportation

Many teeth subjected to retreatment already show morphological alterations such as straightening, which is not reversible. Great care has to be taken not to increase the deviation from the original axis of the root canal, which may be associated with insufficient cleaning and apical leakage.119

Great care has to be taken not to remove excessive amounts of dentin, thereby weakening the tooth (Fig 17-14). Each instrument should be carefully investigated for adhering material. As long as filling material is visible in the flutes, preparation has to be continued, and when only dentin chips are adhering should preparation be stopped.

Reaching and cleaning the apical constriction is a prerequisite of overextension of filling material and, at the same time, one of the important goals of retreatment. ## Success rates of nonsurgical retreatment

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 976 -->

The reported success rates for retreatment are approximately 10% to 15% lower than for primary treatment.42,93,97 The use of improved instruments and techniques has generally resulted in higher success rates in more recent studies. The prognosis is excellent if a tooth without an apical lesion has to be retreated, which highlights that the prevention of apical inflammation should routinely be considered in teeth with insufficient root canal fillings, at least before placing a new coronal restoration or in patients with a compromised immune system. Besides the presence of an apical lesion, extrusion of filling material, and the quality of the coronal restoration, the alteration of root canal anatomy during primary treatment is one of the most important predictors for success or failure of retreatment.47,93

Retreatment of teeth with apical periodontitis and alterations of the internal anatomy showed a success rate of 40%, whereas retreatment of teeth without a lesion and with unaltered anatomy was found to be successful in 94% of the cases (Fig 17-16).47

## Periradicular surgery

The primary aim of endodontic surgery is not the elimination of an intraradicular infection from the entire root canal. Rather, it is the elimination of bacteria from the apical part of the root through the removal of the root tip and the placement of a retrograde filling, thus preventing access for surviving microorganisms to the periradicular tissues and the ingress of nutrients for the microorganisms from the periapical tissues into the root canal.

Primary or secondary surgical intervention may be considered in the following cases if clinical symptoms and/or radiographic signs of apical periodontitis are present:48

Teeth with calcified and/or non-negotiable root canals.
Orthograde root canal treatment is not feasible due to considerable morpholigical variants of the roots.

<!-- PDF page 977 -->

Persistent apical periodontitis with clinical symptoms or increasing radiographically visible osteolysis after complete or incomplete primary root canal filling or retreatment, if this cannot be retreated or improved nonsurgically or only at disproportionate risk.
Fracture of a root canal instrument near the apex that cannot be removed in an orthograde approach if the tooth presents with pain or a lesion does not heal.
Root perforations in the apical part of the root that cannot be managed nonsurgically.
Extruded root canal filling material causing clinical symptoms or with the involvement of adjacent anatomical structures (maxillary sinus, mandibular canal).
Horizontal root fractures in the apical third of the root with infection of the apical fragment.
Teeth that have already undergone surgical treatment – as an alternative to or in addition to the orthograde retreatment.
Injury to root apices during surgical procedures (e.g. cyst removal, biopsy).
Teeth with complex prosthetic restoration or large-volume posts/casts which should not be damaged.
A biopsy must be performed near or at the root apex.

Most contraindications to periapical surgery are related to the patient (psychologic and systemic conditions), the clinician (lack of experience, expertise, and equipment), or anatomical conditions (lack of surgical access). Periradicular surgery is an important approach to manage endodontic (post)treatment disease and to save the affected tooth. Thorough curettage of the apical inflammatory lesion combined with root-end resection (apicoectomy), axial retrograde preparation and filling of at least 3 to 4 mm of the root canal should be performed whenever possible to improve the outcome of periradicular surgery.
Curettage aims at the mechanical removal of any extraradicular infection or foreign materials present in the lesion. Root-end resection and retrograde preparation aim at the elimination of the apical intraradicular infection (at least in the most apical part of the root canal) and/or any extraradicular biofilm covering the root surface.
Finally, the root-end filling seals the apical terminus of the root canal, entombs any bacteria still present in the canal after resection, and prevents the inflow of periapical tissue fluids.

It should be taken into account that the dynamics of healing after surgical treatment and nonsurgical retreatment differ considerably.
Surgical treatment removes the complete lesion, and healing is completed as soon as the bony crypt is refilled with mineralizing granulation tissue. In the case of orthograde retreatment, when the intracanal microorganisms have been sufficiently removed, the inflamed tissue that is still present first has to reorganize, which takes markedly more time.

### Endodontic microsurgery

The introduction of the dental operating microscope represents one of the most significant achievements in endodontic surgery. The use of high magnification and illumination as well as a microsurgical approach in periradicular surgery offer several crucial advantages and are associated with clearly higher success.73,101,122,123

The main advantages are:

Easy identification of the root apex and anatomical variations, especially after methylene blue staining. Any pathologies such as fractures, microcracks, perforations, and resorptive defects, or anatomical details such as isthmuses and lateral canals present on the root and the resected root surface can be more accurately identified and evaluated.
A more precise and complete removal of the inflamed tissue can be achieved.
A minimally invasive approach requires less removal of bone than a conventional surgical approach, resulting in faster healing and less postoperative discomfort.
The root-end filling material can allegedly be applied more easily and in a more controlled manner.
Better documentation of the surgical procedure by video or photography.

### Pre- and postoperative preparation/medication

Patients scheduled for periradicular surgery should be instructed to use 0.12% chlorhexidine gluconate (CHX) rinses starting on the day of surgery, twice daily until the removal of the sutures.7,132 This may represent a valuable chemo-preventive tool, especially immediately after surgery, when oral hygiene is compromised. Nevertheless, CHX has side effects such as the staining of teeth, mucosal irritation, and taste alterations, although these are generally minimal.127 The prescription of a nonsteroidal analgesic (e.g. 400 mg ibuprofen) 1 hour before the procedure was questioned by a recent metaanalysis.28
Postoperative administration of nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or paracetamol (acetaminophen) may be beneficial because postoperative pain may be caused by postsurgical inflammation secondary to invasive procedures, and it markedly affects patient recovery and satisfaction.157 NSAIDs may be prescribed in combination with opioids or corticosteroids.7

Corticosteroids are claimed to reduce swelling and trismus after surgical procedures, but drug route and the kind of administration (intravenous, intramuscular, or orally) still require clarification.7

Concerning antibiotics, there is a lack of evidence to support routine postoperative prescription in healthy patients.7 Hence, antibiotics should be considered in patients with severe systemic diseases with a compromised immune system or in patients with altered defense capacity (congenital or acquired), e.g. patients with infective endocarditis, prosthetic cardiac valves, or recent prosthetic joint replacement.120

The incision design is the first step in accessing the surgical area (the bony area overlying the root tip of the target tooth). Thorough planning of the flap design and extension may determine the quality of the access and influence the healing of the soft and hard tissue. The actual size of the lesion is very often larger than it appears on the radiograph. Thus, the extension of the flap and a vertical incision should never touch the lesion area, and should include at least one or two teeth on each side of the target tooth. The decision about whether to use an intrasulcular or a submarginal incision depends on several factors. Short roots, expected lateral canals, limited width of the attached gingiva, suspicion of a crack, and the posterior position of the targeted tooth favor an intrasulcular incision, whereas submarginal incisions are preferable in the esthetic region, close to prosthetic restorations, and in cases with thin biotypes.

### Flap reflection

Flap reflection is intended to elevate the periosteum and the superficial soft tissues from the cortical plate and should always begin in the attached gingiva area of the vertical incision. A periosteal elevator is moved coronally to elevate the marginal and interdental gingiva with minimal force. Next, the flap is retracted to obtain proper surgical access. The retractor (e.g. Carr, KimTrac, Kim/Pecora,
Langenbeck, Prichard) should rest on sound bone with light but firm pressure. For a secure positioning of the retractor, it is advisable to prepare a small matching groove beyond the root apex in the cortical bone, the main purpose of which is to permit anchorage of a serrated retractor tip in order to promote the steady retraction of the mucoperiosteal flap and to protect the anatomical structures (a groove can also be made above the mental foramen in order to protect it during surgery).

### Osteotomy

In cases with already perforated cortical bone, the apical lesion is apparent immediately after the flap is reflected. If not, the inflammatory lesion has not reached the cortical plate, which looks intact. To locate the lesion in such cases, it is recommended to make a small hole in the bone over the suspected lesion area using a round bur. To check the direction of the osteotomy in cases with very thick cortical bone (especially in the mandible) a radiograph with a guttapercha cone can be used. Round burs used in a brushing motion under copious saline irrigation in a high-speed, low-torque handpiece serve to expose the lesion. An osteotomy window is created over the root apex. In some cases, the approach must be started from a more coronal position, removing the bone toward the apex after identifying the root, especially when anticipating a proximity between the root apex and important anatomical structures (e.g. maxillary sinus, mental

foramen). The smaller the osteotomy, the faster the healing, which favors a minimally invasive approach.

### Periradicular curettage

Once osteotomy is completed and access to the bone crypt and root tip obtained, the apical lesion/granuloma is removed by curettage.
Usually, the lesion is curetted before or during the resection of the root tip, but it is sometimes necessary to perform root resection first in order to gain better visibility and easier access to the lesion.
Straight or angled bone curettes and angled periodontal curettes are suited for curettage. When the lesion is detached from the walls of the bone cavity, it is grasped with a tissue forceps and removed. It is advisable to immediately place the tissue in a flask containing 10% buffered formalin solution for later histopathologic analysis, because radiologic evaluation fails to distinguish between a granuloma, a cyst, an abscess, or scar tissue.14,19 Usually, the apical inflammatory lesion is attributed to intracanal infection. Hence, it is not absolutely necessary to remove the lesion in its entirety. Some soft tissue remnants from a large lesion may need to be left in areas near the apices of adjacent roots so as to avoid surgical exposure of these roots or other anatomical structures. The exact 3D extent of a lesion can only be reliably predicted using a CBCT analysis. Hence, CBCT is a valuable diagnostic aid in the decision-making process for endodontic surgery. The mental foramen, maxillary sinus, inferior alveolar nerve, canalis retromolaris, nasal spine, canalis incisivus, nasopalatine duct, and nasal floor can be reliably evaluated regarding their relationship to the apices.17,85,104,147

Even though lesion remnants may not negatively impact the outcome of the surgery, the entire removal of the lesion should ideally be performed quickly and completely to avoid profuse bleeding by the well-vascularized granulomatous tissue. In the following order, calcium sulfate, collagen with epinephrine, ferric sulfate, gauze packing, or collagen provide an adequate hemostasis.26

### Root-end resection (apicoectomy)

The contemporary surgical concept involves a 3-mm–long root-end resection, intended to remove most of the apical ramifications, lateral canals, and isthmuses, but retain a sufficient root length for tooth retention and function. The formerly recommended bevel angles of 45 to 60 degrees to improve access and visibility are outdated. Modern techniques using the dental operating microscope and ultrasonic tips allow root-end resection almost perpendicular to the long axis of the root in most cases (bevel angle 0 to 10 degrees). The smaller the bevel angle, the better the prognosis of the surgically treated tooth.154 Recently, targeted endodontic microsurgery (TEMS) using
3D-printed surgical guides has been proposed for optimizing volume and bevel angle of the resection.51 Many surgeons perform the apicoectomy with a Lindemann bur, followed by smoothing the cut surface with an ultrafine diamond. However, much more delicate instruments are available such as the EndoExplorer (Komet, Lemgo,
Germany), allowing exact minimally invasive apicoectomy and simultaneous smoothing (Fig 17-23). For a proper inspection of the resected root, the area (root surface, external root contour, and outline of the canal) can be stained with 1% methylene blue solution.21,137 Wiping a small cotton pellet moistened with the dye over the resected root surface for 5 to 10 seconds followed by flushing with sterile saline solution is recommended. The resected root surface is inspected under the dental operating microscope using
4x to 25x magnification and bright illumination to detect any defects or fracture lines. Special micromirrors are positioned at a 45-degree angle to the root surface for proper visualization.

### Root-end filling

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 996 -->

After preparation, disinfection, and drying, the root-end cavity needs to be filled with a biocompatible material that promotes an antibacterial seal. Placing a root-end filling enhances the outcome of surgical endodontic treatment, particularly when persistent infection is the cause of failure.24,78,109 To exert such an influence on the outcome, the root-end filling material should seal the cavity, impeding the influx of protein-rich fluids or exudates into the canal or the egress of bacteria from the canal to the periradicular tissues. Since persistent/secondary intraradicular infections are indisputably the main cause of treatment failure, a root-end filling should always be placed to enhance the prognosis of surgical treatment.

Several materials have been propagated as root-end filling materials. Both dicalcium and tricalcium silicate-based hydraulic cements (e.g. MTA, Biodentine [Septodent, Saint-Maur Cedex,
France], TotalFill BC Putty [FKG Dentaire, Le Crêt-du-Locle,
Switzerland]) and reinforced zinc oxide and eugenol cements such as
Intermediate Restorative Material (IRM; Caulk Dentsply, Milford, DE,
USA) and Super-EBA (ethoxy benzoic acid; Harry J. Bosworth,
Skokie, IL, USA) are well suited as root-end filling materials. They show adequate performance concerning sealability, biocompatibility, and success rates.72,75,133,167

However, recent studies suggest that dicalcium and tricalcium silicate-based materials are associated with an improved prognosis in endodontic microsurgery due to their superior biocompatibility and bioactivity.55,156

After placement of the root-end filling material, bleeding into the bone cavity should be provoked to enhance regeneration of the bony defect. Depending on the retrograde filling material used, setting occurs in about 15 minutes (fast setting materials) or in hours to days (slow setting materials) due to the required moisture/water absorption (hydration process). Ready-to-use hydraulic materials (e.g. TotalFill BC Putty) set after water absorption from the surrounding tissue (hydration), whereas ready-to-mix materials are freshly mixed and provide the liquid required for setting. ### Suturing

The flap is repositioned after the surgical site has been thoroughly irrigated with sterile saline, and a radiograph has been taken to check the cleanliness of the surgical site and the adequacy of the root-end filling. Gentle pressure must be applied to the flap for 1 to 2 minutes using gauze moistened with sterile saline to ensure good adaptation of the flap to its original position. Interrupted sutures are placed, starting at the angle between the vertical and horizontal incisions. The vertical incision may require one to three sutures. Sutures should simply adapt the flap in position. Making sutures too tight may cause ischemia and thus compromise rapid healing. Suture knots should be placed away from the incision line to minimize bacterial accumulation and colonization in that area. Thin, monofilament polyamide sutures (5-0 and 6-0) applied with small needles promote faster healing (Fig
17-25).6,31 Silk sutures are no longer recommended as they favor plaque accumulation and may act as a wick for the migration of bacteria into the wound, resulting in delayed healing or secondary inflammation.83 Once the sutures have been placed, the tissue should be compressed again with firm finger pressure for about 3 minutes to ensure that the space between the flap soft tissue and the bone is reduced, with minimal blood clot formation. Sutures can be removed within 48 to 72 hours. There is no need to leave them in place for up to 1 week, as was recommended previously.

## Persistent pathology after surgical treatment

Conventional apical surgery has been reported to result in a 60% to
80% success rate,52,143 and microsurgical techniques in an approximately 90% success rate.144 These data indicate that, in a number of cases of unsuccessful apical surgery, further treatment is necessary and indicated.

### Reasons for failure of apical surgery

In recent studies,134,155 the following factors have been identified as main reasons for the failure of endodontic surgery:

Persisting infection or reinfection via coronal leakage in 44% of cases (24 of 54 teeth) without retrograde filling.34

Apical leakage in 83.3% of 168 apicected roots.155

Incorrect root-end preparation.134

Anatomical problems preventing sufficient disinfection and obturation of the root canal system (isthmus, lateral extensions) in
5.5% of cases (3 of 54 teeth).
An evaluation of 168 consecutive cases of apical surgery (12) reported craze lines and cracks in 9.5% of the apicected root surfaces.155

If the microorganisms remaining and surviving in the root canal system or recontaminating the root canal manage to multiply to a sufficient number and gain access to the periradicular tissues, a periapical inflammation can be initiated or may persist, even following apical surgery.108,152

In such cases, the radiolucent area may initially decrease in size and then stabilize, or may even increase again with time. Clinical signs of failure may be absent for a while (asymptomatic cases), but symptoms or a sinus tract often occur as signs of late failure.

No study so far has investigated the microbiome in teeth with persisting disease after apical surgery. It can be presumed that the microbial flora is identical or at least similar to that of post-endodontic disease after primary treatment, but modifications of the microbiome as a result of altered environmental conditions after apical surgery may also be present, although definitive proof is still lacking (Fig 1727 ).
