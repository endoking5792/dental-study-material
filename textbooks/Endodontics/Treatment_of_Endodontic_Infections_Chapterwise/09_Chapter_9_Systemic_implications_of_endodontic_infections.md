## Introduction

The interest in and concern about the association of pulpal and periapical infections with systemic disease started in the late 19th century and early 20th century. The microbiologist W. D. Miller observed that some oral, and in particular endodontic, infections may have systemic symptoms and may spread to distant systemic sites.
He also associated a number of systemic diseases and infections with the mouth due to the presence of oral signs and symptoms.103

This later became known as the “focal infection theory.” In his 1910 address to the Royal College of Surgeons, William Hunter claimed that dental sepsis and restorative dentistry for teeth with prior infections were responsible for many systemic diseases.74 For decades after this, and due in part to a series of flawed animal experiments, dentists and physicians advocated the extraction of many teeth in an attempt to cure systemic diseases that had no known etiology or for which there was no other treatment.69 In the
1950s and 1960s, this practice diminished with the discovery of the etiology and treatment for many of the medical conditions that were previously poorly understood, and with the developments in the science and methodologies of public health.

The modern era of research on the association between oral and systemic disease began in the late 1980s and early 1990s with a series of important publications.40,101 Interestingly, these early publications examined oral infections collectively. However, since then, the major emphasis and focus in the literature (with hundreds of publications, major grant funding, and large longitudinal multicenter cohort studies) has been on periodontal disease, and to a lesser degree on tooth loss. Therefore, although the original focal infection theory began with an emphasis on endodontic infections, and despite the contemporary realization that the microbiology and pathogenesis of endodontic and periodontal diseases are similar, endodontic disease has remained poorly studied in this area compared with

periodontal disease. Moreover, many of the public health studies dealing with tooth loss assume that its underlying cause is periodontal disease or caries, but ignore the fact that it is the complications of extensive caries, namely endodontic pathosis, that is of critical importance in tooth loss.28,80

There are several reasons for the recent reduced emphasis on the relationship between endodontics and systemic disease compared with tooth loss or periodontal disease. Endodontic practice suffered tremendously at the beginning of the 20th century compared with other oral disciplines due to practices based on the focal infection theory. Endodontics practitioners were therefore not interested in resurrecting this theory because they believed that it was proven to be inaccurate. In addition, large public health studies necessitated access to large sample sizes, frequently in community settings, with long periods of observation and collaboration among different centers. Endodontic departments had a relatively small cadre of personnel, shorter advanced educational programs, and limited resources for undertaking such large studies. Endodontic studies need access to radiographic and clinical testing data for diagnosis, and were therefore much more difficult and expensive to undertake compared with measurements of enumeration of teeth, carious surfaces, attachment loss, and periodontal health that could easily be undertaken in field trials. In addition, the endodontic burden of disease was inadvertently included under the umbrella of caries complications, and therefore not characterized as uniquely a variant of deep caries, in which irreversible endodontic pathosis occurs.
Tooth loss, which, as previously stated, is one of the most important factors associated with systemic disease, was therefore attributed to caries or periodontal disease without the emphasis on endodontic disease. It is well known that most carious teeth that are extracted due to pain or because they are unrestorable have irreversible pulpal pathosis, but this was not documented in the relevant public health studies.

However, in the last two decades, several important studies have been published that uniquely examine the role of endodontic disease

and how if affects the etiology, pathogenesis, and participation in systemic conditions, with compelling results that mimic those of periodontal findings. The unfortunate issue now is that most of these studies do not account adequately for the periodontal condition of the patient or of the endodontically treated teeth. While this situation is similar, in essence, to the lack of documentation of endodontic disease in the many studies on periodontal and systemic disease, this absence of controlling for periodontal disease begs the question of whether the operative factors are endodontic or periodontal in origin.

The focus of this chapter is on direct clinical information to assist practitioners in making clinical decisions and informing their patients about the available knowledge in this area. Preclinical work using animal models and other preclinical data is cited in cases where there is a scarcity or absence of clinical information or to substantiate key clinical concepts.

## Association versus causation

The concept of association versus causation is germane to the study of oral and systemic disease, as it is in defining the etiology of any disease. Likewise, in pulpal and periapical disease, there are many microorganisms that have been identified in these conditions. These microorganisms may be causative of the disease or they may be selected for in the presence of the disease. Inflammatory mediators also characterize pulp and periapical disease and may have a similar relationship with it. Patients experiencing severe pain, spreading infections, sinus tracts, and periodontal-endodontic communication may have unique microbial taxa, microbial combinations, or host mediators that are causative of these conditions or are merely associated with them.

In 1962, Sir Austin Bradford Hill published an important book describing the strength of causal relationship in medicine and public health such as in the relationship of smoking and lung cancer.72 The so-called “Hill criteria” for causal inference became the basis for

defining causal relationships in disease. The main aspects of this causal relationship are summarized below. For an etiologic factor to cause a disease process there must be:

Temporality (the cause precedes the result);
Dose-response relationship;
Consistency under different conditions and in different situations;
Specificity, where the relationship can be narrowed down to welldefined causes and conditions;
Biologic plausibility (usually shown by animal models demonstrating the mechanism of how the etiology causes the disease);
Strength of the association, such that the relationship is not likely to be caused by random factors;
Independence from confounding factors that must be controlled for in the statistical analysis of the relationship.

Clearly, the situation with disease associations is frequently marred by many variables, as previously stated with periodontal disease, which may or may not be known and accurately documented in each study. In addition, many of the studies that have been undertaken lack sufficient sample sizes, length of duration (in cohort studies), or accurate data on temporality. For example, in the association of oral infections with cardiovascular disease, it is commonly known that atherosclerosis may start in a subclinical mode in the third or fourth decades of life, but not manifest with symptoms and become diagnosed until the fifth or sixth decades. Apical periodontitis (AP) may also develop months or years (asymptomatically) before it is diagnosed. In these situations, establishing temporality becomes challenging. Therefore, for many chronic infections in the oral cavity, including AP, the association with systemic diseases may lack important criteria of the Hill paradigm for causal inference.

It is generally accepted, however, that study types and study designs are conducive to deductive reasoning with respect to

causality, and that the relationship between two disease conditions is constructed based on the totality of available evidence. Disease associations can be inferred from case-control, cross-sectional, and longitudinal cohort studies after important confounding variables are controlled. Longitudinal study designs represent a stronger evidence level because many of the variables involved are controlled, and temporality can be addressed. However, the outcome must still be defined as an association because factors that are not studied or adequately tracked may be operational.

In contrast, there are two types of studies that are considered to infer a causal relationship, the first type being animal studies. As previously stated, animal studies show biologic plausibility and mechanisms of causation of the secondary disease from the primary conditions or diseases. They allow the strict control of environmental, genetic, dietary, and microbial factors to a significant degree, thereby allowing the principal etiologic factors and primary outcomes to be adequately correlated, both physically and temporally. However, animal studies do not reveal the situation in humans, particularly when accounting for diverse patient populations.

The second and most definitive type of study that demonstrates causality is the randomized controlled interventional trial, which allows the control of extraneous factors by defining strict inclusion and exclusion criteria or by measuring other variables that cannot be controlled to include in the analysis. The randomized study approach controls bias, which is an important concern when claims are made that the treatment of the primary disease results in improvement in the secondary one. The major concern with this type of study is that it frequently does not have an adequate control (because it would be unethical to include a no-treatment control). These studies are also very large and expensive to undertake.

A good example of a randomized controlled interventional trial is the PAVE study that was undertaken to show whether periodontal treatment would reduce the incidence of secondary cardiovascular disease in patients with periodontal disease who were also at a high risk of cardiovascular disease.110 Patients included in this study had

## Acute endodontic infections and distant infections

The situation with acute infections is different from chronic asymptomatic infections because the former infections are rapid, and systemic disease could occur if they are not treated promptly. Acute endodontic infections are usually painful and are occasionally described as spreading. Spreading infections are associated with lymphadenopathy of the ipsilateral submandibular, submental, or cervical lymph nodes as well as fever, malaise, and/or spread to fascial spaces close to the source of the infection. They may also be associated with increased sedimentation rate, neutropenia, an increase in systemic acute phase proteins, and proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1 (IL-1), IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α).44 If left untreated, or occasionally as a flare-up after initial treatment (Fig 9-1), the infection may develop into a serious life-threatening condition that could result in emergency hospitalization for effective treatment. Studies have shown that in the
USA, over 400,000 hospital emergency department visits in 2006 were due to pulp and periapical disease,107 and over 8,000 patients with periapical abscess were admitted as inpatients in 2007.2 More recently, it was shown that over the period 2008 to 2014, the number of hospital cases due to periapical abscess in the USA increased, and that 76 outpatients and 807 inpatients died in that period due to this disease.116

The spread of endodontic bacteria systemically may arise from acute as well as chronic infections. The spread of acute infections is easier to identify and localize, but the spread of bacteria from asymptomatic or chronic infections is more difficult to measure and to implicate in distant disease. However, occasionally, flare-ups that are thought to arise following root canal instrumentation and require unscheduled appointments may also result in major spreading infections86 (see Fig
9-1).

Studies have shown that bacteremia may develop in 17% to 54% of cases during nonsurgical root canal treatment, and up to 83% following surgical treatment (Table 9-1). This necessitates prescribing antibiotics prior to endodontic treatment in patients at risk of infective endocarditis (IE),135 and some who are immunocompromised or at high risk of late artificial joint infection.129

One large study has shown that the prescription of antibiotics for patients at high risk of IE has a protective effect against incident IE, but no effect on death due to IE.36 However, there are no data available showing a benefit for antibiotic prescription for the prevention of late artificial joint infection.129 Furthermore, studies have shown that antibiotics do not prevent bacteremia following dental97

and endodontic118 procedures. In addition, normal daily activities such as toothbrushing97 and chewing50 may induce bacteremia. Given the frequency of these daily activities, especially in patients with gingivitis or marginal periodontitis, the additional risk imparted by dental procedures such as endodontic treatment is unknown.

In this context, it is also unclear as to what extent bacteremia may arise from chronic asymptomatic endodontic infections. These infections may be present for months or years before being diagnosed and treated. Moreover, even following treatment, it takes 1 to 4 years for total radiographic resolution, and some of these cases have persistent disease that is monitored for healing for this length of time before further treatment is rendered.

### Cardiovascular disease

Cardiovascular disease encompasses many disease entities, including congenital, inflammatory, and infectious diseases as well as other etiologies. The specific conditions referred to in the endodontic literature include hypertension, atherosclerosis, coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease, stroke, peripheral vascular disease, and IE. The central hypothesis for the pathogenesis of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease involves disseminated vascular inflammatory conditions promoted by a sedentary lifestyle, poor diet, and smoking in a genetically susceptible host.34 It is now recognized that inflammation is further exaggerated by specific microbial taxa that may originate from the gut, the oral cavity, or other sources.24 These microorganisms, mainly bacteria or their metabolites, either contribute to the development of the metabolic syndrome, which is the hallmark of cardiovascular disease, or are found intracellularly or extracellularly within atherosclerotic plaques.

There is a large body of evidence supporting the association between periodontal disease and cardiovascular disease.90,124 As noted previously, this started in the modern era within the context of data associating oral disease and systemic disease.40,101 This association was further substantiated by evidence that periodontal disease increases metabolic syndromes due to the systemic release of periodontal bacteria that cause an increase in C-reactive protein (CRP), serum amyloid A (SAA), and proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1 and IL-6.16,89,124 While this association is independent of other factors and consistent in many studies, evidence for causation is weak. For example, to date, there has been no evidence to demonstrate that periodontal treatment in randomized interventional trials can prevent primary or secondary cardiovascular disease.96

The endodontic and periodontal microbiome have been shown to be similar.64,82,87 Moreover, AP has been associated with an increase in acute phase proteins (e.g.

Periodontal disease, which typically affects the whole mouth, may cause a larger microbial burden than individual chronic periapical lesions. However, the duration of AP (especially when it is asymptomatic), and its association with virulent bacteria and a high bacterial load as well as with several teeth in the mouth may provide equivalence in the microbial burden to periodontal disease.

Cross-sectional, case-control, and longitudinal studies have shown significant associations between AP and cardiovascular disease. For example, case-control studies have shown significant association between myocardial infarction and an increased number of teeth with
AP and missing teeth as well as a reduced number of teeth with endodontic treatment.134 This study also showed an association of IE with periodontal disease. One longitudinal cohort study showed a significant association in males younger than 40 years between AP and coronary heart disease.26 Another cohort study showed that the endodontic burden (AP and endodontic treatment) was significantly associated with incident cardiovascular events.66 Interestingly, in this study, periodontal disease did not have a significant association after controlling for confounding variables.

### Diabetes mellitus

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is an important chronic disease that affects about half a billion individuals worldwide.41
DM leads to cardiovascular, kidney, neuropathic, vascular, retinal, and periodontal diseases as well as mortality.68 Type 1 DM is caused by autoimmune-mediated degeneration of pancreatic beta cells, leading to insulin insufficiency. It is usually, but not always, diagnosed early in life. Type 2 DM is more insidious as it is associated with obesity, hypertension, and a high carbohydrate diet in a susceptible host.
Insulin resistance is the hallmark of type 2 DM.

It has been recognized for some time that patients with type 2 DM are much more susceptible to marginal periodontitis. It has also been shown that diabetic patients with marginal periodontitis have modest improvement in their glycemia following periodontal treatment. This bidirectional relationship of DM and periodontal disease is currently accepted among dentists and physicians treating these patients.27

The relationship between DM and endodontic disease has been recognized for a long time; however, most of the older information was anecdotal.17 More recently, epidemiologic studies have shown a strong association between DM and the prevalence of AP.23,46,99,125

One longitudinal study showed that while diabetes did not affect the

outcomes of endodontic treatment for all endodontic patients, it significantly reduced the prognosis of treatment in cases with preoperative lesions, especially after controlling for several confounding variables.52 Other outcome studies have corroborated these findings on the healing of AP9,91 as well as the survival of the endodontically treated teeth.104,133 Systematic reviews7 and umbrella reviews106 have also corroborated these findings. One recent, large, hospital-based study showed a very strong association between all
ICD-10 diagnoses of AP and type 2 DM in sex-, age-, and ethnicitymatched populations.136

For type 2 DM, it is important for the provider to recognize that many diabetics around the world are not aware of their condition. In the USA, the latest estimate is that about 21% of these patients do not realize that they are diabetic.109 In recent years, there has been a call to promote screening of patients who fit the risk profile for diabetes (obesity, hypertension, periodontal disease, AP, etc) in the dental office. One study showed that of 1,022 dental patients who did not know their diabetic status, 40.7% had a hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) of over 5.7% (the lower limit for prediabetes) and were referred to their physician.58 Of these patients, 35% received a diagnosis from their physician within 1 year. They were diagnosed with diabetes (12%), prediabetes (23%), and no diabetes (64%). Therefore, it is prudent to consider using one of the available chairside tools for screening patients in the dental office (Fig 9-3).

### Renal disease

Renal disease is a direct complication of DM, hypertension, and other forms of cardiovascular disease. Patients with impaired renal function are at high risk in the dental office due to their inability to metabolize important medications such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, local anesthetics, and antibiotics. Patients with renal disease are at risk of developing secondary hyperparathyroidism, which results in multiple bony radiolucencies, which may be confused with endodontic

### Chronic systemic medications

In addition to and because of the chronic systemic diseases that affect many endodontic patients, these patients may be taking several medications on a continual basis. Many classes of medications fall under this umbrella, and it is likely that this will be a growing area of investigation in future. Some of the important associations that have been described in the literature are discussed below.

Patients who take steroids for prolonged periods, for instance, to avoid organ transplant rejection or to treat autoimmune disease, may have significant calcification of the pulp space.108 In addition to these calcifications, these patients are immunosuppressed and may experience higher levels of caries as well as dental and bone abnormalities.111 Statins may also cause pulp stones and root canal calcifications.113 This may be the reason why patients with cardiovascular diseases, who are likely to also have hypercholesterolemia and be taking statins, may have increased pulp stones, as noted previously.43

The chronic use of statins was shown to affect the outcomes of endodontic treatment in teeth with preoperative lesions.1 In this study, patients were included if they were taking statins at the time of treatment until the follow-up of 2 to 4 years postoperatively.1 In these cases, teeth with preoperative lesions healed at the rate of 93%, which was statistically better than for patients who were not taking statins, even after controlling for several important confounding variables.

As noted previously, diabetic patients have significantly more teeth with AP as well as endodontically treated teeth with AP than nondiabetics. This strong association was recently shown to be significantly reduced if the patients were taking statins or metformin, a hypoglycemic drug that also has antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties.136 Taken together, these studies suggest that statins are associated with mineralization and the healing of pulpal and periapical tissues.

The chronic use of bisphosphonates and other medications that affect bone metabolism has long been known to render patients at risk of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ).81,126

This risk is especially high if patients are taking the medication parenterally, if they are taking multiple medications that have this effect, and if they undergo invasive surgery of the jaw.10,11 One study that examined the outcome of endodontic treatment in patients on bisphosphonates did not show a difference between these patients and the control subjects.73 However, more recently, it was shown that the chronic use of monoclonal antibody medications targeting receptor RANKL such as denosumab (Prolia) may be associated with generalized external cervical root resorption.4,38

In recent years, biologic medications such as monoclonal antibody medications targeting cytokines and other inflammatory mediators have experienced tremendous growth in use for a variety of medical conditions. One important study showed that patients with inflammatory bowel disease who took anti-TNF-α monoclonal antibody medication (Humira) healed significantly faster following root canal treatment of teeth with AP.35 This result is not surprising, given that TNF-α is an important proinflammatory and bone-resorptive cytokine involved with the pathogenesis of AP.

Clearly, the role of systemic medications in the healing of AP does not negate the importance of addressing bacteria that are the etiologic factors of AP. These medications likely improve the outcomes and enhance healing by their anabolic, anti-inflammatory, and bone-forming properties in cases in which the bacterial irritants have been adequately controlled. This property is important and warrants further study, as the use of these medications topically or systemically in future may allow faster healing of cases that are destined to heal anyway. Preclinical studies are currently underway to investigate the roles of probiotics, melatonin, leptin, and other agents on the pathogenesis of pulp and periapical disease.

## Adverse pregnancy outcomes

Adverse pregnancy outcomes generally refer to stillbirth, premature birth, and low birth weight. Also included here are pregnancy complications for the mother such as preeclampsia (hypertension due to pregnancy), gestational diabetes, and other conditions that may put the mother at risk of sustained cardiovascular disease or diabetes after pregnancy. A large body of research has been carried out on the association of periodontal disease and its treatment with adverse pregnancy outcomes. While this research has shown a close association between these two diseases, to date there is no conclusive evidence to show that periodontal treatment during pregnancy would improve pregnancy outcomes.117

There is much less information available in endodontics on the adverse effects of AP on pregnancy outcomes. Some of this information has been shown in animal studies.12,13 One case-control clinical study showed that preeclampsia was significantly associated with the presence of AP of at least one tooth, even after controlling for periodontal disease.83 One other cross-sectional study from
Malawi documented premature birth and restricted intrauterine growth in mothers with AP.70

## Sickle cell disease

Sickle cell disease includes sickle cell anemia (SCA), which is the homozygous, more severe variant of the disease. Sickle cell trait is a heterozygous, milder form of the disease. SCA affects over 300,000 newly born children worldwide annually, most of whom are in subSaharan Africa.60 The disease is a monogenic congenital abnormality that results from the substitution of one amino acid in the β-chain of hemoglobin. This variation, which affects up to 80% of hemoglobin in
SCA patients, results in the sickle shape of the red blood cells, which have reduced oxygenation capacity. This results in increased blood viscosity, reduced blood flow, hypoxia, and vascular occlusion.95 The disease is also characterized by acute, generalized, painful events and a myriad of cardiovascular complications.95

Several studies have shown that SCA can result in pulp necrosis and AP in teeth that do not have etiologic factors for these conditions.8,21,39,131 In addition, these patients may have craniofacial pain that mimics endodontic pain but may or may not be related to endodontic pathosis.21,31,39 One study showed that maxillary teeth, other than canines, had reduced arterial oxygen saturation (SpO2) in
SCA patients compared with normal patients.131 In another study, the most frequent dental alterations observed in SCA patients were pulp calcification and external resorption of the root.130 The levels of proinflammatory cytokine, anti-inflammatory cytokine, and chemokine expression in AP of SCA patients, non-SCA patients, and normal subjects were recently investigated.49 While the levels of these mediators were not statistically different in the SCA and non-SCA patients, the SCA patients had more proinflammatory cytokine increases compared with the normal controls than the non-SCA patients.

## Systemic viral diseases

Numerous viruses are present in the human body and are mostly nonpathogenic and of no clinical significance. However, many other

viruses cause important disease that may have acute or chronic presentation and disease progression. Occasionally, viral infection causes an acute disease that is followed by a dormant stage that can be reactivated throughout life. Many herpesviruses follow the latter course, and some of these are relevant to endodontic disease. A list of important viruses that have been investigated with respect to their role in endodontic disease are listed in Table 9-2.

The interest in viral infection in endodontics began with the identification of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) in the dental pulp62,63 and AP,45 and the concern that the immunodeficiency in
CD4+ cells would render endodontic patients at risk of aggressive endodontic infections. Later studies showed that HIV-positive patients were not susceptible to spreading endodontic infections or flare-ups any more than HIV-negative patients.30,61 Prognosis studies have also shown no difference between HIV-positive patients and controls.3,115

A number of case studies and case series have shown the curious role of varicella zoster virus (VZV) infection in the pathogenesis of pulpitis, pulp necrosis, AP, root resorption, and postherpetic odontalgia.54,67,112,127 The localization of the signs and symptoms in the segment of the mouth that corresponds to the path of VZV blisters presents compelling evidence of the involvement of the virus.
However, despite the widespread presence of VZV infection, these dental findings appear to be rare. A recent systematic review identified only 22 cases in the literature where this has been described, and found no prevalence of VZV implication in population studies in which patients were tested for its presence and the presence of the dental findings.77

A series of studies from the University of Southern California described the association of systemic infection with human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) with the presence of larger and more symptomatic periapical lesions.120,121,128

However, other studies, including one systematic review, did not corroborate these findings.29,75,92 One study showed that these herpesviruses are frequently present in asymptomatic lesions, and

## Smoking

It has been known for decades that smoking adversely affects health in many ways. As noted previously, the causal inference principles were initially studied in the 1960s in the context of the association of smoking with lung cancer.72 Since then, numerous other diseases have been found to be caused or exacerbated by smoking. Among these are many oral diseases such as periodontal disease, oral cancer, oral mucositis, and dysplasia. Smoking was found to increase the risk of root canal treatment when used as a surrogate for pulp and periapical disease.88 Recent systematic reviews have shown the association of smoking with AP in teeth with and without previous root canal treatment.5,25,114 In addition, smoking significantly reduces the outcome of endodontic treatment,42,98 and may increase the chances of pain and postoperative infection following endodontic surgery.57

## Introduction to Section 2

In daily practice, clinicians usually face three diagnostically distinct endodontic conditions requiring professional intervention: vital pulps, necrotic pulps, and retreatment cases (Fig S2-1).17,21 Recognition of the differences between these conditions and clinical decision-making based on these differences is the foundation on which the outcome of root canal treatment is based. The fundamental difference between these conditions resides in the fact that root canal infection is absent in vital pulps, whereas necrotic pulps and retreatment cases pose microbiologic challenges that should be approached accordingly.

A diseased vital pulp is irreversibly inflamed but free of microorganisms colonizing the root canal. The infection is mainly restricted to the surface of the pulp exposed to the oral cavity, and the root canal segment apical to the inflamed pulp is often regarded as sterile.8,11,14,15 As long as it remains vital, the radicular pulp usually manages to protect itself from microbial invasion and colonization.
Given the microbial etiology of apical periodontitis, the traditional distinction between vital and nonvital pulps should, in fact, be changed to noninfected versus infected root canals.

Teeth with necrotic pulps lack the essential defense apparatus to overcome infection and are therefore subject to microbial colonization of the root canal system. Infection is the primary cause of apical periodontitis. The fact that a periradicular radiolucency may not be visible on a radiograph does not necessarily mean that an inflammatory periradicular lesion is absent (Fig S2-2).1,13 Therefore, regardless of whether apical periodontitis is detected radiographically, it is advisable to approach (and treat) root canals containing necrotic pulp tissue as infected canals.

Root canal-treated teeth in need of retreatment because of posttreatment disease can also be regarded as a microbiologic problem. These teeth are associated with a persistent or secondary

As extensively discussed in the first section of this book, apical periodontitis is an infectious disease. As it does not develop in teeth with vital pulps, the best way to prevent it is to keep the pulp vital.
However, no clinical procedure thus far can predictably revert irreversible pulpitis into a noninflamed pulp. The best results are achieved by complete removal of the pulp and nonsurgical root canal treatment to prevent apical periodontitis.
