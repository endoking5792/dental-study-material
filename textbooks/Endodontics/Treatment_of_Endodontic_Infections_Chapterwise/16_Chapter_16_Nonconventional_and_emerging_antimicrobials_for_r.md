## Biofilm as a potential target

The infected root canal harbors a polymicrobial population of obligate/facultative anaerobic, Gram-positive, and Gram-negative bacteria in a biofilm mode of growth.5,76-78,102 The susceptibility of bacteria to an antimicrobial is determined by their cell wall characteristics. Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria have profound differences in their three-dimensional (3D) cell architecture.
The membrane barrier of a bacterium limits the diffusion of antimicrobials into the cytosol. The membrane barriers of a Grampositive bacterium are relatively thicker, with interconnecting peptidoglycan layers. The teichoic acid residues of the cell wall provide the negative charge, while serving as binding sites for cationic molecules. In a Gram-negative bacterium, the outer membrane is composed of a thinner peptidoglycan layer. The diffusion of molecules across a Gram-negative cell wall is severely regulated at the outer membrane, which is rich in lipopolysaccharides (LPS).21

In addition to the inherent resistance to antimicrobials, bacteria display considerably high resistance to antimicrobials when they are in a biofilm mode.18 The tolerance mechanisms to antimicrobial agents in a bacterial biofilm are associated with: 1) the extracellular polymeric matrix; 2) the slow growth rate and nutrient availability; 3) the adoption of phenotypic resistance; and 4) the increased persistor cell population (non-growing phenotypic variants).51 Furthermore, the resident bacteria in a biofilm upregulates the expression of stressresponse genes, shock proteins, and multidrug pumps (efflux pumps), switching the biofilm bacteria to a more resistant phenotype.51 It is accepted that no single mechanism accounts for the enhanced tolerance, but that several different mechanisms orchestrate to provide biofilm bacteria with increased tolerance to antimicrobials.23,34 The clinical implications of bacterial biofilm are shown in Figure 16-1.

Apical periodontitis is a typical example of a biofilm-mediated infection caused by the opportunistic bacteria in the oral cavity.86 The elimination or substantial reduction of root canal bacteria and the prevention of recontamination remain the essential elements for a favorable long-term prognosis in endodontic treatment. However, clinical investigations have shown that even after meticulous chemomechanical disinfection, bacteria still persist in the uninstrumented portions and anatomical complexities of the root canal.75 Advances in disinfection strategies and novel antimicrobials are employed in endodontics with the aim of circumventing these challenges. Ideally, these disinfection strategies should eliminate biofilm bacteria from the uninstrumented portions and anatomical complexities of the root canal system without inducing untoward effects on dentin substrate and periradicular tissue.51

## Root canal disinfection

Conventional topical antimicrobials in endodontics are chemical-based disinfectants. The chemical antibacterial effects are attributed to a combination of mechanisms or chemical injury to the bacteria. Sodium hypochlorite, iodine-potassium iodide, hydrogen peroxide, and chlorhexidine are some of the common chemical-based disinfectants.
Sodium hypochlorite (1% to 6%) and iodine-potassium iodide (2% to
5%) are halogen-releasing agents. Hydrogen peroxide (3% to 30%) is an oxidant, while chlorhexidine (0.2% to 2%) is a cationic chlorophenyl-biguanide.38
These chemicals provided significant antimicrobial efficacy, apart from showing conspicuous cytotoxic effects. This is more of a concern in terms of disinfecting the apical 3 to 4 mm of root canals, mainly due to the proximity of this area to the periapical tissues.51 Reports of severe tissue injuries following sodium hypochlorite extrusion have been documented in the endodontic literature.119 Thus, several newer topical antimicrobials have been tested for root canal disinfection. In this respect, advanced nonconventional antimicrobials display strong antibacterial activity

## Immune system and inflammation

The immune system refers to a group of cells and proteins that function to protect the dentoalveolar tissue and other regions from foreign antigens (microbes). The immune system is regarded as having two layers of defense, being innate immunity and adaptive immunity. In a tooth, once the protective enamel of the cementum layer is compromised, innate immunity is activated as the first line of defense in response to invading microbes. The innate layer of immunity is an antigen-independent (non-specific) defense mechanism that is used by the host immediately or within hours of encountering an antigen. Numerous cells are involved in the innate immune response such as phagocytes (macrophages and neutrophils), dendritic cells, mast cells, basophils, eosinophils, and natural killer (NK) cells. Adaptive immunity is the next line of defense. It develops when the innate immunity is ineffective and the microbial insult is

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 903 -->

sustained. It is considered to be an antigen-dependent and antigenspecific host response that is evoked after a lag period following the exposure to bacterial antigens. The functions of the adaptive immune response are the recognition of specific foreign antigens and the generation of pathogen-specific immunologic effector pathways that eliminate the pathogens or pathogen-infected cells specifically. The cells of the adaptive immune system include T cells, which are activated through the action of antigen-presenting cells (APCs), and B cells. Innate and adaptive immunity are complementary host response mechanisms (Fig 16-2). Any deficiencies in either of these systems result in altered host susceptibility.112

## Cytokines and immune modulation

Cytokines are small proteins produced by the immune cells for cell– cell signaling, cell recruitment, and specific immune functions during inflammation. Proinflammatory cytokines, which include chemokines, are small protein-signaling molecules that are secreted from immune cells and other cells that promote inflammation. Some examples of proinflammatory cytokines are interleukin-1 (IL-1), IL-12, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interferon gamma (IFN-γ), and granulocyte-macrophage colony stimulating factor (G-MCSF). These cytokines are mainly produced (neutrophils, macrophages, and dendritic cells) to upregulate the immune responses, which includes activation of macrophages, induction of apoptosis, and recruitment of additional immune cells. The anti-inflammatory cytokines are secreted

<!-- PDF page 905 -->

from the immune cells (regulatory T cells and some macrophages) to suppress inflammation. The list of anti-inflammatory cytokines is also large, but the primary ones that are the focus of this chapter include
IL-4, IL-10, and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β). The role of cells trafficking between sites of infection and the draining lymph nodes is crucial in immune dynamics. Lymphocytes work individually and coordinate with each other to perform their immune functions.
Specific immune cells such as macrophages and some CD4+ T cells secrete proinflammatory signals (IFN-γ, TNF-α, etc) and perform inflammatory functions. Cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and activated macrophages kill pathogens and infected host cells, either directly or by induction of cell apoptosis. In contrast, other cell types such as regulatory T cells serve to suppress or shut down the immune response via cell–cell contact and cytokine secretion.15

In most cases, excess inflammation is the major reason for the dysregulation of normal wound healing. ## Antibacterial nanoparticles

A recommendation of the
European
Commission defines nanomaterials as a natural, incidental, or manufactured material containing particles in an unbound state, as an aggregate or as an agglomerate, and where one or more external dimensions is in the size range 1 to 100 nm for 50% or more of the particles in the number size distribution.79 There are distinct and significant advantages offered by nano-sized systems in therapeutic applications, including: 1) adjustable and controllable functionalities;
2) ability to penetrate different tissue compartments, and 3) ability to effectively interact with biomolecules. While the nanometric physical dimension offers many advantages with respect to transport, passive targeting, and increased surface availability of molecules, the chemistry of core particle and coating (functionalized) constituents provides additional vistas for customizing target specificity, cellular trafficking, and the sustained release of therapeutics (Fig 16-4).
There is also the exceptional possibility of embedding multiple functionalities into a single nanometric particle for controlled bioactive molecule release systems.94

### Types and applications

There are two types of antibacterial nanoparticles: 1) the bare nanometric particle that possess antibacterial characteristics (Ex:ZnO); and 2) a nanoparticle wherein an antibacterial component is functionalized with a non-antibacterial nano-sized substrate (e.g.
vancomycin functionalized on mesoporous silica nanoparticles).
Magnesium oxide (MgO) and calcium oxide (CaO) slurries inhibit
Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria in a bactericidal manner,37

while zinc oxide (ZnO) slurry acts in a bacteriostatic manner and exhibits stronger antibacterial activity against Gram-positive than
Gram-negative bacteria.113 MgO, CaO, and ZnO may also generate

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 909 -->

active oxygen, which is responsible for their antibacterial effect. The nanoparticles of these metallic oxides with high surface area and charge density offer significant antibacterial efficacy.88
The electrostatic interaction between cationic nanoparticles and anionic bacterial cells results in the aggregation of nanoparticles on the bacterial cell wall, which in turn results in increased membrane permeability and rapid membrane dysfunction. Additionally, different heavy metal ions produce different effects on bacterial cell functions.85,100,114 Copper ions induce oxidative stresses, resulting in membrane and DNA damage,16 while silver ions inactivate proteins and inhibit DNA replication.26 Nanoparticles of silver (Ag), copper oxide (CuO), and ZnO are currently used as antimicrobials.47

The incorporation of antimicrobials or antibiotics in a biodegradable polymer has been an effective strategy to achieve a controlled release pattern of the retained drug.51 The nature of interaction between the drug and the polymer substrate would influence the pattern of drug release. In the encapsulation technique, the drug is embedded within a micro- or nano-sized polymeric substrate. On the other hand, in the adsorption technique, the drug is adsorbed to the micro- or nano-sized polymer surface. Based on the technique used for drug incorporation and the rate of degradation of the polymeric substrate, different drug release patterns are anticipated.
The following criteria are recommended when engineering a controlled drug releasing system: 1) It is essential to achieve a controlled release profile that matches the treatment paradigm with optimal efficiency; 2) the drug loaded system should retain the biologic activity of the drug for the entire therapeutic period;
and 3) the biomaterial substrate used for drug loading should be biocompatible with acceptable biodegradability.51,94

Adherence of microorganisms to a biomaterial surface is an important and early step in biofilm formation. Microbial adherence enables bacteria to evade the flushing actions of saliva, while permitting them to survive a harsh environment.2,11,12,44 Application of chemical antimicrobials would result in varying degrees of ultrastructural and chemical alterations on dentin, which in turn

promotes bacterial adherence in some cases. Chemically induced demineralization of dentin exposes the collagen matrix, which is an ideal substrate for bacterial binding, including E. faecalis.49 Thus, it could be suggested that the reactivity of different chemicals used in root canals would influence bacterial recolonization. When used to condition dentin, cationic antibacterial nanoparticles have the potential to annul such iatrogenic effects. These nanoparticles adhered electrostatically to the dentin surface, and although it was a weak interaction, the nanoparticle-conditioned layer impeded bacterial recolonization.49

Chitosan (CS) is a natural cationic biopolymer derived from the deacetylation of chitin. It shows excellent antimicrobial and antifungal properties. The precise mechanisms of the antibacterial action of CS and its derivatives are still not well understood, although the electrostatic interaction between cationic nanoparticles and bacteria is believed to cause cell killing.84 The rate of bacterial killing by cationic antibacterial nanoparticles depends on the concentration and duration of interaction. In a study93 testing a 72-hour interaction, planktonic bacteria were totally eliminated, in contrast to biofilm bacteria. However, CS and ZnO nanoparticles retained their antibacterial properties even after aging in saliva for 90 days.93

Previous studies have examined the antimicrobial properties of ZnO and epoxy resin-based endodontic sealers loaded with CS and ZnO nanoparticles.48 The addition of antibacterial nanoparticles in root canal sealers improved the direct and diffusible antibacterial effects in endodontic sealers without compromising the flow characteristics. CS nanoparticles also reduced bacterial adherence to dentin. The treatment of root dentin with ZnO nanoparticles, ZnO/CS mix nanoparticles, CS-layer ZnO nanoparticles, or CS nanoparticles resulted in a reduction of the adherence of E. faecalis to dentin.48

It is reported that engineered CS nanoparticles not only decreased microbial bioburden with time, but also reduced proinflammatory mediators such as nitric oxide, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL6, while increasing the anti-inflammatory marker TGF-β1, cell survival, and cell spreading over time.42 This favorable host cell/immune response produced by engineered CS nanoparticles requires further attention. It is important to recognize that the effective application of nanomaterials in endodontics will depend on the therapeutic efficacy of the applied nanoparticles as well as their dispersibility into the root canal system. These aspects need further research.

### Potential toxicity

The distinct properties that make nanoparticles/nanomaterials useful for therapeutic applications may also present potential harmful consequences in humans. It is suggested that there are general features and material-specific features that determine the levels of

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 914 -->

toxicity (biologic hazard) in nanomaterials.
The physical characteristics such as size, surface area, shape, aspect ratio, surface chemistry, dispersion state, and bioretention constitute some of the general features. The chemical properties such as dissolution chemistry, band-gap energy, and surface defects are some of the material-specific features that govern the potential for toxicity in nanomaterials. It is suggested that the variations in the general features and the material-specific features could contribute individually or combinatorically to toxicity in nanomaterials.30

Generalizing the nanoparticle–cell/tissue interactions and their potential therapeutic impact or toxicity is challenging, since different nanoparticles present unique sets of features, which differ between different tissues, disease conditions, and therapeutic applications.
Developing standardized testing methods to understand and/or predict the interaction between nanoparticles and biologic systems is an evolving field. It is crucial to correlate well-conducted in vitro experimental findings with in vivo assessments prior to the clinical application of nanomaterials/nanoparticles.
Furthermore, nanoparticles can enter the human body via inhalation, ingestion, or skin contact. Therefore, nontherapeutic routes of exposure should be prevented in both patients and health care providers.30

## Antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (APDT)

APDT applies a photosensitizer solution, which photosensitizes the bacteria, followed by light irradiation at an optimal wavelength/energy to kill bacteria, viruses, and spores. The photosensitizer in the ground state is a spectroscopic singlet (S0). After the absorption of photon, it passes from the ground state to the first excited state (S1), then returns to the ground state or passes to a triplet excited state (T1) via intersystem crossing. The photosensitizer in the T1 state is extremely reactive, and therefore destroys the bacteria in the following pathways: 1) Type I reaction, where the photosensitizer in the T1

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 915 -->

state can react with a target other than the oxygen by hydrogen or electron transfer, resulting in radical ions that can react with oxygen, forming reactive oxygen intermediates; or 2) Type II reaction, where the photosensitizer in the T1 state can transfer the excitation energy to the ground state molecular oxygen to produce the excited singlet oxygen (1O2).20

Singlet oxygen is a strong oxidizing agent with high reactivity, besides a lifetime of < 0.04 μs in a biologic environment and a radius of action of < 0.02 μm.72 Breaks have been reported on Grampositive and Gram-negative bacteria in both single- and doublestranded DNA with APDT as well as the disappearance of plasmid supercoiled fraction.6,70 The photooxidative effect of APDT also causes degradation of bacterial membrane proteins, damage to membrane integrity, and inactivation of LPS33,53,106 (Fig 16-6). These findings strongly support the theory that APDT is a viable alternative antimicrobial, with a mode of action markedly different from that of other typical antimicrobials. Previous studies have demonstrated the antibacterial efficacy of APDT with a range of photosensitizers, including methylene blue and toluidine blue (TBO) as photosensitizers in root canal models.69,96

### Photosensitizers and light sources

A photosensitizer is a chemical that produces reactive oxygen species when activated with light. Large numbers of photosensitizers have been tested for APDT. Photosensitizers such as methylene blue,
TBO, rose bengal, erythrosine, chlorin (e6), and hematoporphyrin have been investigated against oral pathogens.
Ideally, a photosensitizer solution should be stable in vivo, while an ideal concentration and volume of photosensitizer should not be able to induce any cytotoxic effect. In dentistry, compounds based on phenothiazinium are emerging as promising photosensitizers for
APDT, e.g.
methylene blue and
TBO.33,104
Apart from phenothiazinium compounds, porphyrins,105 phthalocyanines,71 and chlorins39 have gained in popularity as photosensitizers. Besides their antimicrobial effects, the application of photosensitizers such as 5aminolevulinic acid (ALA) and silver nanoparticle-loaded TiO2 nanotubes (Ag/TiO2-NTs) have been shown to provide a significant immune modulatory effect in vitro.13,41

Conjugating photosensitizer to other chemical moieties can improve photosensitizer characteristics in APDT. These modifications are commonly aimed at improving antibacterial efficacy, reducing toxicity, or achieving bacterial selectivity in APDT. Covalently binding rose bengal (photosensitizer) to small polystyrene beads allowed improved photosensitization of bacteria and subsequent killing.7

## Antimicrobial peptides and peptide mimetics

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) represent a wide range of hostdefense molecules with potent antimicrobial activities against a broad spectrum of microorganisms, while displaying low propensity for bacterial resistance.3,36,45 These molecules also play key roles in developing innate immunity and immunomodulation.4,14,57 The majority of mammalian AMPs are small cationic molecules. The overall positive charge of AMPs favors rapid interaction with the microbial cell membrane. On the contrary, the mammalian cell membrane is prone to be zwitterionic (no net charge), and therefore is less favorable for electrostatic interaction. This variation in cell membrane characteristics explains the selectivity of AMPs for microbial cells.
Although the cationic nature of AMPs facilitates their initial interaction with the microbial cell membrane, it is their hydrophobic and/or amphipathic nature that enables their penetration into the lipid membrane.81 Generally, AMPs target microbial cell membranes, causing the formation of isolated pores and membrane disruption, which in turn results in the leakage of cytoplasmic contents and cell

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 922 -->

death in minutes. AMPs may also cause bacterial cell death by inhibiting microbial DNA/protein synthesis or by interacting with other intracellular targets81 (Fig 16-8).

### Natural antimicrobial peptides

Natural AMPs that possess antimicrobial activities against oral pathogenic bacteria are found in the oral cavity.63 Odontoblasts, fibroblasts, nerves, and stem cells are some of the sources for AMPs in dental pulp. Natural AMPs found within dental pulp contribute to the clearance of infection, promote immunomodulation, and initiate repair and regeneration. These peptides can be exploited for therapeutic control of infection and to improve regenerative endodontic therapies.
Recently, interest in AMPs has been growing due to the rise in antibiotic resistance. There are three important families of AMPs in the oral cavity: defensins, histatins, and cathelicidins. Histatins are histidine-rich μ helical peptides found in human saliva103 but not detected in dental pulp. Defensins comprise two peptide families, i.e.
α- and β-defensins. Defensins have six cysteine residues that form three intramolecular disulfide bridges. The characteristics of the disulfide bridges determine their classification. The only member of the cathelicidin family found in humans is LL-37. Disulfide bridges are absent in LL-37. They have a random coil conformation in hydrophilic environments but maintain an α-helical structure in hydrophobic environments.63

## Natural polyphenols

Natural polyphenols are present in a variety of plants and are characterized by the presence of more than one phenol units per molecule.10,24 They are well known for their antimicrobial properties, which are influenced by the bacterial species/strains tested, type of polyphenols, concentration of polyphenols, density of microbial cells, synergistic effects with other antimicrobials, and temperature.
Anacardic acid found in the liquid extract of cashew nut shell has been shown to exhibit antimicrobial activities against Streptococcus mutans and Staphylococcus aureus.54,55 Morinda citrifolia is another herb that has a broad range of antibacterial, antiviral, antifungal, analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and immune-enhancing effects.59,111

Morinda citrifolia juice (MCJ) contains the antibacterial compounds Lasperuloside and alizarin. When assessed on E. faecalis-infected root dentin, it was observed that chlorhexidine gluconate (100%) produced better antimicrobial efficacy, followed by 2% povidoneiodine (PVP-1) (87%), propolis (71%), MCJ (69%), and calcium hydroxide (55%).46 Another study showed that the efficacy of MJC to remove the smear layer was similar to sodium hypochlorite alternated with EDTA.74

## Summary

Apical periodontitis is a biofilm-mediated infection. Therapeutic methods to eliminate root canal biofilm is the primary focus of root canal treatment. Despite some noteworthy technological advances over the past two decades, the root canal environment still remains a challenging niche in terms of predictably eliminating surface-adherent bacterial biofilm. Advanced non-conventional antimicrobials are a class of antimicrobials that display potent antibiofilm characteristics with low rates of bacterial resistance and cellular toxicity, besides their distinct immune modulatory capabilities. These agents, if employed as a final irrigant or intracanal medication, have the potential to present supplementary antimicrobial as well as immunomodulatory effects, reducing periradicular inflammation while promoting rapid and predictable periapical tissue healing.
