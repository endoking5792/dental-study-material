Healthcare professionals are involved with the prevention and treatment of diseases. In order to successfully diagnose, prevent, or treat any disease, the clinician must have advanced knowledge of the etiology, pathophysiology, and clinical manifestations of the disease.
Insufficient knowledge of these aspects can cause considerable frustration to both clinician and patient as a result of inadequate diagnosis and treatment.

Endodontists are primarily concerned with apical periodontitis, an inflammatory disease of the periradicular tissues with an infectious etiology (Fig 3-1). Inflammation is the main response of periradicular tissues to a variety of stimuli that cause tissue injury. The intensity of the inflammatory response depends more on the intensity than on the type of the aggression. Since aggression disrupts tissue integrity, the inflammatory response is intended to localize the damaged tissues and prepare the area for the subsequent repair process. However, in many diseases, host defenses may be responsible for significant tissue damage. In apical periodontitis, tissue destruction caused by the host defenses seems to be more significant than the direct effects of microbial products egressing from the infected root canal, even though it is the microorganisms that triggered the entire periradicular inflammatory process.

Injury to the pulp and the apical periodontal ligament can be of a biologic, physical (thermal or mechanical) or chemical nature.
Although physical and chemical injury can initiate pulp and periradicular inflammation, these types of injury are usually transient.
Therefore, inflammation develops but does not persist. As microorganisms represent a form of biologic aggression that is often persistent, the inflammatory response to microbial injury is also persistent. For this reason, microorganisms are essential for the development and perpetuation of apical periodontitis.3,7,9,14,20 Such persistence is related to the fact that microorganisms colonizing the necrotic root canal are by and large protected from the effects of the host defenses that are located in the periradicular tissues (Fig 3-2).
Only those microorganisms that leave the root canal for the inflamed periradicular tissues can be effectively combated and eliminated. The

inflammatory response at the periradicular tissue level limits the spread of the infection to the bone and other body sites. Apical periodontitis is usually characterized by bone resorption, which permits bone to strategically retreat from the infected area (root canal) and be replaced by inflamed tissue. ## Host defenses against bacteria – an overview

The immune response to bacteria penetrating and proliferating into host tissues is divided into innate immunity, which is the first line of defense, and adaptive immunity, which is a more sophisticated and effective response in terms of recognizing bacterial antigens, combating infection, and preventing reinfection. Innate immunity is an initial defense acting immediately after the introduction of bacteria in tissues. The principal mechanisms of innate immunity against bacteria are complement activation, phagocytosis, and the inflammatory response. In innate immunity, complement activation can occur either by the alternative pathway (induced by bacterial structural components such as peptidoglycan, lipopolysaccharide [LPS], and lipoteichoic acid [LTA]) or by the lectin pathway (induced by bacteria expressing mannose on the cell surface). Complement activation results in byproducts involved in:

opsonization for enhanced phagocytosis of bacteria;
formation of membrane attack complexes, which lyse bacteria;
stimulation of inflammatory responses.

An adaptive immune response associated with the generation of antigen-specific effector cells that specifically target the pathogen and memory cells that can prevent reinfection with the same bacteria will not occur unless the innate host defenses are sidestepped, evaded, or overwhelmed. Adaptive immunity is divided into two branches: humoral immunity and cellular immunity.

Humoral immunity entails the production of specific antibodies with various mechanisms to combat infection, including neutralization of virulence factors, opsonization, and phagocytosis of bacterial cells, and activation of complement by the classical pathway.

Cellular immunity is related to the action of CD4+ T cells activated by proteic bacterial antigens. Activated CD4+ T cells produce cytokines that:

stimulate antibody production;
induce local inflammation; and enhance the phagocytic and killing effects of macrophages and neutrophils.

The adaptive immune response is mounted in the lymph node that drains the site of infection (Fig 3-4). Then, the infectious process is usually controlled, and the participating bacteria are contained or killed.

## Innate immunity

The innate immunity is mostly represented by mechanical and chemical barriers against microbial invasion (e.g. dental enamel and the epithelium of the mucosa and skin), phagocytic cells (neutrophils and macrophages), dendritic cells, mast cells, natural killer (NK) and

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 116 -->

other innate lymphoid cells, and blood proteins (e.g. components of the complement system and other inflammatory mediators).

After microorganisms overcome the natural mechanical and chemical barriers and reach the subjacent connective tissue, the first line of innate defense can be subdivided into an early preinflammatory phase, where resident macrophages and the complement system try to eliminate bacteria, and the inflammatory response, characterized by induced vascular changes and extravasation and migration of neutrophils and monocytes to the infected site. Actually, the hallmark of innate immunity against microorganisms is inflammation. The term inflammation is derived from Latin word infflamare, which means “to set on fire.”28

Inflammation can be defined as the reaction of the microcirculation to tissue injury, with consequent movement of intravascular elements such as fluids, molecules, and cells to the extravascular space.17

Microorganisms that penetrate tissues and begin to replicate are immediately recognized by resident macrophages. These cells express surface receptors that are able to recognize and bind constituents of bacterial surfaces. Binding of bacterial components to these receptors triggers the engulfment of bacterial cells by macrophages and induces the release of cytokines and other mediators which, in turn, induce the vascular and cellular changes that will ultimately characterize the inflammation process. Vessels become dilated, their permeability increases, and endothelial cells become increasingly adhesive to circulating leukocytes. Chemokines first attract neutrophils (polymorphonuclear leukocytes [PMNs]) and then monocytes to enter the tissue. Therefore, resident macrophages first encounter bacteria invading the tissue, but they are soon reinforced by large numbers of neutrophils and, later, macrophages recruited to the infected site.

The complement system activated by the lectin and alternative pathways is also an important factor participating in the early phase of the innate response. In the lectin pathway, activation is triggered by mannan-binding lectin, a normal serum constituent that binds some encapsulated bacteria. In the alternative pathway, activation is triggered directly by components of the bacterial cell surface. As mentioned before, the three main consequences of complement activation are opsonization of pathogens, recruitment of inflammatory cells, and direct killing of pathogens.

The innate pre-inflammatory response leads to activation of macrophages and the complement system, resulting in the release of proinflammatory substances. Also, microorganisms can cause some tissue damage that induces the release of mediators from cells in the damaged area. The inflammatory response that then develops is intended to localize the injured area, eliminate the injurious agents, and remove degenerated tissue. The clear purpose of all these activities is to prepare the affected area for subsequent healing. In this context, inflammation involves:

### Vascular events in inflammation

In response to tissue injury and initial recognition of microorganisms by phagocytes and the complement system, substances are released that induce vasodilation (initially in arterioles) with a subsequent increase in vascular blood pressure and blood delivery to the affected area (hyperemia). The increase in blood pressure provokes an increase in transudation of fluids with low proteic content at the capillary level. As more blood reaches the area, capillaries and venules also dilate.
The next phase in the inflammatory response is an increase in vascular permeability due to the increased blood pressure and, in particular, the action of chemical mediators released in the injured area. Venules are mostly affected. The increase in vascular permeability permits a protein-rich fluid (exudate) to leave the vessel for the extravascular compartment. Exudation results in an increase in blood viscosity and a decrease in blood flow. This abnormal leakage of fluids from the vessels also results in the accumulation of exudate in the extravascular space (edema), which leads to an increase in tissue hydrostatic pressure. This results in swelling and pain, two important signs of inflammation, and allows for the accumulation of plasma proteins involved in host defense.
As the blood flow continues to decrease, vascular stasis may eventually occur. The loss of fluid leads to the concentration of red blood cells and increased viscosity. PMNs leave their axial

position in the vessel and take a more peripheral position near the endothelial lining (margination).
Cytokines such as interleukin-1 (IL-1) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), which are produced at the injured site in response to the recognition of bacterial structural components, induce the expression of adhesion molecules on the endothelial cells of venules (Fig 3-5).
These adhesion molecules mediate the binding of circulating leukocytes to the vessel wall. The combination of slowed blood flow and induced adhesion molecules allows leukocytes to attach to the endothelium and migrate into the tissues. The process of leukocyte migration involves a series of steps, starting with lowaffinity leukocyte binding to and rolling along the endothelial surface. In sequence, the leukocytes become firmly attached to the endothelium. Temporal differences in expression of adhesion molecules on endothelial cells explain the different affinities of leukocyte receptors to endothelium. The next step in the process is migration through the endothelium in a process called diapedesis. By this mechanism, leukocytes extend pseudopods through the widened interendothelial junctions and migrate to the extravascular space. Chemokines act on the adherent leukocytes and stimulate the cells to migrate through interendothelial spaces toward chemotactic stimuli that drive the cells to the injured area (Fig 3-6). As with increased vascular permeability, diapedesis occurs predominantly in venules. Emigration of the leukocyte in tissues toward the site of injury is called chemotaxis.

The magnitude of the acute inflammatory response is related to the intensity of the injury and not necessarily the type of injury. The more virulent the microorganisms and/or the higher the microbial density involved in an infectious process, the higher the damage to the tissues and consequently the higher the intensity of inflammation.
Infections by less virulent microorganisms and/or reduced microbial load tend to cause a milder host defense response.

Inflammation can also result in additional tissue damage. For instance, the same reactions of neutrophils and macrophages that function to eradicate extracellular bacteria can also cause tissue damage by local production and release of reactive oxygen species and lysosomal enzymes. In most instances, inflammatory reactions are self-limited and controlled, but persistence of the source of infection may lead to prolonged and sometimes uncontrolled inflammation.

Cytokines secreted in response to bacterial products also stimulate the production of acute-phase proteins and lead to systemic manifestations of the infection. Sepsis is a dramatic result of severe infection by some Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria (as well as some fungi), in which viable microorganisms and/or their products flourish in the blood to cause systemic disorders. Septic shock is the most severe form of sepsis and is frequently fatal. It is characterized by circulatory collapse and disseminated intravascular coagulation.
The early phase of bacterial sepsis is caused by cytokines produced by macrophages in response to bacterial cell wall components, especially LPS, LTA, and/or peptidoglycan. Tumor necrosis factor (TNF), IL-6, and IL-1 are the main cytokine mediators of sepsis, but
INF-γ and IL-12 may also contribute.

### Chemical mediators of inflammation

Vascular changes are induced by a variety of inflammatory chemical mediators released in response to bacterial recognition and tissue injury.12,16 They are responsible for induction, control, and amplification of the vascular and cellular events of inflammation.

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 123 -->

These substances are called endogenous chemical mediators of inflammation and can be vasoactive and chemotactic to inflammatory cells, act as opsonins, destroy bacteria, cause tissue damage, and induce pain (Table 3-1).

Chemical mediators originate from either plasma or cells. Plasmaderived mediators are present in plasma in a precursor form that must be activated, usually by a series of proteolytic cleavages.
Activation usually occurs after damage to the blood vessels or leakage of plasma into the tissues. Plasma-derived mediators include bradykinin and the complement, fibrinolytic, and clotting systems.
Cell-derived mediators can be found in a preformed intracellular state or can be immediately produced by host cells in response to a stimulus. Examples of these mediators include histamine, arachidonic acid metabolites (prostaglandins and leukotrienes), lysosomal enzymes, matrix metalloproteinases, oxygen metabolites, platelet activating factor, nitric oxide, neuropeptides, cytokines, and chemokines.
Cellular sources include neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, mast cells, platelets, endothelium, fibroblasts, osteoblasts, and epithelium. Once activated and/or released from the cell, most of these mediators are short-lived.

Acute-phase response involves a shift in the proteins secreted by the liver into the blood plasma and results from the action of IL-1, IL6, and TNF-α on hepatocytes. Proteins whose synthesis is induced by these cytokines are called acute-phase proteins. One of these proteins, C-reactive protein, can bind to bacterial cells and act as an opsonin or activate the complement system.

### Eliminating extracellular bacteria

The main defense mechanisms of the innate immunity against extracellular bacteria consist of phagocytosis, extracellular traps, and complement activation.

### a) Phagocytosis and extracellular traps

Phagocytes, including neutrophils and macrophages, are cells whose primary function is to recognize, ingest, and destroy microorganisms.
These cells ingest bound microbes into vesicles by the process of

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 129 -->

phagocytosis, which is an active, energy-dependent process of engulfment of large particles (> 0.5 μm in diameter).

The first step in phagocytosis is the recognition of bacterial cells by phagocytes. After migrating to the tissue, phagocytes must first recognize the cells or particles to be phagocytosed; herein resides the importance of opsonization. Through the recognition of some components of the bacterial surface, bacteria coated by opsonins such as C3b, IgG1, and IgG3, pentraxins (e.g. C-reactive protein), collectins, or ficolins are readily phagocytosed because phagocytes have surface receptors specific for these ligands.

Binding of the opsonized particle or cell to the leukocyte receptor triggers engulfment. Once a bacterial cell or particle binds to receptors on a phagocyte, the plasma membrane in the region of the receptors begins to redistribute and extends a projection around the cell/particle to be engulfed (Fig 3-7). When the membrane projection extends beyond the diameter of the cell/particle, the top of the projection closes over so that the cell/particle becomes completely enclosed within an intracellular vesicle or phagocytic vacuole. This vacuole, or phagosome, contains the ingested cell/particle and becomes segregated from the cytoplasm by the plasma membrane (Fig 3-7). The cell surface receptors deliver activating signals that stimulate the microbicidal activities of the phagocyte. Neutrophils and macrophages are professional phagocytes; once activated, their phagocytic capacity increases significantly.

In sequence, the membrane lining the phagosome fuses to the limiting membrane of cytoplasmic granules (e.g. lysosomes), forming the phagolysosome, and the contents of these granules are discharged into the phagocytic vacuole (see Fig 3-7). These cytoplasmic granules contain several microbicidal elements, including myeloperoxidase, defensins, azurocidin, cathelicidin, lactoferrin, and lysozyme.

Therefore, if they enter the extracellular environment, they are capable of causing tissue injury. Pus formation is primarily due to leakage of granular contents of neutrophils.

### b) Complement activation

Bacterial structural components such as peptidoglycan, LTA (from
Gram-positive bacteria), and LPS (from Gram-negative bacteria) can activate the complement system by the alternative pathway. Bacteria that express mannose on their surface may bind a mannose-binding lectin, which activates the complement system by the lectin pathway.
One result of the complement activation is opsonization and enhanced phagocytosis of extracellular bacteria. Some products of complement activation are anaphylatoxins that mediate inflammation by causing vasodilation, release of histamine from mast cells, and increased vascular permeability. Finally, complement activation results in the formation of the membrane attack complex on the bacterial surface to cause cellular lysis and death.

### Outcome of inflammation

Depending on the results of the inflammatory response, the inflammation may resolve completely (repair) or become chronic. If the acute inflammatory response succeeds in eliminating the injurious agent, repair of the damaged tissues occurs by regeneration, scar formation, or both. Resolution of inflammation is the usual outcome when tissue destruction is mild and the damaged parenchyma is expected to repair. Resolution involves:10

decay of chemical mediators and subsequent reestablishment of normal vascular permeability;
drainage of edema into lymphatics and subsequent return to normal tissue pressure;
phagocytosis of apoptotic leukocytes and necrotic debris by macrophages;
production of growth factors by macrophages, which orchestrate the subsequent repair process.

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 132 -->

However, there are situations where the tissue response advances to chronic inflammation. This may follow acute inflammation, and the transition from acute to chronic inflammation often occurs when the acute inflammatory response cannot be resolved, mainly due to persistence of the injurious agent. However, in certain cases, the response may be chronic right from the onset. Chronic inflammation has a strong component of the adaptive immune response. Persistent injury and chronic inflammation usually result in tissue damage.

Macrophages are dominant cells associated with most chronic inflammatory conditions, including apical periodontitis. To have their functions optimized, macrophages can be activated by two pathways:
classical and alternative. Classical activation occurs by microbial products (LPS, LTA, etc) and T-cell–derived signals, especially IFN-γ.
Macrophages activated by the classical pathway are also known as the M1 phenotype and are involved with killing microorganisms and producing proinflammatory cytokines.
On the other hand, macrophages activated by the alternative pathway (M2 phenotype), which involves cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13, are not actively killing microorganisms and their main function is to produce growth factors that orchestrate the tissue repair process.10

## Adaptive immunity

Adaptive immunity is initiated when innate immunity fails to eradicate the infection, and microbial antigens in soluble form or most frequently captured by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) are delivered to the draining lymph nodes. Here, antigens are regarded as molecules that are either expressed on the microbial cell surface or released in soluble form and recognized as non-self by lymphocyte surface receptors. Naive T and B lymphocytes are continually recirculating through lymph nodes. They come to the node through an artery, leave the circulation, and enter the stroma of the node through specialized vessels, called high endothelial venules. When a recirculating naive lymphocyte encounters its specific antigen in the lymph node, it is

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 133 -->

induced to proliferate; it then differentiates into effector cells that will orchestrate the elimination of the infectious agent (Fig 3-9). A subset of these lymphocytes differentiates into memory cells, which are prepared for a subsequent encounter with the same antigen to which they are specific.

The adaptive immune response increases in magnitude and efficacy with each successive exposure to a given antigen. Adaptive immunity presents exquisite specificity for distinct antigenic molecules. Thus, it has an extraordinary capacity to distinguish between different microorganisms and molecules, even if closely related. For this reason, it is also called specific immunity. The adaptive response can also remember and respond more vigorously to repeated exposure to the same antigen, a property referred to as immunological memory.

The main components of adaptive immunity are lymphocytes and their secreted products, including antibodies and cytokines. There are two types of adaptive immune responses:

Humoral immunity, which is mediated by B lymphocytes and their secreted products (antibodies), the molecules involved in the recognition of foreign particles; and
Cell-mediated immunity, which is mediated by T lymphocytes and their secreted products (mostly cytokines).

The adaptive immune response is the major defense component involved in chronic inflammation to persistent microorganisms.

Antigen recognition occurs after a microbial antigen interacts with surface receptors on lymphocytes. In B lymphocytes, these receptors are mainly membrane-bound antibody molecules that recognize antigens of different types, including proteins, polysaccharides, and lipids. In T lymphocytes, receptors recognize only small peptide sequences bound to major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules presented on APCs.1 Antigen recognition provides specificity to the immune response.

Lymphocyte activation comprises proliferation and differentiation.
Basically, three signals are required for lymphocytes to proliferate and further differentiate into effector cells: antigen binding by lymphocyte surface receptors, costimulator molecules, and cytokines.
After recognition of the antigen by a specific lymphocyte clone, the latter proliferates, resulting in clonal expansion. This amplifies the defense response as the number of clones specific for a given antigen increases substantially.

Next, lymphocytes differentiate into the effector cells responsible for antigen elimination. B lymphocytes differentiate into plasma cells, which are antibody-secreting cells, while T cells differentiate into effector T cells, which activate macrophages or directly lyse cells infected by intracellular pathogens such as viruses.

### Humoral immunity – sequence of events

Within 1 or 2 days of entry into the host tissue, macrophages and/or dendritic cells take up the antigen and travel to the regional lymph nodes where the antigen will be presented to naive CD4+ T cells. After antigen recognition, T cells are activated, induced to express the CD40 ligand (CD40L)/CD154, and produce cytokines. Following a chemokine gradient, activated helper T cells then migrate toward the follicle of the lymphoid organ. Naive B cells reside in or recirculate through the follicles. Macrophages also produce cytokines involved in clonal expansion and differentiation of lymphocytes.
B cells specific for an antigen are activated by the antigen, which may be present in soluble form or presented by APCs. B cells internalize, process, and present the antigen, and migrate toward the T-cell zone in the lymph node. After interacting with activated
TH lymphocytes, the B cells are activated upon interaction of

## Bone resorption and the immune response

Bone is resorbed by osteoclasts, which are giant multinucleated cells formed by the fusion of precursors of the monocyte/macrophage lineage (Fig 3-16). Chemical mediators primarily involved in regulation of osteoclastogenesis include receptor activator of NF-κB ligand (RANKL), macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), and osteoprotegerin (OPG).26 Differentiation of the precursors into osteoclasts requires the concomitant presence of osteoblasts and marrow stromal cells, which produce both RANKL and M-CSF (Fig 317).11,25 M-CSF is a secreted molecule that binds to its receptor (cFms) on osteoclast precursors and then provides essential signals for survival and proliferation of these cells.24 Contact between osteoclast precursors and osteoblasts/ stromal cells is required for osteoclastogenesis to occur because RANKL is a surface molecule essential for differentiation of precursors into osteoclasts through interaction with RANK expressed on the osteoclast precursor.24 In addition to the membrane-bound form, RANKL can also occur in a soluble form. Binding of RANKL to RANK activates a signal transduction cascade that leads to osteoclastogenesis in the presence of survival factor M-CSF.

Osteoprotegerin is also produced by osteoblasts and stromal cells. It serves as a soluble “decoy” receptor that competes with

Under normal physiologic conditions, there is a well-regulated balance between bone resorption and bone formation. In certain inflammatory bone conditions, however, the balance is altered so that excessive bone formation or resorption may occur. Excessive formation of bone in certain conditions such as condensing osteitis may be attributed to an abundance of OPG or to the reduced expression of RANKL, resulting in a decreased RANKL/OPG ratio.

### How osteoclasts resorb bone

Differentiated osteoclasts adhere to the bone surface via binding of the ανβ3 integrin in the clear zone of the osteoclast to the amino acid motif Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) present in proteins of the bone matrix such as osteopontin and bone sialoprotein (Fig 3-21).25 After osteoclast surface integrins bind to bone, an intracellular signal cascade is generated that promotes reorganization of the osteoclast

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 155 -->

cytoskeleton and induces acidic vesicles to migrate to the ruffled border region.

After adhesion, the bone surface beneath the osteoclasts becomes segregated from the environment, and acids and enzymes released by these cells concentrate in this region to resorb bone. The resorptive compartment formed is often referred to as Howship’s lacunae.

Under the action of the enzyme carbonic anhydrase II and in the presence of water, CO2 entering the osteoclasts can be converted into bicarbonate (HCO3) and H+. These protons are released in the isolated microenvironment between the osteoclasts and the bone surface by H+-ATPase proton pumps, which concentrate in the ruffled border region of the cell. The chloride/bicarbonate exchanger prevents the intracellular pH from increasing as a result of proton extracellular transport by H+-ATPase. Chloride ions entering the cells in exchange for bicarbonate are transported into the resorptive compartment via chloride channels coupled to the H+-ATPase. Thus, the combined action of the H+-ATPase proton pump and chloride channels in the ruffled borders acidifies the resorption compartment.13 HCl formed in the resorption compartment lowers the pH to about 4.5.24 At this pH magnitude, bone is demineralized and the exposed organic matrix is subsequently degraded by collagenolytic lysosomal proteases cathepsins (mainly cathepsin K) and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), which are secreted through the ruffled border.21,25 Both cathepsin K and MMP-9 degrade type I collagen in an acid environment. Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) is highly expressed on osteoclasts and is also secreted in the resorption lacunae, where it contributes to bone matrix degradation.21
