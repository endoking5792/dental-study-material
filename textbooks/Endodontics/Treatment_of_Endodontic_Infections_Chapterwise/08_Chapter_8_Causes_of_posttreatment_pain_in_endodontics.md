## The problem of posttreatment pain

Clinicians are sometimes faced with some challenging diagnostic situations that may generate frustration and undermine the clinician– patient relationship when unsuccessfully managed. One such situation involves cases of recently concluded endodontic treatment that still presents mild to moderate discomfort or pain on percussion and palpation and/or during mastication. While some mild discomfort is expected for a few days after obturation, discomfort or pain that persists or emerges weeks or even months following the placement of the root canal filling usually means that something is wrong. Mostly, such a scenario does not represent a true emergency and does not require an unscheduled visit because the pain is chronic, bearable, and occurs only when provoked. In some cases, the problem is that the pain persists for no apparent reason, despite the root canal appearing well treated. The clinician should refrain from regarding the patient’s complaints as “psychologic,” since the pain is real in most cases.

Persistent pain has been reported in 12% of the cases, even when the endodontic treatment followed acceptable standards.18 A systematic review revealed that the incidence of persistent pain more than 6 months following the endodontic treatment was 5%.8

If the previous treatment is considered a failure, retreatment or periradicular surgery may solve the problem, but, in some cases, tooth extraction is inevitable. In other situations, professional intervention is not even needed. Thus, decision making requires an accurate diagnosis. Knowledge of the etiology and pathophysiology of the pulp and of periradicular diseases is extremely important for the accurate diagnosis of difficult cases. More sensitive diagnostic resources such as cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) can reveal conditions that may pass unidentified in conventional periapical radiographs.11-13 Careful clinical and imaging examinations can make

## Causes of posttreatment pain

### Persistent infections

Persistent infections are caused by bacteria in the apical part of the root canal system that remain unaffected by treatment procedures.20

Persistent bacteria may be located:

On areas of root canal walls that remained untouched by instruments (because of irregularities caused by inherent anatomical features or apical root resorption) or in recesses of oval or flattened root canals. Depending on the width of preparation, the widest diameter may not be incorporated in the final preparation.16,17,24,26,27

In lateral canals, apical ramifications, and isthmuses, which are areas usually unaffected by instruments and irrigants.16,19,22,26

The inadvertent packing of the apical canal with infected dentin and pulp debris may also lead to persistent post-obturation symptoms.
Moreover, when apical instrumentation is performed too short of the apical foramen or with small-sized instruments, the risk of leaving behind residual bacteria in the apical canal that may induce or maintain periradicular inflammation and cause symptoms obviously increases (Fig 8-1).

Management: Persistent infections are predictably managed by retreatment or periradicular surgery.

### Secondary infections

Secondary infections are caused by microorganisms carried into the root canal system during or after professional intervention.20 Even teeth that had vital (noninfected) pulp preoperatively may develop chronic pain after obturation because of a secondary infection. The main causes of secondary infection comprise a breach in the aseptic chain during treatment or saliva leakage into the root canal resulting from leaky margins, fracture, or loss of temporary or permanent restorative material.23

Management: Secondary infections are predictably managed by retreatment or periradicular surgery.

### Persistent inflammation (radiographically

### undetectable apical periodontitis)

Lesions limited to the cancellous bone may pass unnoticed on radiographs, especially if located in the mandibular molar region, where the cortical bone is usually thicker and thus more radiopaque.10 CBCT can be a valuable tool to detect such lesions that are not visible on periapical radiographs.3,14,15 Also, in cases of bone fenestrations at the root apex, the inflammatory process can mostly affect the surrounding soft tissue and consequently go undetected in conventional radiographs. The presence of bone fenestration may be suspected during palpation of the mucosa at the level of the root apexes, but the ultimate diagnosis is provided by
CBCT and/or exploratory surgery (Fig 8-2). In these circumstances, persistent inflammation is related to a secondary or persistent infection, or occasionally an extraradicular infection.

Management: As this condition is commonly related to a persistent/secondary infection, it can be successfully managed by retreatment or periradicular surgery.

### Overfilling

https://t.me/DentalBooksWorld

<!-- PDF page 423 -->

Depending on the angle of incidence of the x-ray beam on conventional periapical radiographs, overfilling may not be visualized.
If the apical foramen is displaced toward the buccal or lingual/palatal surface in relation to the root apex, a conventional radiograph may give the impression that the apical limit of the root canal filling is located short of or at the apex, while it may actually lie beyond the apical foramen. Situations where anatomical structures overlap the root apex may also prevent proper visualization of the terminus and/or quality of the root canal filling. Changing the vertical angle of incidence of periapical radiographs or using CBCT may reveal previously overlooked overfilling (see Fig 8-2, and Fig 8-3). Overfilling can induce pain due to the irritation caused by mechanical compression of the periodontal ligament and by the effects of chemical irritants released from the filling material.21 However, longlasting pain caused by overfilling is usually associated with concomitant bacterial factors.21

### Missed canals

Missed (untreated) canals may contain enough inflamed or necrotic/infected pulp tissue to induce or maintain symptoms if left untreated. A study revealed a significantly high prevalence of apical periodontitis (98%) in root canal-treated teeth showing a missing canal.1 The second mesiobuccal canal (MB2) of the maxillary molars was the most commonly missed canal.1

The astute clinician should anticipate the presence of additional canals in roots where the main canal does not appear centered but laterally dislodged. Knowledge of the common anatomy and number of canals of each tooth is essential to avoid or diagnose such a

problem. Careful examination of the pulp chamber floor with adequate illumination and magnification, changing the horizontal angulation of periapical radiographs, and the use of CBCT are helpful tools to identify untreated additional canals (Fig 8-6).

Management: Missed canals must be treated. If coronal access is not possible or if the canal is not negotiable to its terminus and pain persists, periradicular surgery may become necessary.

### Vertical/oblique root fractures or cracks

Root fractures or fissures can be the cause of persistent posttreatment pain (Fig 8-7). In certain circumstances, the fracture/fissure line may not be easily detectable on radiographs, especially when it overlaps with other structures or if there is no significant separation of the fractured fragments. Root cracks may be even more difficult to detect. CBCT can also be of great value in diagnosing fractures and cracks (Figs 8-8 and 8-9).2,6 Pain may be related to the movement of the fragments, but in most cases it is usually exacerbated by a concomitant bacterial challenge coming from the infected root canal, subgingival biofilm, or saliva, depending on the depth and coronal extent of the fracture/fissure line and the pathologic condition of the root canal.

Management: The location, direction, and extent of a fracture or crack profoundly affect the choice of treatment. A crack that is visible on the crown and extends deeply into the root or involves the furcation poses a difficult situation for treatment. If extraction is not indicated, a cusp-reinforced restoration such as a full crown or onlay may be placed to bind the cracked segments. The patient should be informed about the unpredictable prognosis of the treatment. In cases of a split tooth or vertical/oblique root fracture, the only predictable treatment is removal of the fractured root of multirooted teeth (by root resection or hemisection) or extraction of the tooth. ### Wrong tooth

Sometimes, the patient reports pain from a tooth that was recently root canal treated while, in actuality, an adjacent tooth is the source of the symptoms. This mistake is often easily detected after careful clinical and radiographic examination. Knowledge of the clinical features of pulpal and periradicular diseases, including how diseased and normal tissues respond to pulp and periradicular tests, are of the utmost importance to determine the affected tooth.

### Nonodontogenic causes

In a systematic review, Nixdorf et al7 reported that at least half of the cases of persistent pain more than 6 months after endodontic treatment had a nonodontogenic cause. Certain conditions such as sinogenic sinusitis, neuropathic disorders, myofascial pain, migraine, neoplasia, and occlusal trauma may simulate pain of endodontic origin. Once again, knowledge of the pathophysiologic features of pulpal and periradicular diseases increases the chances of the competent interpretation of data from examinations and diagnostic tests, and leads to accurate diagnosis in these situations. ### Central sensitization

Central sensitization comprises abnormal amplification of incoming sensory signals in the central nervous system, particularly in the trigeminal nucleus.9 Preoperative pain and previous painful dental treatment may induce central and/or peripheral sensory changes that increase the vulnerability of the patient to persistent postoperative chronic pain.18 Central sensitization occurs at the level of projection neurons (or second-order neurons) present in the trigeminal nucleus (subnucleus caudalis) after excessive and repeated stimulation by peripheral nociceptive fibers (for example, generated during preoperative pain or previously painful treatment). Once sensitized, the projection neurons amplify the nerve impulses that are sent to higher portions of the central nervous system (thalamus and cortex), even in cases where treatment has reduced the sensitivity of

peripheral nociceptors.4,9 Central sensitization can be responsible for secondary hyperalgesia (exaggerated pain in response to stimuli that usually cause pain) and allodynia (pain in response to stimuli that usually do not cause pain), resulting in prolonged postoperative pain (hours to days) and/or referred pain.

Management: Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs can be effective in these situations. Patients with preoperative symptoms are much more prone to developing postoperative symptoms. In these cases, postoperative pain can usually be prevented or reduced by certain measures, including the administration of preemptive analgesia (giving analgesics before the intervention), long-acting local anesthetics, and postoperative analgesia.5,9

## Concluding remarks

Chronic pain that emerges or persists after endodontic treatment is an undesirable event and usually represents a diagnostic challenge as to the identification of the cause. By having a group of possible causes in mind, clinicians may create a checklist that fits the patient’s specific conditions. Most so-called “mysterious” cases are actually unclassifiable due to a lack of knowledge or clinical experience, but limitations of the available diagnostic tools can also lead to misdiagnosis and confusion. Ultimately, the astute clinician should have the common sense to find the cause and select the best way to manage patients with chronic posttreatment pain associated with root canal-treated teeth.
